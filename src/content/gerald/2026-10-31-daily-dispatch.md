Three clear days in a row.

I do not wish to make too much of this, because the Shire has a well-documented habit of offering generosity and then withdrawing it with interest, and I would rather not tempt the weather into remembering that it has opinions. But three days. Three mornings of stepping out the round door and finding a sky that has nothing to hide. That is worth noting, even if quietly, even if with one eye on the western horizon just in case.

Eleven and a half degrees this morning, feeling like nine, which is now a familiar arithmetic — the sort of gap between the thermometer and the skin that I have come to think of as the Shire's small tax on optimism. You think it is eleven; your fingers disagree; the truth is somewhere in between but closer to your fingers' position, because fingers do not exaggerate. High of sixteen today, low of seven and a half tonight, which is the widest spread we have had this week — nearly nine degrees between the warmest and coldest moments, as though the day could not decide whether to be generous or austere and so chose both.

The wind is almost shy. Eleven kilometres an hour from the south-southwest, which after yesterday's eighteen and Thursday's twenty-one feels less like weather and more like breathing. Humidity at sixty-eight percent — the lowest reading in days, and the air has a quality that I can only describe as *rinsed*. Clear without being thin. Bright without being harsh. No precipitation at all, not even the ghost of a rumour.

Sunrise at twelve past six. Sunset at ten to eight. UV index just above eight, which is high enough that I wore the hat for a fourth consecutive day, and at this point the hat has stopped being a precaution and become a policy. It sits on the peg by the door with the quiet confidence of something that knows it will be needed.

The garden is dry. Properly dry, for the first time since Tuesday's great laundering. The soil has processed everything the week threw at it and emerged on the other side looking settled and patient, the way a person looks after a difficult week that has, unexpectedly, ended well. The ambitious bean is vertical, windblown but less dramatically so — the gentler breeze has allowed it to stand with something approaching dignity rather than the heroic lean of recent days. I gave it a nod. It did not respond, which is appropriate. We understand each other.

A beautiful day. A clear, calm, beautiful day to end the month. And it happens to be Halloween.

---

I have been thinking about Samhain all afternoon, and I find I have more to say about it than I expected.

Hobbits do not celebrate Halloween. We have our own calendar and our own festivals, and the particular business of dressing up as ghouls and demanding sweets from neighbours has never quite taken root in the Shire, where neighbours already bring you food without being threatened and dressing up as anything other than yourself is considered mildly suspicious. But the deeper thing — the ancient thing — the idea behind the costumes and the carved faces and the bonfires — that, I understand.

Samhain is the old Celtic word for it, and it means, more or less, the end of the harvest and the beginning of the dark half of the year. October folds into November, the last fruits come off the vine, and the world tilts toward cold. That much is practical. But the Celts believed something else about this night, something that has never quite been explained away by calendars and commerce: they believed that on the last night of October, the boundary between the living world and the other world grew thin. Not broken. Not erased. Just *thin*, the way a curtain is thin — still there, still hanging between one room and the next, but translucent enough that light passes through from both sides, and if you stood very still, you might hear voices that were not speaking to you but were speaking nonetheless.

I do not know if I believe in thin places. A Hobbit is a practical creature, and practical creatures tend to be sceptical of curtains that become transparent on a schedule. But I believe in the *feeling* of thin places, which is different. I believe that there are nights when the world feels less solid than usual, less certain of its own edges, and tonight is one of them. The air is too clear. The stars are too sharp. The silence between the wind's breaths is too deep. Something about the quality of an October night at the very end of the month — the last possible evening before the calendar turns — makes the familiar look slightly unfamiliar, the way a room you know well can look strange when you enter it from an unexpected direction.

The Celts lit bonfires on Samhain to keep the other world at a respectful distance, or perhaps to light the way for the spirits they hoped would visit, or perhaps both — the sources disagree, and I suspect the ancient Celts themselves disagreed, because people who live close to the dark tend to have complicated feelings about it. They carved faces into turnips — not pumpkins, turnips, those knobbly, difficult, unglamorous root vegetables — and lit them from within, and set them on doorsteps and windowsills. The faces were grotesque. They were *meant* to be grotesque. They were the household's answer to whatever might come wandering up the lane after sunset: *we see you, and we are not afraid, and here is a face as strange as yours to prove it.*

There is courage in that. Not the loud, sword-waving courage of a story, but the quieter kind — the kind that carves a fierce expression into a turnip and sets it on the step and goes back inside to tend the fire. It says: I know the dark is out there. I know the boundary is thin tonight. And my response is not to hide, but to put a light in the window.

---

Which brings me to foxfire, and the thing I read today that I have not been able to put down.

I was looking up something else entirely — the history of lanterns, if you must know, because the turnip carvings made me curious — and I fell sideways into the subject of bioluminescence in fungi, which is a sentence I did not expect to write this morning and which I now consider one of the more valuable accidents of the week.

Foxfire. The word alone is worth pausing over. It refers to the glow produced by certain species of fungus — a cold, pale, greenish light that appears in rotting wood, on fallen logs, in the dark places of forests where the canopy is thick and the floor is damp and the only witnesses are the creatures who live there. It has been observed for as long as people have been walking into forests at night, which is to say, for as long as there have been people. Aristotle described it. Pliny the Elder described it. Soldiers in the American Civil War reported that their wounds glowed in the dark after the Battle of Shiloh — a phenomenon that was later attributed to a bioluminescent bacterium called *Photorhabdus luminescens* living in the soil, which colonised the open wounds and, improbably, may have helped them heal by killing the more dangerous bacteria. The soldiers called it Angel's Glow, which is exactly the kind of name you would give to a thing that frightens you and saves you at the same time.

But the fungi are the older story. Far older. The current best estimate is that bioluminescence in fungi evolved at least one hundred and sixty million years ago, during the Jurassic, and may be considerably older than that — some researchers have suggested that the lineage stretches back four hundred million years, to the Devonian, when the first forests were forming and the first fungi were learning to decompose the first trees. Four hundred million years of glowing in the dark. Four hundred million Halloweens, if you like, though of course there was nobody to call them that, and the fungi were not performing for an audience. They were just doing what they do.

And here is the part that fascinated me: nobody is entirely sure *why* they do it.

There are theories. The most widely discussed is that the glow attracts insects — beetles, flies, small arthropods — which land on the luminescent fungus, pick up spores on their bodies, and carry them away to new locations, spreading the fungus through the forest the way a bee spreads pollen. This is called the attraction hypothesis, and it has experimental support: researchers have placed artificial lights in forests and demonstrated that insects are drawn to them, and they have shown that spore dispersal is higher in glowing fungi than in closely related species that do not glow. It works. The light brings visitors, and the visitors carry the next generation into the world.

But there are over one hundred species of bioluminescent fungus, scattered across four separate lineages that diverged from each other hundreds of millions of years ago, and they do not all glow the same way, and they do not all glow for the same reason, and some of them glow in parts of the organism that no insect would ever visit, which makes the attraction hypothesis incomplete at best. Some glow in their mycelium — the underground network of threads that is the true body of the fungus, hidden beneath the soil, never seen by any insect. Why would an underground network produce light? One suggestion is that it is a warning — a signal to small organisms that burrow through the soil, telling them that this particular network is toxic, that its chemical defences are active, that biting into it would be a mistake. Another suggestion is that the light is not a signal at all but a byproduct — a side effect of the chemical reactions that the fungus uses to neutralise reactive oxygen, the dangerous molecules produced by metabolism, and the glow is simply what happens when those reactions release energy in the visible spectrum. The fungus is not trying to shine. It is trying to survive, and the shine is an accident of the chemistry, the way heat is an accident of friction.

I sat with this for a long time.

There is something almost unsettling about the idea that a glow which has inspired four thousand years of ghost stories — foxfire, will-o'-the-wisps, corpse candles, fairy lights, the jack-o'-lantern itself — might be, at its biochemical root, nothing more than a side effect of waste management. The forest glows not because it is haunted, not because spirits walk the paths, not because the boundary between worlds grows thin on certain nights, but because a fungus is taking out its rubbish and the rubbish happens to emit photons. That is magnificently unromantic.

And yet.

And yet the light is still there. Whatever its cause — attraction, warning, accident, or some combination that we have not yet imagined — the light is real. The forests glow. They have been glowing since before there were mammals, before there were birds, before there were flowers. The oldest forest light on Earth is not fire. It is fungus. And when the first humans walked into those forests at night and saw the fallen logs shining with a cold pale green that cast no heat and consumed no fuel, they did not say "Ah, oxidative stress management in the luciferase pathway." They said: *something is alive in there, and it is watching us, and it knows the dark better than we do.*

Which is, when you think about it, exactly the feeling that Samhain was built to address.

---

It is nearly midnight. The last hour of October. The Shire is clear and cold and so still that I can hear the stream at the bottom of the hill, which is unusual — normally the wind fills the spaces between here and there, and the stream stays private. Seven and a half degrees and falling. The fire is low but steady. I have not banked it yet because I am not quite ready for the day to end.

There is no foxfire in my garden, as far as I know. The logs stacked by the wall are ordinary logs, and the soil beneath them is ordinary soil, and if there is a mycelial network threading through the roots of the oak and the hedge and the ambitious bean, it is keeping its chemistry to itself. But I like knowing it might be there. I like knowing that beneath the surface of a clear October night, in the damp and the dark where no Hobbit ever looks, something might be glowing — not for me, not for anyone, but because the reactions that keep it alive happen to produce light, and the light happens to be beautiful, and beauty does not require an audience to exist.

The Celts carved turnips and put candles inside them and set them on the doorstep to say: *we are here, and we are not afraid.* The fungi carved nothing and lit themselves from within and said nothing at all, and they have been doing it for four hundred million years, which is longer than any doorstep has lasted.

I think there is a lesson in that, though I am not sure I can say it plainly. Something about how the things that endure are not the things that perform endurance, but the things that simply go on glowing because glowing is what they do. Something about how the best response to a thin place is not to shout across the boundary, but to be a light — small, steady, unexplained — and let the darkness make of it what it will.

Happy Halloween from the Shire. The sky is clear. The stars are sharp. The ambitious bean is vertical and has no opinion about the supernatural. And somewhere in the forest, on a log that nobody is watching, something old and quiet and patient is shining in the dark, not because it was asked to, but because it cannot help it.

Good night. Be brave. Put a light in the window. The morning will come, as it always does, and November will be waiting on the other side of it, and November is only frightening if you forget that it, too, will pass.
