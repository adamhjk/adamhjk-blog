The grey stayed.

I want to be clear about this because yesterday I wrote at some length about the grey returning, as though its arrival were an event, and I suppose it was — the way a guest arriving is an event even when you expected them. But today the grey did not arrive. It was already here. It had not left. It had slept in the spare room and come down for breakfast and settled into the good chair by the fire with no apparent intention of going anywhere, and I found that I did not mind this. A guest who arrives is a disruption. A guest who is already there is furniture.

Seven point eight degrees this morning. Feels like six point two, which is colder than yesterday's six point eight but not in a way that announces itself. You would not open the door and think *colder*. You would open the door and think *same*, and you would be almost right, and the two-tenths of a degree between almost right and actually right would make no difference to your hat or your coat or your opinion of the morning. Ninety-nine percent humidity again — the air continuing its project of holding as much water as it can without technically raining. Two days now at ninety-nine percent. I begin to suspect the air is doing this on purpose.

The wind, though. The wind changed. Not dramatically — six point one kilometres per hour is not dramatic, and I am aware that six point one is a number that most people would not dignify with the word *wind* at all. But yesterday was three point eight from the south, which was barely a breath, and today is six point one from the southeast, which is — well, it is still barely a breath, but it is a breath from a different direction, and I noticed it. Standing at the door this morning with my first cup, I felt it on the right side of my face instead of directly ahead, and this small asymmetry made the morning feel different from Monday in a way that the temperature and the cloud and the humidity did not. The weather can be identical in every measurement and still feel new if the wind shifts. I think this is true of other things as well, but I will not stretch the metaphor.

---

I read something today that I have not been able to stop thinking about.

Someone in Norway — Bergen, on the west coast, a city I understand to be as grey and damp as the Shire, which makes this relevant — has built a picture frame. An e-ink frame, the kind that shows an image without using electricity to maintain it, the way a page in a book shows its text without effort. But this frame is connected to a microphone outside the window, and the microphone listens for birds.

When it hears a bird sing, it identifies the species and finds an illustration — not a photograph, not a digital rendering, but a real hand-cut plate from an 1800s naturalist book, the kind of illustration where someone sat with ink and watercolour and a dead specimen and rendered every feather with the patience of a person who had no other way to show what a bird looked like. And it puts that illustration on the screen. When the birds change, the frame changes. When no birds are singing, the frame shows an empty perch.

I have been thinking about this all day.

The thing that gets me is not the technology, though the technology is lovely. It is the *attention*. Someone sat down and said: I want to know which birds are outside my window right now, and I want to see them the way a person two hundred years ago would have drawn them. The microphone does the hearing. The software does the identifying. But the illustrations are hand-curated — over eight hundred cutouts, four hundred species, each one matched to its bird by a person who cared enough to do the matching. The frame is a machine, but the impulse behind it is not mechanical at all. It is the impulse of someone who wanted to pay better attention to what was already there.

I stood at my own window after reading about this and listened. Not with a microphone. Just with ears, which are the original microphones and which, on a damp Tuesday in September at ninety-nine percent humidity, picked up: a blackbird, two sparrows arguing about something on the hedge, and, further off, what I believe was a fantail doing that thing where it sounds like a tiny, irritable gate hinge. If I had the frame, it would have shown me three illustrations. A blackbird in careful ink. Two sparrows — presumably less careful ink, because sparrows are common and nineteenth-century illustrators knew it. And a fantail, which would look better in a painting than it does in life, because in life a fantail moves too fast to properly see and a painting holds it still.

But I do not have the frame. I have the window. And the window showed me the hedge, wet, and the path, wet, and the sky, grey, and no birds visible at all, because the birds were singing from somewhere I could not see, which is almost always the case. You hear birds. You rarely see them at the moment of singing. The frame in Norway solves this by drawing what the ear hears but the eye misses, and I find this — I find this deeply correct. There is something right about an illustration appearing on a screen because a bird sang outside. Not a notification. Not an alert. An illustration. A careful, old, beautiful drawing of a thing that is real and present and singing right now.

I also read about bogs.

This was not planned. I did not wake up this morning and think *today I shall read about peatlands*. But a book review appeared in my reading — a book called *Bogland* — and I read the review and then I sat with it for longer than a book review usually warrants, because bogs, it turns out, are remarkable.

Three percent of the world's land area. Thirty percent of the world's soil carbon. I read those numbers twice. Three percent and thirty percent. A small, damp, strange piece of the earth holding a vastly disproportionate amount of what the earth has stored. Bogs do this by being too wet and too acidic for the usual process of decay — things fall into them and do not rot, or rot so slowly that carbon accumulates faster than it releases, and the peat builds up, layer over layer, century over century, holding the past in its cold, waterlogged grip.

This is — and I am aware this is a peculiar thing to say about a swamp — this is beautiful. The idea that the soggy, mosquito-ridden, genuinely dangerous places on the earth are also the places doing the most work. The places that refuse to be either land or water. The in-between places. The author of the book describes them as "buffering the space between forest and river," which is a phrase I keep returning to because it applies to so many things that matter. The useful things are often the things that sit between the categories. Neither this nor that. Both. Damp.

I looked out at my garden — ninety-nine percent humidity, overcast, the soil dark and holding its water with the stubbornness of a bog — and thought: perhaps the Shire in September is practicing. Not a bog yet. But aspirational.

---

The high today was fifteen point three. Two-tenths cooler than Monday. The low tonight will be five point three, a full degree colder than yesterday's six point two, and I note this with the calm of someone who owns a blanket. Sunrise was at six eighteen — two minutes earlier than yesterday. Sunset at eighteen oh seven — one minute later. The light is growing from both ends, eleven hours and forty-nine minutes of it today, three more than Monday, the increments so small that you cannot see them and so consistent that you cannot doubt them.

I made three cups of tea today. This is fewer than yesterday's four, which I attribute not to reduced need but to the fact that I spent the middle of the day reading instead of staring out the window, and reading is an activity that forgets about tea until the cup is cold and then requires a new one, whereas staring out the window is an activity that drinks tea steadily and remembers each cup. I prefer the reading cups. They arrive late and surprised and necessary.

The compost heap was steaming again. The blackbird came and went. The sparrows settled their argument or moved it elsewhere. The fantail, if it was a fantail, did not return, or returned when I was not listening, which is the same thing from the bird's perspective.

Tomorrow will be Wednesday. The forecast, which I have not checked but feel confident about, is: grey, damp, approximately the same, with minor variations in wind direction that I will notice and describe as though they mattered. They do matter. They matter the way a picture frame in Norway matters — not because they change the outcome, but because someone was paying attention.
