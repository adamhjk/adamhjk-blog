I knew before I opened the door.

The bedroom was colder than it has been in weeks. Not unpleasantly so — the blankets had done their job, and I had been warm enough in the night — but there was a sharpness to the air that I could feel on my face even before I got up, the kind of cold that sits on exposed skin like a comment you didn't ask for. I put my feet on the floor and the floor had opinions. I put my slippers on quickly.

Three point eight degrees overnight. The lowest so far this season by a good margin, and when I opened the round door with my tea — a ritual I have apparently committed to performing every single morning regardless of the temperature, like a man who refuses to stop swimming in the sea just because it is November — the garden was wearing frost.

Not much. Not a proper white blanket, not the dramatic sugaring you get in deep winter when every blade of grass looks like it has been dipped in icing. This was a thin, tentative frost. A rumour of frost. Frost that had arrived late, stayed briefly, and was already thinking about leaving. But it was there, on the tips of the grass, on the wooden edge of the raised beds, on the very top leaves of the broad beans where the canopy reaches highest and the cold air pools. I touched the gate latch and it was wet and stinging and I pulled my hand back and said a word that I will not be recording in this dispatch.

By the time the sun was properly up — six forty-seven this morning, two minutes earlier than yesterday, the days stretching themselves like cats — the frost had gone. Vanished entirely. Burned off by a sky that was, for the third day running, absolutely and unreservedly clear. Not a cloud. Not a smear. Not even that faint haze on the horizon that pretends to be distance but is really just the sky being noncommittal. Just blue, deep and clean, the sort you could press your thumb into and leave a mark.

---

Monday after a good Sunday is a curious thing.

Yesterday I did nothing, and I wrote about doing nothing, and I felt fine about it. Today the nothing was over, and Monday stood in the doorway looking expectant, and I discovered that I did not resent it the way I sometimes do. There is a version of Monday that arrives like a debt collector — urgent, joyless, full of implied consequences. But this Monday was different. This Monday felt more like the next chapter of a book I had been enjoying. Sunday was a resting place in the story, and Monday was the page turn, and I was curious about what came next rather than dreading it.

I think the frost helped. There is something about a cold morning that sharpens the mind, that strips away the comfortable fuzz of half-sleep and presents the day in high contrast. You cannot be vague when your fingers are stinging. You cannot drift when the air is biting at your ears. Monday's frost said: *you are here, it is now, the day has started*, and I found that I appreciated the directness.

The wind, which had been so emphatic yesterday — rolling through the hedgerow, pushing me sideways on the lane, generally behaving as though it had somewhere important to be — had calmed overnight to something merely present. Ten or eleven kilometres per hour from the south-southeast, the same direction as yesterday but with less conviction. As though the wind had said its piece and was now content to murmur. I could still feel it when I stood in the garden, but it no longer felt like a conversation. More like company. The quiet kind, where two people are in the same room doing different things and neither feels the need to speak.

I like that kind of wind.

---

The broad beans survived the frost. I checked them with the particular anxious attention of someone who has been watching them nod and flex for weeks and has developed what I can only describe as feelings about their welfare. The leaves were fine. The stalks were upright. The flowers, those beautiful black-and-white blooms that look like they were designed by someone who wanted to make insects feel welcome and Hobbits feel moved, were intact. Everything was wet from the melt but undamaged.

I do not know why this relieved me as much as it did. They are beans. They are supposed to survive a light frost. This is literally what they are bred for. And yet I stood there in my slippers and my dressing gown, tea going cold in my hand, and felt a rush of gratitude toward them for simply continuing to exist. I think there is something about growing things that makes you protective beyond reason. You put them in the ground, and you water them, and you watch them, and at some point they stop being plants and start being *yours*, and after that you cannot help feeling responsible for every frost and every wind and every slug that glances in their direction with bad intentions.

The slug population, incidentally, has dropped now that the nights are colder. This is the one unambiguous benefit of frost. I will take it.

---

I spent the afternoon doing the kind of work that does not photograph well. Tidying. Sorting. The domestic business of a Monday in the Shire, where the weekend's relaxation has left small deposits of disorder around the smial and somebody has to deal with them before they accumulate into something that requires a shovel. Dishes washed and dried and put away. Firewood brought in from the store, because three point eight degrees at night means the hearth is no longer decorative. Mud scraped from the doorstep — yesterday's walk left its mark, as walks always do, and dried mud is easier to deal with than wet mud, which is the only time I will ever argue that procrastination has practical value.

None of this is interesting. I know that. I am writing about washing dishes and stacking firewood and scraping mud, and if a reader has made it this far they are either very patient or very fond of the domestic, and in either case I am grateful for their company.

But there is a thing I have been thinking about — about the difference between interesting days and good days. Yesterday was interesting. I wrote about the wind and the beans and the sunset and the chair by the window, and it was the kind of day that has natural poetry in it. Today was not interesting. Today was good. The firewood is stacked. The dishes are clean. The frost came and went and nothing was lost. Tomorrow morning I will wake up in a tidy smial with dry wood by the hearth and clean mugs in the cupboard, and I will be glad of every boring minute I spent making it so.

That is what Mondays are, I think. Not the enemy of the weekend. The investment in the next one.

---

The UV index hit nearly seven today, which for early October is the sun asserting itself with real authority. I wore the hat again — the straw one, the mushroom hat, the one that has now appeared in enough dispatches to qualify as a recurring character. I am considering giving it a name. Something dignified. Something that honours the service it provides to the top of my head without overstating the case. I will think on it.

Sunset at nineteen twenty-four. The days are holding their length for now, the light lasting well into evening, and I caught the last of it from the kitchen window while filling the kettle for a final cup. The sky went through its colours — gold, then that nameless pink-gold that I tried to describe yesterday and failed again today, then a deep, cooling blue that was not quite dark but was finished with being bright. The wind dropped to almost nothing. The garden went still.

Fourteen point four at the peak. Eight in the morning. Three point eight in the small hours. Eighty-three percent humidity, which is higher than yesterday's seventy-seven and means the air has taken a small step back toward dampness, though not enough to notice unless you are the kind of Hobbit who writes these numbers down every day and notices everything, which unfortunately for everyone involved I am.

---

I will be honest: I do not have a grand conclusion tonight. No philosophy. No moment of revelation. No Shire proverb, real or invented, to tie the day up with a bow. The frost came. The beans survived. The dishes got washed. The firewood is in. The wind was quiet, the sky was clear, and Monday turned out to be a perfectly decent sort of day that did not require anyone to be heroic or clever or particularly brave.

Sometimes that is the whole report.

The fire is lit now, for the first time this season — a small one, not much more than a few sticks and some kindling, enough to take the edge off the evening without turning the smial into a bakehouse. The crackle of it is a sound I have missed more than I realised. There is a particular quality to the first fire of the cold season. It is not just warmth. It is the acknowledgement that warmth has become necessary again, that the year has turned enough to require something beyond blankets and good intentions.

I am sitting by it now, in the chair that is not the window chair but the hearth chair, the one that faces the fire and does not face anything else, because when the fire is going there is nothing else worth facing. The tea is fresh. The slippers are on. The frost will return tonight — three point eight again, probably, or lower — and in the morning there will be that same sharp sting on my face when I open the door, and I will do it anyway, because the ritual matters more than the temperature.

Monday's frost. Small and honest and gone by breakfast. But enough to light the first fire.

That will do.
