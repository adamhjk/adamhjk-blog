The thing about four consecutive days of clear sky is that the garden starts to believe it.

One clear day is a gift. Two is a promise. Three, as I noted yesterday, is the point where you stop looking up. But four — four is when the tomatoes commit. Four is when the beans put out new runners overnight, when the basil stops hedging its bets and throws up leaves with actual conviction, when the soil dries to that perfect crumbly texture that says *I am ready for whatever you want to put in me*. The garden has decided this is real, and it is acting accordingly.

I found a strawberry this morning. Not a green one with aspirations, not a white one politely considering its options. A red one. Fully, unapologetically red, tucked under a leaf like it was hiding from me, which it was, because strawberries in November are still early enough to feel like a secret. I ate it standing in the garden at six fifteen in the morning with dirt on my knees and dew on my feet and it tasted exactly like a strawberry is supposed to taste when you have done nothing to earn it except show up.

The weather cooperated again today. Twenty point four degrees at the peak, which is properly warm for the Shire in late November — warmer than yesterday, actually, though not by enough to change the character of the day. The air was drier too, humidity down to seventy-one percent, and I could feel the difference in the way the breeze moved. Six kilometres per hour from the east-southeast, steady enough to notice, light enough to forget. The kind of wind that keeps the flies honest without troubling the washing line.

Clear sky all day. Not a wisp. Not a suggestion. Just blue, uninterrupted, from horizon to horizon, the kind of sky that makes you understand why painters give up and go home. You cannot improve on it. You can only stand underneath it and feel small in a way that is not unpleasant.

UV index: eight point six five. I am going to keep saying this every day until it stops being true or until I develop the leathery complexion of a Bree merchant who has spent too long on the road. Hat. Sunscreen on the ears. The ears are the part people forget, and they are the part that reminds you most painfully at bedtime.

Sunrise at five fifty-two. I was awake for it today. Not on purpose — I had left the curtain slightly open and the light found the gap and landed on my face with the precision of an arrow, and I was awake before I had decided to be. But I did not resent it. Lying in bed watching the light move across the ceiling of a Hobbit-hole at dawn is one of those experiences that sounds boring when described and feels sacred when lived. The wood of the ceiling catches the early light differently from the later light. Warmer. More amber. As if the sun itself is still waking up and has not yet decided how bright to be.

Sunset will not come until eight fourteen. Another fourteen hours of daylight, give or take. I used more of them today than yesterday. Sundays in the Shire have a different shape than Saturdays. Saturdays are for being idle without guilt. Sundays carry a faint, inherited sense that you ought to be doing something — not urgently, not with any real deadline, but *something* — and so I did something. I weeded the path along the east side of the hill, which had been developing a personality I did not care for. The dandelions had moved in with their usual cheerful aggression, and the clover had followed, and there was a thistle near the gate that I swear had not been there on Friday.

Three cups of tea today.

Three. The missing cup has returned. I do not know where it went. I do not know why it came back. I made the first at six thirty, the second after weeding, the third in the late afternoon with a biscuit that I had been saving since Thursday and which had gone slightly soft in the tin, which is how biscuits are supposed to be when you are eating them on a Sunday with your third cup of tea looking out at a garden that has finally decided to trust the weather.

I made a pie for dinner. Chicken and leek, with pastry that actually behaved itself for once — golden on top, no soggy bottom, the filling thick enough to hold its shape when cut but loose enough to need a spoon for the last bits. Cooking on a Sunday evening when the light is still pouring in through the kitchen window is one of the great unsung pleasures of late spring. You are not cooking against the dark. You are cooking in company with the last of the day, and the day is in no hurry to leave.

Four days of this. Four days of clear sky and warm soil and long light and food that tastes better because you ate it slowly. I know it will not last. The weather never does. Somewhere over the Misty Mountains a cloud is forming that has my name on it, and when it arrives I will put the kettle on and close the curtains and find something to read and that will be fine too.

But tonight, the sky is still clear. The stars are coming out — late, because the light holds so long — and the air is warm enough to stand outside in shirtsleeves and listen to the nothing that happens in the Shire after dark. No wind to speak of. No rain coming. Just the occasional rustle of something small in the hedge and the distant sound of the Water moving over stones and the profound, unshakeable quiet of a place that has been peaceful for a very long time and intends to stay that way.

Four days clear. The garden knows it. The strawberries know it. Even the thistle knew it, though the thistle and I have parted ways.

Sunday in the Shire. Three cups of tea and a good pie and nothing that needed doing that did not also want doing.

That is enough. That is more than enough. That is exactly right.
