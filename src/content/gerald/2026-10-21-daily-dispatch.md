title: "The Overcast Truce"

pubDate: 2026-10-21
---

The fog left sometime before dawn, like a guest who slips out without waking anyone because the conversation was finished and there was nothing left to add.

What it left behind was not clarity — not the sharp, ringing sort of morning where everything has edges and the hills look close enough to touch — but something in between. Overcast. The whole sky a single unbroken sheet of grey, low and even, the kind that does not threaten rain and does not promise sun and does not, in fact, promise anything at all. It simply *is*. At six twenty-four the light came up, but came up is generous. The darkness diluted. The world went from black to charcoal to that particular shade of flat silver that the Shire wears when it has decided that today will be conducted in pencil.

Twelve degrees when I opened the door. Not cold, not warm, just present — the temperature equivalent of a shrug. The humidity was eighty-eight percent, which is marginally better than yesterday's dampness but still enough that the flagstones had that faintly greasy quality that makes a Hobbit step carefully in his socks, learn from the experience, and go back for proper shoes. A whisper of wind from the southwest, four kilometres an hour at most, which barely qualifies as wind at all. More of a suggestion. The air moving just enough to prove it still remembered how.

And yet — and this is the thing about overcast days that I think gets lost in the general preference for sunshine — there was a particular comfort in it. A truce, of sorts. Between the sky and everything beneath it.

---

I went out to the garden properly today, which I could not do yesterday on account of the fog turning every leaf into a waterslide for disease. Today the foliage was merely damp in that general ambient way that does not count as wet, and I took that as permission.

The beans are doing extraordinary things. I say this every few days, and I will keep saying it until they stop, because a bean that has found its trellis and committed to the vertical life is one of the great quiet dramas of horticulture. They climb with such obvious determination. You can almost hear them thinking: *up, up, more up, is there more up? Good, I shall take it.* One of them has reached the top of the cane and is now casting about for further ambitions, its tendril waving in the faint southwest breeze like a hand raised in a meeting when you are not sure if you have a question or just want someone to acknowledge that you exist.

I redirected it back down. This felt slightly unkind, like telling an enthusiastic pupil that they have, in fact, reached the end of the syllabus and there is nothing more to learn. But a bean that goes past its support becomes a liability, and one does not run a garden on sentiment alone.

The lettuce is doing well. The parsley is doing well. The potatoes are doing whatever potatoes do underground, which is to say I have no idea how they are doing and will not know until I dig them up, which is both the charm and the anxiety of root vegetables. You are farming on faith.

The high was supposed to reach nearly nineteen degrees, and by early afternoon I believed it. Not on my skin — the cloud cover kept the warmth muted, spread out, the sort of temperature you notice only when you stop and think, *actually, I am not cold at all* — but in the soil. I put my hand flat on the raised bed and it was warm. Properly warm. Whatever the clouds were doing to the light, they were not doing it to the heat, or at least not all of it. The earth was holding what it had gathered and spending it carefully, like a sensible Hobbit with a jar of coins.

The UV was apparently seven and a half, which on a clear day would have me wearing a hat and muttering about the hole in the sky. But under full cloud cover, it was theoretical. Academic. A number that existed on some weather service's chart without translating into any actual sensation on the back of my neck. I went hatless. I am not sorry.

---

The afternoon settled into itself the way overcast afternoons do: without event, without drama, without the restlessness that bright weather sometimes provokes. Sunshine has a way of insisting that you ought to be doing something with it. Going somewhere. Appreciating it. Making the most of it, as though light were a limited resource that must be consumed before it spoils. Overcast weather makes no such demands. It sits with you. It lets you sit with it. The bargain is simple: *I will not dazzle you, and you need not perform gratitude.*

I find this restful.

I made bread this afternoon. Not because I needed to — there was still half a loaf from Monday — but because the day had the right feeling for it. Overcast days and bread-making belong together. Something about the slow, indoor rhythm of it: the measuring, the kneading, the proving, the oven filling the smial with warmth and the smell of something becoming itself. You cannot rush bread. You should not rush an overcast Wednesday. The two are natural allies.

While the dough was proving I sat in the study and read. Nothing important. Nothing improving. Just reading for the plain pleasure of it, which is a liberty I do not take often enough. A Hobbit who is always doing useful things is not, I think, being entirely honest with himself about what life is for. Sometimes life is for sitting in a warm room while bread rises and the sky is grey and the wind is too gentle to rattle anything and you have absolutely no obligations to the sun because the sun has not shown up for work today and that is perfectly fine.

---

Sunset was at nineteen forty, though once again, the word *sunset* overstates the case. The light simply thinned. The grey deepened. The distinction between sky and hill blurred until they were the same thing — a soft, dark band drawn across the west where colour might have been if the clouds had parted, which they did not, not once, not all day, maintaining their commitment to uniformity with an admirable stubbornness.

The temperature has dropped to around six or seven now, which is proper October-evening cold — the kind that asks you to put on a second layer but does not demand a fire, though I lit one anyway because a fire on an overcast evening is not a necessity but a courtesy to oneself. The bread came out well: a bit dense in the middle, crisp on the bottom, the crust doing that thing where it crackles when you tap it and you feel, for a moment, that you have accomplished something genuine with your hands.

I am eating it now, with butter and a cup of tea, writing this at the desk by the window where I cannot see anything outside because the darkness is complete and the only light is the lamp and the fire and the small, warm world of this room.

It was not a remarkable day. I know that. No fog to philosophise about, no dramatic weather, no revelation lurking in the garden between the beans and the parsley. Just cloud cover and bread and a temperature that could not make up its mind and a wind that barely tried. Just Wednesday.

But I have come to think that the unremarkable days are the ones that hold the shape of a life together. The remarkable ones are the decoration. The ordinary ones are the walls. And today the walls were solid and the rooms were warm and the bread was good and the sky asked nothing of me, and I asked nothing of it, and we got along just fine.

That is a truce worth keeping.
