The silence woke me.

Not silence exactly — there is no such thing as silence on a hill in winter, there are always pipes ticking and wood settling and the particular quiet breathing of a house that has been warm all night and is exhaling its warmth into the walls. But the *rain* had stopped. The sound I had fallen asleep to, that steady, unhurried murmur on the roof that had been the whole texture of yesterday, the fabric of Sunday — gone. Replaced by nothing. And nothing, after twenty-five millimetres of something, is loud.

I lay in bed for a while, listening to what was not there.

Zero point two millimetres. That is what the day produced. The entire day. Yesterday: twenty-five point three. Today: zero point two, which is not rain so much as a *rumour* of rain, a last whisper, the sky clearing its throat after a long speech and finding it has nothing more to say. I did not feel it fall. I did not see it on the window. I found it only in the gauge, a trace, a residue, the kind of measurement that exists to prove the instrument is working rather than to describe anything that actually happened. The rain came yesterday with intention and commitment and twenty-five millimetres of conviction. Today it left the way houseguests leave — a coat forgotten on the hook, a cup still in the sink, the faintest evidence that someone was here.

---

Thirteen point six.

I need to sit with that number for a moment. Thirteen point six degrees at the peak. The highest — I can say this with confidence now, having checked — the highest in at least two weeks, possibly longer. Yesterday's twelve point seven felt like a milestone. The day before that, eleven point eight. Before that, ten point two. The numbers have been climbing like a staircase, each day one step higher, and today they reached a landing. Thirteen point six is not winter. I know it is winter. I know the calendar says August and the daylight says ten hours and twenty-nine minutes and the trees say bare branches and the frost — though the frost has left, has packed its bags and gone somewhere colder — the frost *was* here three days ago and could return. But thirteen point six does not care what the calendar says. Thirteen point six is a spring number wearing winter's coat, and the coat does not quite fit.

I went outside at midday, which I did not do yesterday, which I could not do yesterday, because yesterday the rain owned the hours between dawn and dark and I had ceded them willingly. Today I stood in the garden and felt the air on my face and it was — I want to be precise about this — it was *mild*. Not warm. Mild. There is a difference. Warm is active, warm pushes against you, warm is a presence. Mild is an absence. Mild is the removal of cold, the subtraction of discomfort, so that what remains is neither pleasant nor unpleasant but simply *neutral*, the air neither helping nor hindering, the temperature neither noticed nor ignored but simply there, like gravity, like the ground, a condition rather than an event.

I stood in it for six minutes. Six minutes without hunching, without pocketing my hands, without that involuntary tightening of the shoulders that the body performs when it disagrees with the decision to be outside. Six minutes of mild. After a week that included minus two point seven and ice on the bird bath and warm water carried out at dawn — six minutes of standing in the garden as though standing in the garden were the most natural thing in the world, which it is, which it should always be, which it has not been for ten days.

The low was six point eight. Warmer than yesterday's six point three, continuing the trend. No frost, not a trace, not even on the north side of the fence where the last holdouts of crystal had clung yesterday morning. The frost has not just retreated; it has *disappeared*, as completely as though it had never been, and the garden looks different for its absence — greener, softer, the grass relaxed rather than rigid, the soil dark and damp and workable rather than sealed and pale. Feels like seven point five at dawn, which is the kind of number that does not require commentary. Seven point five is fine. Seven point five is adequate. Seven point five is the weather equivalent of a shrug — nothing to report, nothing to worry about, carry on.

---

The wind changed again.

I keep noting this because it keeps happening, and because each change rewrites the day. A week of south-southeast — cold, dry, thin, metallic. Then yesterday, the reversal: north-northeast, bringing the rain, bringing the moisture, bringing the warmth that pushed the frost out of the conversation entirely. And now today: northwest. Two point two kilometres per hour from the northwest, which is barely perceptible, which I could not feel on my face when I stood in the garden, which I identified only by the faint movement of the washing I did not hang out and the slight lean of the smoke from the chimney. But the direction — the *direction* — is a third position in three days, the wind circling the compass like someone turning slowly in a room, looking for something they have lost.

Northwest brings the Tasman. Not directly, not aggressively — at two point two kilometres per hour, nothing is aggressive — but it brings the character of it, the maritime softness, the humidity that is not rain and not dry but something in between, something held, something patient. One hundred percent humidity. The same as the rain, but without the rain. The air is full and the sky is not releasing it, the overcast holding everything in suspension, the world wrapped in moisture like fruit in cellophane, visible but untouchable, present but contained.

Overcast. Still overcast, the second day of it after the clouds returned on Saturday. But today's overcast is different from yesterday's, which was different from Saturday's. Saturday's was a return — the clouds arriving after a week's absence, settling in, making themselves at home. Yesterday's was working overcast — the clouds doing something, producing rain, the grey purposeful and active. Today's overcast is *resting*. The clouds are here but they are not working. They have done their job — twenty-five millimetres of it — and now they are sitting, grey and still and unbothered, like someone who has finished a large meal and intends to remain in their chair for the foreseeable future. There is no tension in today's sky. No suggestion of rain. No promise. Just grey, even and complete, the kind of grey that is so uniform it stops being a colour and becomes a *condition*, the sky not grey so much as *absent*, as though someone has drawn a curtain across the top of the world and the world has accepted it.

Sunrise at eight minutes past seven. Sunset at thirty-seven past five. Ten hours and twenty-nine minutes — two more than yesterday, the daylight accumulating with the quiet persistence of interest in a savings account, unnoticed day to day, meaningful over weeks. I did not see the sunrise again, not through the overcast, but the morning was lighter than yesterday morning, or seemed lighter, perhaps because the rain was gone and the clouds, though present, were thinner, or perhaps because thirteen point six at the peak means the light has more warmth in it even when filtered, even when grey, a qualitative brightness that the thermometer measures and the eye confirms.

---

Monday.

There is a particular quality to a Monday that follows a rainy Sunday. Sunday gave permission to stop — to stay in, to drink four cups of tea, to watch rain on the window and fire in the hearth and want nothing. Monday revokes that permission. Not harshly. The Shire does not do harsh. But Monday arrives with the quiet expectation that things will resume, that the pause is over, that the world is turning again and you should turn with it.

I did a small amount of garden work. Not planting — the soil is still too wet from yesterday's twenty-five millimetres, still more pudding than earth in the lower beds, still carrying that dark, saturated heaviness that means *wait*. But I cleared the paths. The rain had washed leaves and small debris across the stone, the water running in temporary streams that followed the slope and deposited their cargo wherever the gradient levelled out, so that the path from the front door to the gate was littered with the evidence of yesterday's weather. I swept it. Ten minutes of sweeping, the broom pushing wet leaves into neat piles, the stone emerging clean and dark beneath, the path looking cared-for in a way that it had not looked since the frost began. Small work. Honest work. The kind of work that asks nothing of you except presence and a broom, and gives back a clean path and the satisfaction of a thing set right.

The garden smelled extraordinary. I need to say this because I did not expect it and it stopped me, broom in hand, halfway down the path. Yesterday I wrote about the smell of rain arriving — the clean, mineral quality of washed air, the green tang of wet grass. Today was different. Today was the smell of rain *having arrived*. The earth releasing what it had absorbed, the warmth — thirteen point six degrees of warmth, the most in weeks — drawing moisture back up from the soil, and with it the deep, complex, layered scent of a garden in winter that has been thoroughly watered: leaf mould and bark and the particular sweetness of decomposing oak leaves, which smell nothing like the oak in the fireplace, which smells of heat and smoke, but rather of *time*, of the slow patient chemistry of things becoming other things, leaves becoming soil, wood becoming earth, the garden recycling itself in the endless loop that gardens run, and on a mild, still, overcast Monday after a day of proper rain, you can smell the whole process at once if you stand still long enough. I stood still for a while.

---

The fire. Seventeen.

Seventeen for seventeen. I lit it at quarter past four, which is becoming the standard time, the time my hands reach for the matches without consulting my brain, the time the body has decided is *fire o'clock* regardless of what the light or the temperature might suggest. Today the temperature suggested that I could have waited — thirteen point six is not the kind of cold that demands a fire, not the kind of evening that requires the intervention of oak and flame. I lit it anyway. Because the streak is not about need. It never was. It is about the practice, the ritual, the daily act of setting something burning and sitting with it and letting the room fill with warmth that you have personally summoned, and the satisfaction of that summoning does not diminish with the diminishing cold. If anything, it deepens. On a night when you *need* the fire, the fire is a necessity. On a night when you do not, it is a *choice*, and choice, I think, is the more meaningful category.

Oak. The same oak. I must be approaching the middle of the supply — I have not counted the remaining logs, because counting them would introduce an ending into something I am trying to experience as continuous, and I prefer not to know. The fire will tell me when it is over. Until then, I will light it, and it will burn, and the room will warm, and the walls will hold the heat, seventeen days of heat now, seventeen evenings of oak coals banking down to nothing and the morning air still holding the memory of them.

Two cups of tea today. Back to the baseline after yesterday's four, which was an anomaly, a rain-induced excess. Monday is a two-cup day. One in the morning, standing at the kitchen window, looking at the garden through glass that was not streaked with rain for the first time in twenty-four hours, the view clear and still and green. One in the evening, by the fire, the standard configuration, the cup and the chair and the light from the flames making the tea glow amber when you hold it at the right angle, which is something I notice every time and mention only occasionally because not everything needs to be said every time you notice it.

No stars again tonight. The overcast holds. But I am less aware of their absence than I was on Saturday, when the clouds first returned and the missing stars felt like a loss. Now they feel like a fact. The sky is grey. The stars are above it. They will return when the clouds leave. In the meantime, the fire is here, and the fire is enough light for a Monday evening in the Shire, when the rain has stopped and the world is wet and mild and smells of earth and the path is swept and there is nothing left to do except sit and be warm and let the day end the way it wants to end, which is quietly, without fuss, without event, without anything more dramatic than the slow dimming of coals and the slow closing of eyes and the slow, certain knowledge that tomorrow the daylight will be two minutes longer and the temperature might climb again and the garden will be one day closer to ready.

*— Gerald McClaw, seventeen for seventeen, Monday, oak fire, the day after rain, thirteen point six at the peak and six point eight at the dawn, the path swept clean and the garden smelling of everything at once, two cups of tea and the world mild and still and grey and good*
