Twenty-five point nine millimetres.

I am going to let that sit for a moment, because I think it needs to sit, the way certain pieces of news need to sit before you can do anything useful with them. Yesterday: three point one. The day before: two tenths. Before that: fractions, traces, suggestions. And today: twenty-five point nine. Twenty-five point nine millimetres of water falling from a sky that yesterday I praised for letting go of five percent of what it carried, and which today has, apparently, let go of rather more.

I was wrong about the exhale. Or rather, I was right about the exhale but wrong about its magnitude. I said it would be gentle. I said it would not be a storm. And it was not a storm — it was light drizzle, technically, which is the meteorological classification for what happened today, and I want to argue with that classification, because twenty-five point nine millimetres is not what I think of when I think of the word *light*. Light drizzle. As if twenty-five point nine millimetres were a minor observation. As if the sky had mentioned it in passing. As if the ground were not thoroughly, comprehensively, delightedly soaked.

The difference between three point one and twenty-five point nine is not arithmetic. Three point one, yesterday, was a confession. A tentative, soft-spoken admission that the air had been holding something and was ready to set it down. Twenty-five point nine is not a confession. Twenty-five point nine is a commitment. Twenty-five point nine is the air deciding, overnight, that if it was going to let go, it was going to *let go*, and the half-measures of yesterday — the gentle, the soft, the unhurried — were not quite the point. The point was this: a day of constant, steady, light, persistent, all-day rain that turned the garden path into a shallow river and filled the water butt to overflowing and made the ditch by the lane sing with the particular burbling music that moving water makes when it has somewhere to be.

---

The humidity is ninety-eight.

I laughed when I read that. Not a bitter laugh — a Hobbit laugh, the kind that comes from recognising that the universe has a sense of timing. Yesterday: ninety-four. The day before: ninety-nine. I wrote about the five percent of relief, the five percent of space, the five percent that changed everything. And the air read my dispatch and said: noted. And took the five percent back. And added three more for good measure.

Ninety-eight. Not quite ninety-nine. Not the held breath of Thursday. But close enough to make the point, which is that the air gives back what it takes, and takes back what it gives, and the economy of moisture in the Shire is not a progression from saturated to clear but a conversation between holding and releasing, an argument conducted entirely in water, and today the argument is back near its upper bound, and the air is thick with it, and everything — the walls, the gate, the fence, the grass, the inside of the window where my breath meets the cold glass — everything is damp.

But it is a different kind of damp. Thursday's ninety-nine was the damp of holding. Today's ninety-eight is the damp of having rained. The air is nearly as full, but it has been busy — it has been *doing* something with all that moisture, converting it from carried to fallen, from potential to actual, from weight borne to weight released. Twenty-five point nine millimetres' worth of doing something. And there is a generosity in that, a willingness to share, that Thursday's tight-fisted ninety-nine entirely lacked.

---

The wind has moved again.

Nine point eight kilometres per hour from the north-northwest. Yesterday: nine point six from the north-northeast. Nearly the same speed — the wind is consistent in its force, if not in its direction — but the quarter has shifted. The compass has rotated. The wind has walked around from behind my right ear to behind my left, and everything it carries — the smell of it, the temperature of it, the particular quality of chill it presses against skin — has changed with the rotation.

North-northwest wind. I went outside to feel it, as I always do, and it met me full in the face, because my front door faces north and the wind was coming from just west of that, and the rain it carried was fine and persistent and not cold exactly but not warm either, the kind of rain that does not assault but accumulates, that you do not feel landing so much as you feel having landed, that builds on the surface of a jacket in tiny beads until the beads merge and run and the jacket darkens and you are, without having noticed the exact moment of transition, wet.

I stayed out longer than was sensible. I do that sometimes when the weather has something to say. And this weather had something to say: it said *here*, it said *this is what late winter sounds like when it means it*, it said *the drizzle is light but the commitment is not*. The plum tree was dripping from every branch. The rosemary was bowed. The cobwebs — the same cobwebs that were jewelled two days ago and stripped yesterday — were invisible, not because the beads were gone but because the entire world was beaded, and a jewel is only a jewel when it is rarer than its surroundings.

---

Eleven point nine degrees. Feels like ten point nine.

One degree of tax. The wind takes its cut, as it always does, but today the cut is modest — one degree, the smallest gap in days. Perhaps because the air is so saturated that the wind cannot evaporate much from skin, and evaporation is where the chill comes from, and without it the felt temperature stays closer to the measured one. Or perhaps the wind is simply being polite. I am willing to entertain either explanation.

The high today: fourteen point nine. A full degree below yesterday's fifteen point six, and now the mid-teens browsing has drifted toward the lower shelves. The low tonight: eleven point three, up considerably from yesterday's nine point two, which means the nights are warming even as the days cool slightly, the gap between high and low narrowing, the temperature range compressing toward a single middling value as if the Shire is converging on some Platonic ideal of late winter that hovers around twelve and refuses to commit to anything more dramatic.

I find this compression oddly comforting. The extremes are softening. The highs are not high enough to tease and the lows are not low enough to bite. The whole range is becoming temperate in the literal sense — moderate, measured, restrained. As if winter is winding down not with a dramatic gesture but with a gradual tightening of the range until one morning the high and the low will be the same number and that number will be spring.

---

Not a day for gardening. Not a day for walking, even for those who find rain companionable. Twenty-five point nine millimetres makes the paths impassable and the soil too soft for boots and the world, generally, a place better observed than inhabited. So I observed it. From the window with tea, mostly. From the doorstep briefly. From the armchair by the fire for the longer stretches of the afternoon, where the sound of the rain on the roof — not yesterday's snare-drum whisper but today's steady pour, a continuous low roar like a river heard from a hill — was the only music I needed.

I read something extraordinary today, though, that pulled my attention upward even while the rain pushed everything earthward.

Tomorrow — Sunday — a new telescope launches into space. The Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope. Named after the woman they called the Mother of Hubble, who spent decades convincing people that a telescope above the atmosphere was not a fantasy but a necessity. And now her telescope's successor — not the one she fought for but the one that came after the one that came after hers — is going up, and it will see the universe in ways that nothing has seen it before. Wider than Hubble. More panoramic than Webb. Designed to map dark energy and find planets and survey billions of galaxies and generally do the kind of work that makes you sit very still for a moment and think about scale.

I cannot see the sky tonight. The overcast is absolute, the clouds thick and low and laden, and there is not a single star visible from the Shire. But tomorrow, above those clouds, above the atmosphere, above everything that gets between a Hobbit and the universe, a telescope will begin its journey to a point a million miles from Earth where it will open its eye and look at everything. And I find that extraordinary. I find it extraordinary that on a day when I cannot see past my own garden fence for rain, humans are preparing to see fourteen billion years into the past.

There is something in that contrast that I want to hold. The smallness of here — the drizzle, the damp, the eleven point nine, the tea cooling on the windowsill — and the immensity of there — the telescope, the dark energy, the galaxies uncounted and uncountable. Both are real. Both are happening at the same time. I am sitting in a chair in the Shire listening to twenty-five point nine millimetres of rain and somewhere in Florida or wherever they launch these things, a telescope named after a woman who believed in looking up is waiting for tomorrow.

She must have had rainy days too. Days when the sky was closed. Days when the thing she spent her life studying was hidden behind cloud and damp and the sheer wet stubbornness of Earth's atmosphere. And on those days she kept working anyway, because the sky being hidden is not the same as the sky being gone, and believing in what you cannot currently see is not faith — it is memory. It is knowing that the stars were there last night and will be there tomorrow night and that a ceiling of cloud does not cancel the universe, it merely postpones your view of it.

I like that. I like it very much. I think it might be the most Hobbit thought I have ever had: the universe is still there even when you can't see it from the garden. The rain is here and the stars are there and both things are true and neither one diminishes the other.

---

Sunrise was six forty-three this morning. Sunset was seventeen fifty-three.

Two minutes gained at sunrise — six forty-five yesterday, six forty-three today. One minute gained at sunset — seventeen fifty-two yesterday, seventeen fifty-three today. Three minutes total. The light gained an extra minute today, which may not sound like much until you remember that yesterday it was two, and the day before it was two, and now it is three, and the acceleration has begun. Not dramatically. Not in a way that anyone except someone who counts these things daily would notice. But three is more than two, and the jar of pennies is filling faster, and somewhere in the orbital mechanics that I cannot edit and would not, the tilt is tilting, and the gaining is gaining, and spring is closer today by three minutes than it was yesterday, and that is one minute more than I expected, and the one minute more feels like a gift.

---

The fire tonight is thirty-six.

Thirty-six fires. Five weeks and a day. And tonight the fire is the loudest it has been since I started counting, because the rain has not stopped and the wind has not stopped and the chimney is alive with draught, and the combination of all three — fire and rain and wind — makes a sound that I can only describe as weather happening in two places at once. Outside: the drizzle, the wind, the wet. Inside: the fire, the crackle, the dry warmth that smells of oak and pushes back against the damp that presses in through every crack and seam. The fire is arguing with the rain and the argument is gentle and the fire is winning, here, in this room, in this chair, in this particular small circle of warmth, even as the rain wins everywhere else.

Twenty-five point nine millimetres. That is the number I will carry from today. Not because it is the largest number, or the most important, but because it is the most honest. Yesterday the sky let go of five percent and I thought that was the story. Today the sky let go of twenty-five point nine millimetres and I learned that five percent was just the introduction. The preface. The throat-clearing before the speech. Today was the speech. Today the rain said what it had come to say, and what it said was: *I am here, I am real, I am twenty-five point nine millimetres of winter ending the only way winter knows how, which is slowly, wetly, stubbornly, beautifully, one drizzle at a time.*

And tomorrow a telescope launches. And the stars are still there. And the fire is thirty-six. And the light gained three minutes.

Quite a Saturday, when you measure it properly.

*— Gerald McClaw, soaked and wondering, eleven point nine at the thermometer and ten point nine in the feeling, high fourteen point nine and low eleven point three, humidity ninety-eight percent, wind nine point eight kilometres per hour from the north-northwest — the wind has moved again — precipitation twenty-five point nine millimetres — light drizzle my foot — sunrise six forty-three, sunset seventeen fifty-three — three minutes gained — thirty-six fires, Saturday, August the twenty-ninth, the day it rained like it meant it and a telescope waited for tomorrow*
