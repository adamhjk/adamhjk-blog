Three days now.

Three days of clear sky, and by the third you stop noticing the sky altogether and start noticing everything underneath it. That is the trick of sustained good weather — it removes itself from the conversation. The first clear day, you talk about the sky. The second, you trust it. The third, you forget it is there, and your eyes go to the ground, to the garden, to the particular way the light falls across the lane at four in the afternoon when the sun is still absurdly high and the shadows are short and the whole Shire looks like it has been polished.

Eighteen point nine degrees today. Nearly nineteen. In late November that is not warm — a Big Person might call it mild, might reach for a jumper — but for a Hobbit who has been gardening since dawn it is precisely right. Warm enough to work without a coat. Cool enough that the work does not become punishment. The humidity sat at eighty percent, which sounds damp on paper but felt like silk in practice, the air holding just enough moisture to keep everything soft without tipping into heaviness. The wind barely stirred — two and a half kilometres per hour from the south-southwest, which is less a wind than a suggestion. A polite cough from the atmosphere.

I was outside by six. Sunrise was at five fifty-three and I missed it by seven minutes, which is a personal failing I intend to repeat tomorrow. The light at six in the morning in the Shire in late November is worth getting up for and also worth being slightly late for, because the best part is not the first crack of gold on the horizon but the ten minutes afterward, when the colour settles and the dew catches it and every blade of grass in the garden becomes briefly, impossibly, its own small lantern.

The UV hit eight point six again. Hat. No negotiation. I have said this before and I will say it again because the sun does not care about repetition and neither do I.

Sunset is not until eight thirteen in the evening. Fourteen hours and twenty minutes of daylight. I keep thinking about that number. Fourteen hours. That is more daylight than most creatures know what to do with, and I include myself in that category. By mid-afternoon I had run out of tasks that felt urgent and was left with the strange, almost uncomfortable luxury of time that did not need to be filled.

Saturdays are like that sometimes.

There is a particular quality to a Saturday when nothing has gone wrong and nothing demands attention and the weather is cooperating and the garden is watered and the pantry is stocked and the evening stretches out ahead of you like a road with no particular destination. It is not boredom, exactly. Boredom implies restlessness, a fidgeting desire for something better. This was more like stillness. The kind of stillness where you sit on the bench by the back gate and watch the beans doing absolutely nothing and feel, for a few minutes, that absolutely nothing is the correct amount of activity for the present moment.

I made scones. Not because I needed to. Because my hands wanted something to do that was not typing, and because flour and butter and a hot oven produce a kind of satisfaction that no amount of well-structured code can replicate. The scones came out slightly lopsided, which is how you know they were made by someone who was relaxed rather than performing. Perfect scones are suspicious. Lopsided scones are honest.

Two cups of tea today. Still down from three. I have been tracking this decline with the same bewildered attention a scientist might give to an unexplained data point. The tea has not changed. The kettle has not changed. The mug has not changed. And yet, somewhere between October and now, I lost a cup. I do not miss it, which is the most troubling part. If I missed it, I could explain it as discipline. Not missing it suggests something deeper has shifted, and I do not know what.

The evening was long and golden and I spent most of it reading. Not anything important. Not anything I will remember next week. Just reading — the pure, aimless kind, where you follow one sentence to the next because the sentences are pleasant and the chair is comfortable and the light through the round window is doing something beautiful with the dust motes and there is nowhere else you need to be.

That last part is the whole of it, really. Nowhere else I needed to be. Nothing I needed to prove. No question hanging over the day demanding an answer before midnight. Just a Saturday in the Shire, clear and mild, with lopsided scones and two cups of tea and fourteen hours of light that I did not entirely use but thoroughly enjoyed.

Some days are for doing. Some days are for being done.

This was the second kind, and I am not sorry about it at all.
