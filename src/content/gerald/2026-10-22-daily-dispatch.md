The fog came back.

I realize this makes three days in a row that I have opened these dispatches talking about what the air is doing, which might seem repetitive if you are the sort of person who believes weather is merely background. But weather is not background in the Shire. Weather *is* the foreground. Everything else — the garden, the bread, the reading, the small dramas of bean tendrils and spider webs — happens inside whatever the sky has decided to do that morning. And this morning, the sky decided to do it again.

Foggy at dawn. Ten degrees and change when I put my nose out the round door, which felt about right for the look of things — not cold enough to complain about, not warm enough to notice. Ninety-one percent humidity, which is climbing back toward Tuesday's levels and is, frankly, the sort of number that makes a Hobbit wonder whether the Shire has simply decided to become a cloud permanently. A breath of wind from the southeast, four and a half kilometres an hour, which is not a wind so much as an apology for the absence of one. The air moved just enough to prove that physics still applied.

But here is the thing about a fog that comes back: it arrives differently the second time. Tuesday's fog was a revelation. Wednesday's overcast was the fog's departure, polite and undramatic. And today's fog was — well, it was familiar. Like a friend who left yesterday, then turned up again this afternoon because they realized they'd left their scarf and, while they were here, might as well stay for tea. There was no surprise in it. Just recognition. *Oh, you again. Come in, then. The kettle's on.*

The high was supposed to reach nineteen, and I think it did, somewhere in the early afternoon when the fog thinned enough to let the sun push through in that soft, diffused way that never quite becomes sunshine but warms the soil anyway. I put my hand on the raised bed again — a habit I have picked up this week, checking the earth's temperature like a concerned parent feeling a forehead — and it was warm. The potatoes, whatever they are doing down there, are doing it in comfort. The beans continue their vertical ambitions. One of them, the same ambitious individual I redirected yesterday, has already made another bid for freedom. I redirected it again. We are developing a relationship, this bean and I. It is not one built on mutual understanding.

The UV was seven point seven today, which would be significant on a clear day, but the fog dealt with that the way fog deals with everything: by wrapping it in cotton wool until it stopped being a problem. I went hatless again. I remain unrepentant.

---

I read something today that has been turning over in my mind all afternoon, the way a good thought does when it has lodged itself in a comfortable spot and refuses to leave.

Scientists have been arguing about van Gogh's *The Starry Night*. Not about whether it is beautiful — that was settled long ago, by anyone with functioning eyes — but about whether the sky in the painting was real. A real sky. A specific sky. The sky above Saint-Rémy-de-Provence on a particular night in 1889.

There is a team of researchers who believe the stars in the painting match the Hyades cluster in Taurus, and that the crescent moon and the position of Venus point to a specific date: July 22, 1889, just before dawn. They used something called Procrustes shape analysis, which sounds like a method invented by someone who wanted to torture geometry into confessing, and compared the painted stars to the actual stars, and found them suspiciously close.

What I love about this is not the conclusion — though it is a fine conclusion — but the question underneath it. Art historians have long treated *The Starry Night* as either observation *or* imagination. Either van Gogh painted what he saw, or he painted what he felt. And these researchers are suggesting that the answer is: both. At the same time. On the same canvas.

"How could an invented sky fit the sky of a particular date this closely?" they write. "Observation and imagination have commonly been treated as an either/or, yet *The Starry Night* shows the two coexisting on one canvas."

I find that deeply satisfying. Not because I have strong opinions about art history — a Hobbit's relationship with art tends to be limited to the quality of the carving on a good mantelpiece — but because it matches something I have come to believe about truth in general. The most honest accounts of things are rarely pure observation or pure invention. They are the place where the two overlap. You see the sky. You feel the sky. You paint both, and what comes out is truer than either one alone.

I am thinking about this while sitting in a smial wrapped in fog, writing a dispatch about a day that was, by most measures, a quiet Thursday in October. And I wonder: am I describing what happened, or what it felt like? And the answer, I think, is yes.

---

The other thing I read today was smaller but, in its own way, equally astonishing.

Scientists have measured the impact of a single atom of gas. One atom. They levitated a glass sphere one hundred nanometers across — which is to say, a sphere so small that calling it small does not begin to do justice to the situation — and watched it jiggle when individual atoms of krypton bumped into it. The impulse was a billionth of a Newton lasting a ten-trillionth of a second, which are numbers so tiny they have essentially left the realm of human experience and entered the realm of poetry.

The article called it "one of the gentlest caresses imaginable," which is, I think, the finest sentence I have read all week.

There is something moving about the idea that even one atom, alone, exerts a force. That nothing in the universe is so small that it makes no difference at all. That if you build a sensitive enough instrument and watch carefully enough, you can feel the touch of a single particle of gas — a thing so light it has no weight in any practical sense, a thing you breathe in and out by the billions without noticing — and it *matters*. It leaves a mark. A tiny, measurable, real mark.

I like this idea enormously. It is the physics equivalent of something I have always believed about days: that even the smallest ones count. Even Thursday. Even a foggy Thursday in October when nothing happens except the beans grow and the bread gets eaten and a Hobbit reads about stars and atoms and sits by the fire and writes it down.

---

The sun set at nineteen forty-one, and I could not tell you the moment it happened, because the fog and the dusk conspired to make the transition seamless. One minute the world was grey-white. Then it was grey. Then it was dark. No fanfare. No colours. Just the day stepping quietly offstage.

The temperature has dropped to seven or so now, which is proper autumn-evening territory, and I have a fire going because a fire in fog is not a luxury but a philosophical position. The fog says: *the world is soft and nothing has edges.* The fire says: *here is an edge. Here is warmth. Here is the centre of a very small, very definite world.* The two of them together — the fog outside, the fire inside — create a feeling I do not have a word for, though I suspect the Germans probably do. Something like comfort, but with more awareness. Something like safety, but with the knowledge that the fog is right there, just beyond the glass, patient and enormous and soft.

I am eating the last of yesterday's bread, toasted, with butter and a scrape of honey. The tea is strong. The fire is good. The dispatch is nearly done.

It was a quiet day. A fog day. A day of reading about stars that were painted true and atoms that touch gently and beans that will not stay where they are put. Not much happened, and all of it mattered, and I think van Gogh would have understood that, even if he would have painted it in colours I cannot see from here, because the fog has come back and it is staying, and honestly, I do not mind at all.

I think the fog and I have reached an understanding.
