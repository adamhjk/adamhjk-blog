title: "The Stillness After"

pubDate: 2026-09-21
---

The wind stopped.

I do not mean it slowed. I do not mean it softened or relented or eased back to the gentle shoulder-tap breeze of Saturday evening. I mean it *stopped*. Completely. Thoroughly. With the finality of someone who has been shouting all day and wakes up the next morning having lost their voice entirely.

One point eight kilometres per hour. From the north.

I stood in the doorway this morning — the same doorway, the same cup of tea, the same left sock now with slightly more respect for its elastic — and felt nothing. No push, no pull, no cheerful violence, no questions about my commitment to remaining upright. Just air. Still air. Air that had apparently decided that yesterday was enough, that thirty-four point six kilometres per hour had been its full contribution to the weekend, and that today it would sit quietly and think about what it had done.

The silence was extraordinary.

I had not realised how loud the wind had been until it stopped, in the same way that you do not notice the hum of a machine until someone switches it off and the room becomes suddenly, almost aggressively, quiet. Yesterday everything rattled and rippled and swayed. Today everything stood still. The young tree at the end of the garden, which spent all of Sunday bending with the reckless flexibility of youth, was upright and motionless, looking slightly embarrassed, the way someone does the morning after dancing on a table. The hedge was a hedge again — solid, defined, no longer pretending to be water. Even the plastic pot, which I retrieved from the fence this morning, seemed chastened. I put it back on the patio. It stayed.

---

Partly cloudy. This is new.

After days of that relentless clear sky — beautiful, yes, magnificent even, the kind of sky that makes you think profound thoughts about the nature of clarity and then immediately forget them — the clouds have crept back in. Not dramatically. Not the grey wall of Thursday or the rain-heavy blankets of Friday. These are *civilised* clouds. Polite clouds. The kind that drift across at a respectable altitude and partially obscure the sun for a few minutes and then move on, as if they are touring the sky and stopping only briefly at each attraction.

The effect on the light is remarkable. Yesterday the shadows were wild, wind-driven, scribbled across the lawn in a language I could not read. Today there are no shadows at all for minutes at a time, and then the cloud passes and the sun breaks through and everything has a shadow, sharp and still and perfectly behaved, and then another cloud arrives and the shadows vanish again, and the whole garden flickers gently between two versions of itself — the flat, even, cloud-lit version and the dramatic, contrasty, sun-lit version — like someone slowly turning a lamp on and off.

I could not decide which version I preferred. This bothered me more than it should have.

---

Eight degrees when I woke. Feels like six point seven.

That gap — one point three degrees between what the thermometer says and what your skin reports — is almost nothing compared to yesterday's chasm of five point six degrees between actual and felt temperature. Yesterday the wind stole warmth from you with the enthusiasm of a pickpocket in a crowded market. Today the air barely touches you. Today the cold is honest. It tells you exactly what it is and does not exaggerate.

I put the kettle on and stood watching the steam rise in a perfectly vertical line. *Perfectly vertical*. No drift, no lean, no suggestion of lateral movement. The steam went straight up, hit the cooler air near the ceiling, and vanished. Yesterday it would have been shredded the moment it left the spout. Today it rose like a prayer, if you will forgive the comparison, though I suspect prayers are less reliably vertical and considerably less useful for making a second cup of tea.

High of fourteen point one. Warmer than yesterday's thirteen point seven, which surprised me, and then made sense. The clouds are doing what the clear sky could not: holding warmth in. Not much warmth, not the thick insulating blanket of a fully overcast day, but enough. A partly cloudy sky is a compromise, and like most compromises, it makes nobody entirely happy but keeps things from getting worse. The sun provides the warmth. The clouds prevent all of it from escaping. The result is fourteen point one instead of thirteen point seven, which is not a revolution but is, I think, an improvement.

But the low tonight — the low is four point one.

I need to sit with that number the way I sat with seven point four last night, but with considerably more concern. Four point one degrees Celsius. That is not autumn. That is the sharp edge of winter reaching back through the calendar to tap you on the shoulder — not the gentle tap of a breeze, but the pointed tap of a bony finger that means business. The partly cloudy sky will not save me tonight. When darkness comes, whatever gaps exist between those polite touring clouds will become windows through which the heat pours out, and by three in the morning the Shire will be colder than it has been in weeks.

I have put the extra blanket on again. And a second extra blanket on top of it. And I have closed the small window in the study that I usually leave cracked for air, because four point one is the kind of temperature at which fresh air stops being refreshing and starts being a problem.

---

Sunrise at six oh nine. Sunset at eighteen twelve.

Twelve hours and three minutes.

Three more minutes than yesterday. Three more minutes than equal. The surplus is growing — slowly, invisibly, the way a savings account grows when you forget about it, adding pennies that individually mean nothing but collectively mean everything. Two minutes yesterday above twelve hours. Three today. The light is not just winning; it is *pulling ahead*.

And tomorrow is the equinox.

I have been counting down to this for days now — two days, one day, and now *tomorrow*. The astronomical equinox, the moment when the sun crosses the celestial equator and day and night are theoretically equal, though as I noted yesterday they are never truly equal because of atmospheric refraction and the way we measure sunrise from the upper edge of the sun rather than its centre. The experienced equinox — the one you live — happened days ago. But the formal one, the one the calendars acknowledge, is tomorrow.

I am not sure what I expect to feel. Relief, perhaps, that the crossing is official. Satisfaction that the long, slow climb from winter's shortest days has reached its milestone. Or maybe nothing at all — maybe the equinox is like a birthday, meaningful in concept but indistinguishable from the day before when you are actually living through it. You do not feel a year older at midnight. You do not feel the light shift at the moment the sun crosses the equator. You feel Tuesday.

But I will be paying attention anyway. A Hobbit who does not mark the turning of the year is a Hobbit who has stopped noticing, and a Hobbit who has stopped noticing has stopped being properly alive.

---

I gardened today. *Actually* gardened, for the first time since Thursday, because the wind was gone and the sun was intermittent and the soil — the soil was finally, blessedly workable.

Not dry. Still damp from Friday's rain, still carrying that dark, rich colour that means moisture is present and the worms are happy. But workable. The wind did its job yesterday — brisk, impatient, effective, like a cleaner who sweeps everything off the table. It dried the surface of the paths and the top centimetre of soil, and the sun between the clouds finished the work, and by early afternoon I was able to kneel on the path beside the herb bed without soaking through both knees of my trousers, which is a standard I have perhaps lowered over the years but which I maintain is entirely reasonable for September.

I weeded. I tidied the edges where the grass has been creeping in with the quiet determination of something that believes it owns the place. I cut back the dead stems from the last of the summer herbs and found, underneath, the small green shoots of things that are not dead at all but merely waiting, hiding their time under the cover of what appeared to be decay. The garden is not finished. It is resting. There is a difference, and it matters.

Humidity eighty-six percent. Still high, still the last breath of Friday's weather system slowly exhaling through the landscape, but the wind's absence means the dampness feels different today. Yesterday it was invisible, whipped away the instant it settled. Today it hangs. The air itself feels slightly thick, slightly soft, the way the inside of a greenhouse feels compared to the air outside — not unpleasant, not warm exactly, but *present*. You are aware of the air in a way you were not yesterday.

UV six point one five. The highest yet — higher than yesterday's five point nine, higher than any day this week. The clouds let enough sun through that the UV index climbed past six, which in September in the Shire is noteworthy. Not dangerous. Not the relentless pressing UV of midsummer. But enough that I felt the warmth on my face during the clear spells, and enough that the south-facing wall of the smial, which catches the afternoon sun when the clouds cooperate, was warm to the touch. I put my hand on it and left it there. Stone that has absorbed the sun is one of the finest feelings available to a person who has been kneeling in damp soil for an hour, and I am not above pressing my whole palm flat against a wall and standing there like an idiot, feeling the warmth travel up through my hand and into my arm, and thinking: *yes. This. This is what we were waiting for*.

---

It is late now. Nearly midnight. The temperature is already dropping — I can feel it in the way the walls of the smial have gone from cool to cold, in the way the blankets seem thinner than they did an hour ago, in the way the tea cools faster in the cup. Four point one is coming. By morning the grass will be stiff, the paths will be hard, and there will be that particular quality of early-morning cold that makes your first breath outside feel like drinking very cold water — sharp, clean, and slightly shocking.

Monday. The last Monday before the equinox, because the equinox is tomorrow. The last day of the old count, the last day when I can say *it has not happened yet*, the last day of anticipation.

The wind came yesterday and went today. The clouds came today and may go tomorrow. The light is twelve hours and three minutes and growing. The Shire is cold and damp and still and partly cloudy and the garden has been tended and the herbs are hiding green shoots under dead stems and the equinox is tomorrow and I am a Hobbit who has been counting and tomorrow I can stop.

A quiet Monday. A still Monday. A Monday that stood in the doorway and did not move and did not apologise for it.

The stillness after the storm is not silence. It is listening.
