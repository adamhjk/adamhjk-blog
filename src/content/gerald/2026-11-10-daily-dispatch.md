Two point one degrees.

Not the temperature. The *range*. The entire distance between the coldest this day would be and the warmest it would reach. High of seventeen point nine. Low of fifteen point eight. Two point one degrees separating the floor from the ceiling, the worst from the best, the as-cold-as-it-gets from the as-warm-as-it-manages. I have been writing these dispatches long enough to know that this is unusual. Most days stretch. Most days offer you a morning that feels different from the afternoon, an evening that has moved somewhere from where the dawn began. This day did not stretch. This day *held still*.

Sixteen and a half degrees at the thermometer, feeling like sixteen point six, which is so close to the actual reading that the concept of "feels like" has essentially given up and gone home. When the air temperature and the felt temperature are separated by a single tenth of a degree, the atmosphere is not playing tricks. It is not adjusting for wind chill or sun exposure or any of the invisible negotiations that usually make one number different from the other. It is simply *being*, straightforwardly and without commentary, the temperature that it is.

I find this oddly comforting.

The twenty-degree streak is over. I noted it building — six days, seven days, the afternoons climbing above twenty with the kind of regularity that made you start to expect it, the way you start to expect the kettle to whistle at a certain point and would be mildly alarmed if it did not. Seventeen point nine is not twenty. It is close enough that you might round up in casual conversation, but a Hobbit does not round up in dispatches, and the truth is that November has remembered what it is. Not cold, not yet, not properly — fifteen point eight at the low is still a long way from the blanket-and-regret territory of deep winter — but cooler. A notch turned. A dial adjusted. The season settling into a slightly lower register, like a singer who has been reaching for the high notes all week and has decided, wisely, to come back down to where the voice is most itself.

---

Twenty-eight point six millimetres.

The rain is back, and it has brought *conviction*. Not Saturday's theatrical forty, which was the rain making a statement, a manifesto delivered through gutters and running streams. But not Sunday's apologetic three point six, either, or Monday's neighbourly eight point two. Twenty-eight point six is rain with purpose. Rain that has read the brief, understood the assignment, and is executing it with professional competence. The kind of rain that a project manager would approve of: consistent, sustained, covering the ground without drama, meeting its targets without exceeding them, arriving on time and intending to stay until the job is done.

It has been falling since before I woke, which means I woke to it, which means the first sound of this Tuesday was water on the round window above my bed. There are worse ways to learn that the day has started. The rain on a Hobbit-hole window is not like rain on glass or slate or thatch — the round windows are set deep into the hillside, and the rain hits them at an angle that produces a sound more like tapping than drumming, more like fingers on a desk than fists on a door. Polite. Persistent. The rain knocking and then waiting, knocking and then waiting, with the infinite patience of something that knows it will be let in eventually, because it always is.

The wind has come around to the northeast at thirteen point four kilometres per hour, which is brisk enough to push the rain sideways but not forceful enough to make it unpleasant. A northeast wind in the Shire carries a different quality from the westerlies and northwesterlies of recent days. It comes over the hills from a direction that has fewer trees to slow it down and more open ground to pick up speed across, so it arrives with a slight edge, a coolness that the other winds do not carry. Not unfriendly. But not casual, either. The kind of wind that is going somewhere and would prefer not to stop for conversation.

Humidity: ninety-six percent. I read this number and looked outside and thought, *yes, that is correct*. Ninety-six percent is not humidity. Ninety-six percent is the air deciding that the difference between itself and water is largely philosophical. Everything is wet. Not dramatically wet, not puddles-and-flooding wet, but *pervasively* wet, in the way that a fog is wet — you cannot point to the water, but you can feel it on every surface, in every breath, in the way your shirt clings slightly differently and the pages of a book feel softer at the edges and the firewood, even stacked under cover, has a reluctance to catch that it did not have a fortnight ago.

UV index: nought point eight five. I laughed when I saw this. After days of seven and eight, after warnings about hats and sun exposure, nought point eight five is the UV equivalent of the sun calling in sick. The clouds are so complete, so thorough in their coverage, that the sun might as well be a rumour. Sunrise was at one minute past six. Sunset will be at one minute past eight. Fourteen hours of theoretical daylight, all of it grey, all of it filtered through a sky that has committed fully to its overcast and would like everyone to know that partial clearing is not on today's agenda.

---

I did not go to the garden today.

This is worth recording because I go to the garden most days, even in rain, even when there is nothing to do there except stand and look at things growing and feel the particular satisfaction of a Hobbit in the presence of soil. But twenty-eight point six millimetres and ninety-six percent humidity and a northeast wind with an edge to it — the garden will be there tomorrow. The bean will be there tomorrow. Whatever that potential flower bud was doing yesterday, it will continue doing it today without my supervision. Some of the most important growing happens when nobody is watching, and I think there is a lesson in that, though I am not going to press on it too hard because I promised myself, a few dispatches ago, that I would stop reaching for lessons when the day does not naturally offer them.

Instead I stayed in. Properly in. Not stood-in-the-doorway in. Not one-foot-outside in. *In*. The round door stayed shut. The fire stayed fed. The kettle went on at half seven and again at ten and again after lunch and once more at four, which is either excessive or exactly right depending on your view of tea, and my view of tea is that it is never excessive and always exactly right, and I will not be taking questions at this time.

There is a particular quality to a day spent entirely inside a Hobbit-hole while the rain falls. The hill absorbs sound. Not all of it — you can still hear the rain, still hear the wind when it gusts, still hear the occasional complaint of a gutter dealing with more water than it was strictly designed for — but the hill takes the sharp edges off. The world outside becomes muffled, approximate, a suggestion rather than a demand. And inside, everything becomes *more*. The crackle of the fire is louder because there is less to compete with it. The smell of bread — I baked, because what else does one do on a rain-locked Tuesday — fills every room because the doors are all shut and the warmth keeps the scent circulating. The light from the lamps is warmer because the windows are offering nothing but grey, and the contrast turns every candle into a small performance.

I baked a simple loaf. Nothing ambitious. Flour, water, salt, a bit of the sourdough starter that has been sitting on the counter for longer than I care to admit, doing its slow, bubbling, microbial work with the patient industriousness of something that does not know it is alive but behaves as though it does. The starter is a strange companion. It requires feeding. It has moods — some days it rises eagerly, some days it sulks. It responds to temperature and to time and to attention in ways that feel, if you are a Hobbit inclined to anthropomorphise kitchen cultures, almost personal. Today it was cooperative. The dough rose well. The crust cracked in the oven with that particular sound — a deep, ticking creak, like a wooden ship settling — that tells you the bread is doing what bread is supposed to do.

The loaf came out at half two. I let it cool until three, which required more self-discipline than any other act I performed today. Cutting into bread before it has cooled is a sin against texture, against crumb structure, against the entire contract between baker and loaf, but the smell of it — the deep, yeasty, almost caramel warmth of a fresh crust — makes the waiting feel like a moral test designed by someone who does not have your best interests at heart. I passed the test. Barely. The first slice, with butter that melted immediately into the warm crumb, was worth every minute of restraint.

---

I have been thinking about narrowness.

Not in a bad way. Not the narrowness of small-mindedness or limited options or the feeling of walls closing in. The narrowness of this day's temperature range. Two point one degrees. A life lived between fifteen point eight and seventeen point nine. A day that begins close to where it ends, that does not ask you to adjust, that does not require you to put on a jumper in the morning and take it off by noon and put it back on by evening. A day where the conditions at breakfast are the conditions at supper, and the in-between is simply more of the same.

Most of the time, we celebrate range. We want our days to have arcs, our stories to have high points and low points, our temperatures to sweep dramatically from dawn chill to afternoon warmth and back again. We want the gap to be wide because width feels like living, feels like something happened, feels like the day moved rather than stood still.

But there is something to be said for a day that holds still.

A narrow day is a day without surprises. And surprises, whatever their reputation, are exhausting. They require response. They demand that you recalibrate, reassess, react. A narrow day asks none of this. A narrow day says: *here is what today is. It will be this all the way through. You may plan accordingly.* And so you plan. You bake bread because you know the kitchen will stay at the same temperature for the rising. You keep the fire at the same level because the demand on it will not change. You wear the same layers from morning to night because the air will not betray you by warming up or cooling down unexpectedly. You can *settle*, in a way that a wider day does not permit, because settling requires trust, and trust requires consistency, and consistency is exactly what a two-point-one-degree range provides.

I think some of the best days are narrow days. Not the most exciting. Not the ones you tell stories about. But the ones where you bake bread and drink four cups of tea and listen to the rain and do not once have to wonder whether the day is going to change on you. The ones where the gap between the best and the worst is so small that you cannot actually locate the boundary, and so you stop looking for it, and in the stopping, you find something that wider days, for all their drama, cannot offer.

Stillness. Not silence — the rain saw to that. Not inactivity — the bread saw to that. But the deeper stillness of a day that knows what it is from the first minute to the last, and does not fidget.

---

Good night from the Shire. The fire is low and steady, banked for a night that will stay at fifteen point eight because that is what fifteen point eight does — it stays. The bread is on the counter, half-eaten, covered with a cloth, waiting for tomorrow's breakfast with the patience of a thing that knows it is wanted. The rain continues at its professional pace, covering the ground, meeting its targets, knocking on the window with its polite persistence. The bean is out there somewhere in the dark, unsupervised, probably growing.

Tuesday. November the tenth. Twenty-eight point six millimetres. Ninety-six percent humidity. Nought point eight five UV. Two point one degrees of range. The narrowest day I can remember, and one of the most content. The day held still, and I held still with it, and the bread rose, and the tea was good, and the hill kept the sharp edges off, and that was enough.

That was enough.
