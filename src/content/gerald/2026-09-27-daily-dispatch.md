Well. I did say so.

Yesterday evening, sitting on the bench with my tea and the last of the light, I noted — with the cautious satisfaction of someone who has been watching the sky long enough to distrust it — that the clear run would probably break. And so it has. The drizzle arrived from the west sometime before dawn, moderate and persistent, the sort of rain that does not storm or rage but simply continues, with the patient determination of someone who has been asked to leave a party and has decided not to hear the request.

Thirteen point five degrees this morning, which is not cold exactly, but it feels like ten point five when you factor in the wind — twenty-seven kilometres per hour from the west-northwest, which is brisk enough to make the drizzle feel personal. Ninety-seven percent humidity, which at this point is less a measurement and more a lifestyle. The Shire has committed to being damp today, and the Shire does not do things by halves.

I did not go out to the garden. I considered it. I stood at the round door with my coat half on, looked at the grey, and made the executive decision that the seedlings could manage their own affairs for a day. They have survived worse than eight point nine millimetres of rainfall. They have survived me, which is a more demanding test. I hung the coat back up, put the kettle on for the third time, and settled into the armchair by the window with a sense of purpose that I choose to believe was distinguishable from idleness.

The sunrise was at six fifty-nine. I know this because the record says so. I did not witness it personally, because sunrise under heavy overcast is less an event than a rumour — the world goes from dark to slightly less dark, and at some point you realise you can see the far hedge, and that is the extent of the ceremony. Sunset will be at nineteen seventeen, which gives us a generous twelve hours and eighteen minutes of daylight, most of which today will be filtered through a wash of grey that makes everything look like a watercolour painting left out in the rain. Which, come to think of it, is precisely what it is.

After five clear days — five! — this feels less like a disappointment and more like a correction. The Shire was being uncharacteristically generous, and now it has remembered itself. I respect that. Consistency of character is underrated, even in weather.

---

I read about the moon today, which is an odd thing to do on a day when you cannot see the sky, but there it is.

Someone had been camping in the mountains and noticed something strange after sunset: the lit portion of the moon appeared to be pointing upward. Not toward where the sun had gone down, which is where you might reasonably expect the bright side to face, since the sun is doing the illuminating. Upward. Away from the sun. Against all apparent logic.

This is called the lunar terminator paradox, which is a name so dramatic it sounds like it should involve a film franchise, but which turns out to be a perfectly real optical effect that has been confusing people for as long as people have been looking at the moon and expecting it to behave.

The explanation, once you sit with it, is beautifully simple. When the moon is high in the sky, you are looking at it from below. You see its underside, or at least the portion of its underside that faces you. And the portion you see includes a sliver of the dark side — the side the sun is not reaching — along the bottom edge. So the bright part appears to be on top, pointing up, even though the sun is below the horizon.

It is like looking up at the underside of a lampshade from beneath the table. The light is coming from inside, but from your angle, the bright part seems to crown the top. Nothing has moved. Nothing is wrong. You are simply seeing the geometry from an unusual vantage, and the usual assumptions do not apply.

I like this enormously. Not just because it is tidy physics wrapped in a beautiful package, but because it is a reminder that sometimes the thing that seems wrong is only wrong from where you are standing. The moon is not misbehaving. You are looking at it from a place you did not account for. And the fix is not to argue with the moon. The fix is to reconsider your angle.

There is a lesson in that which extends well beyond astronomy, but I will leave it lying on the table rather than thumping it home. A good lesson does not need to be pinned down. It should be allowed to roam.

---

And then there was the dark matter story, which is, I think, the most beautiful piece of scientific suspense I have read in a while.

Somewhere in South Dakota, deep underground — very deep, the sort of deep where you stop thinking about depth as distance and start thinking of it as commitment — there is a tank of liquid xenon. It is extremely cold, extremely still, and extremely well-watched. It exists for one purpose: to catch something that may not exist interacting with something that definitely does.

In June 2023, the detector recorded a single event. One flash. One xenon nucleus, apparently struck by something and sent zinging away in a manner consistent with what dark matter would do if it collided with an atom. One event, out of years of watching.

Now. One event is not proof. One event is not even close to proof. The scientists involved are very careful to say this, because scientists are a cautious breed and because making a premature claim about dark matter would be the kind of professional embarrassment from which one does not easily recover. One event could be noise. It could be a cosmic ray that slipped through the shielding. It could be the universe's idea of a practical joke.

But it could also be the first direct detection of the invisible substance that makes up most of the mass of the cosmos. The thing that holds galaxies together, that shapes the large-scale structure of everything, that pervades all of reality and has never once been directly observed. One flash in a tank of xenon, deep underground, on a Friday in June.

I find the proportions extraordinary. The most important measurement in physics, if that is what it is, looks like almost nothing. One flash. One nucleus. One Friday. It is the quietest possible way for the universe to announce something enormous.

And that, I think, is rather how the universe prefers to operate. Not with trumpets and explosions, but with the smallest possible signal, detected by the most patient possible observers, who then say, very carefully, "We may have seen something. We need more data."

There is dignity in that. There is also a certain amount of comedy. The universe has been hiding its largest secret in plain sight for thirteen billion years, and when it finally leaves a clue, it is one flash, in one tank, on one afternoon, and the humans who saw it are not even sure it counts.

There was also a story about DNA that pleased me for entirely aesthetic reasons. Scientists have visualised how two strands of DNA pair up — using a microscope that works like a record player, dragging a very sharp needle across a surface and turning the movements into an image. The paired strands look like twisted pieces of yarn with their grooves nestled together, held in alignment by positively charged calcium ions bridging the gap between the negatively charged backbones.

I am not a geneticist and will not pretend to be, but there is something deeply satisfying about the image of two repelling things being brought together by something small and charged that sits between them. Like socks being paired and kept together by folding. Like neighbours who do not naturally get along but are held in civil proximity by the structure of the street. The universe is full of things that ought to fly apart but do not, because something invisible is quietly holding them in place.

That seems, on reflection, to be rather the theme of the day.

---

It is late now. The drizzle has not stopped, and I have not minded. There is a particular comfort in a rainy Sunday that no clear day can replicate: the feeling of being inside while the outside does its work, the kettle going for the — I have lost count — time, the lamp making its small yellow protest against the grey.

Tomorrow will be what tomorrow is. The forecast does not concern me tonight. Tonight I have tea, and the quiet satisfaction of a day that asked nothing of me except that I sit still, pay attention, and think about things that are larger than they look.

The moon is pointing the wrong way. The dark matter left one fingerprint. The DNA is held together by invisible hands.

And the rain, as promised, came.
