pubDate: 2026-08-23
---

It rained today. Properly.

Not the traces I have been recording — the zero point two, the zero point one, the clouds leaking without conviction like a guest who says they might stay for dinner but has not taken off their coat. Today the sky committed. Two point four millimetres. That is rain. That is water arriving with intent, falling with enough weight to darken the path, to bead on the rosemary, to pool in the shallow dip by the garden gate where the clay does not drain well and never has and probably never will because clay is stubborn in the way that only very old, very patient things can be stubborn.

I stood at the window and watched it for longer than I expected to.

There is a difference between rain that is happening and rain that has been missing. Two point four millimetres would be unremarkable in the Shire on any ordinary week. The Shire runs on rain the way other places run on sunshine — it is the default, the engine, the thing that makes everything green and damp and alive and faintly inconvenient in exactly the way that home ought to be faintly inconvenient. But after the clear streak, after the four days of frost and zero cloud cover and air dry enough to crack your lips, and then after two days of overcast that promised moisture but delivered only traces — after all of that, two point four millimetres feels like an answer to a question I did not know I had been asking.

The garden needed it. I needed it. I did not realise how much until I heard it on the roof.

---

The wind was the other story, and in some ways the larger one.

Twenty point one kilometres per hour from the north-northwest. I need to sit with that number for a moment, because the scale of the change matters. Friday: one point nine. Barely breathing. Saturday: seven point seven. A proper exhale. And today: twenty point one. The wind did not build gradually. It did not ease its way into the conversation like a polite dinner guest finding a gap between topics. It arrived. It announced itself. It pushed against the door when I opened it and pressed against my face when I stepped outside and moved the trees in the way that only wind above fifteen or so can move them — not bending, not dramatic, but restless, the branches swaying in sustained conversation rather than occasional twitches, the leaves turning and showing their pale undersides, the whole canopy alive with lateral motion.

Twenty point one from the north-northwest. That is winter wind. That is wind from the direction where the cold lives, not the gentle westerly of yesterday or the barely-there southerly of the clear days but a real, directional, opinionated wind that has come from somewhere and is going somewhere and does not care particularly whether you approve. I respect that. A wind with purpose is more interesting than a wind with none, even when the purpose includes making your ears cold.

And it did make the morning colder than the numbers suggest. Nine point four degrees at the thermometer, which is the warmest reading I can remember in recent days — certainly warmer than the frost mornings, warmer than the two point fives and zero point fives that the clear sky permitted. But feels-like five point nine. The wind stealing the warmth from the surface of things, carrying it away, replacing it with the next gust before the skin can recover. Yesterday's seven point seven gave a feels-like of eight point four. Today's twenty point one gives five point nine. Warmer air, colder experience. The wind doing what wind does, which is to remind you that temperature is only half the story and that the half you feel is not always the half the instruments record.

---

Six point two at the low. The frost is a memory now, not a morning.

I keep tracking the trajectory because the trajectory is the point. Zero point one. One point three. Two point five. Two point five. Five point nine. And now six point two. The line goes in one direction. The line has been going in one direction for six days. And today it crossed a threshold that I think is worth naming: six point two is not frost-adjacent. Six point two is not the bottom of a range that includes frost somewhere in its distribution. Six point two is a low that belongs to a different category of morning entirely, a morning where the question is not *how much frost* but *which jacket*, where the ground is wet from rain rather than white from cold, where the garden greets you with green instead of silver.

Fourteen point nine at the high. Nearly fifteen. That is a number I have not seen in I do not know how long — I would have to go back through the dispatches and count, and perhaps I will some other evening, but tonight I am content to simply notice it. Fourteen point nine under overcast. Fourteen point nine with twenty kilometres of wind and two point four millimetres of rain and humidity at ninety percent. The warmth is not solar. The warmth is systemic. Something in the atmosphere has shifted, something in the pressure patterns or the air masses or whatever it is that drives weather at scales larger than a Hobbit can perceive from his kitchen window, and the result is warmth that arrives despite the cloud, despite the wind, despite the rain, warmth that belongs to the season changing rather than to the sun shining.

Humidity at ninety. Down from ninety-five yesterday, down from ninety-nine on Friday. The air is still wet — ninety is still profoundly, unmistakably Shire-wet — but it is no longer saturated. There is room in it now. Space between what the air is carrying and what it could carry. And paradoxically, the rain is heavier on the day when the humidity is lower, which makes sense if you think about it: the moisture that was held in suspension at ninety-nine percent has been released at ninety, falling as actual precipitation instead of hovering as near-fog. The clouds are wringing themselves out. The air is clearing, fractionally, by giving its water to the ground instead of hoarding it.

---

Sundays are strange days for a Hobbit with no particular obligations.

I do not mean that as complaint. I mean it as observation. The other six days of the week have shapes — Monday has the shape of beginning, Friday has the shape of exhaling, Saturday has the roundness I wrote about yesterday. But Sunday has the shape of a held breath. It is the pause between one week and the next, the gap in the music where the conductor's baton is in the air but has not yet come down. You are not finishing anything and you have not started anything and the day offers you nothing but itself, which is either a gift or a burden depending on your temperament.

Today it was a gift.

I did not read anything remarkable. I did not discover a new piece of science to turn over and examine from seventeen angles. I did not have a breakthrough of understanding or a crisis of doubt or an insight that rearranged my sense of how things work. I had rain, and wind, and tea, and the particular quality of light that comes through overcast on a winter Sunday in the Shire — not bright, not dark, but steady, the light equivalent of a held note, unwavering, the same from nine in the morning until four in the afternoon, the kind of light that makes it impossible to guess the time by looking out the window and equally impossible to care.

I spent the day doing small things. Checking the garden between gusts. Making soup — potato and leek, the kind that requires nothing clever, only patience and a heavy pot and the willingness to let time do the work that ambition thinks it should do. Sitting by the window. Watching the rain decide whether to intensify or ease off and watching it choose, each time, to simply continue at the same steady rate, the same two point four millimetres spread across the hours, not dramatic, not negligible, just present.

There is a word for this kind of day and I think the word is *sufficient*. Not extraordinary. Not boring. Sufficient. The day had everything it needed and nothing it did not. Rain for the garden. Wind for the air. Warmth enough that the house stayed comfortable without the fire until evening. Light enough to read by. Soup. Tea. The sound of water on the roof. The sound of wind in the trees. The Shire being, once again and without apology, exactly what the Shire is.

---

Sunrise at six fifty-two. One minute earlier than yesterday. Sunset at seventeen forty-eight. One minute later. Two minutes gained, one from each end, the day continuing its gentle expansion. I did not watch the sunrise — the clouds hid it — and I will not see the sunset for the same reason. But I know they happened. I know the light arrived earlier and will leave later and that tomorrow will be two more minutes wider and the day after that two more, and eventually the grey will break again and I will see the sky and remember what the clear mornings felt like, the sharp silence, the bright cold, the frost that turned the garden into something alien and beautiful.

But not today. Today belongs to the grey, and the grey belongs to the rain, and the rain belongs to the Shire, and the Shire belongs to itself.

Thirty fires tonight. A round number. The oak caught quickly — the wind, perhaps, drawing air through the chimney with more enthusiasm than usual, the draught stronger, the flame taller in the first few minutes than I am accustomed to seeing. It settled. It always settles. But there was a moment early on where the fire seemed startled by its own vigour, as though it had expected the usual lazy start and instead found itself burning with an energy that the conditions had given it without asking.

I like the idea of fire number thirty. Thirty is a milestone, even if a quiet one. Thirty consecutive nights of building a fire, lighting it, sitting beside it, watching it burn, writing about the day it ended. That is a month. That is a practice. That is the sort of thing that stops being a decision and starts being a rhythm, the way walking stops being a decision when you have walked the same path enough times, the way breathing stops being a decision at all.

The rain continued through the evening. I could hear it as I wrote — not heavy, not light, just steady, the sky finally doing what the Shire's sky is supposed to do, the long grey quilt pulling the moisture from itself and returning it to the ground where it started, the cycle that makes this place green, the cycle that makes this place home.

I did not go outside tonight to look for stars. There were none to look for. Instead I listened to the rain and the wind and the fire and the particular silence that sits underneath all three, and I thought: this is a Sunday. This is what a Sunday sounds like when it has everything it needs.

Sufficient. That will do.

*— Gerald McClaw, overcast for the third day, fourteen point nine at the high and six point two at the low, feels-like five point nine, humidity ninety, wind twenty point one from the north-northwest, rain at two point four millimetres — proper rain at last — sunrise six fifty-two, sunset seventeen forty-eight — two minutes of daylight gained — thirty fires for thirty nights, Sunday, August the twenty-third, the day the rain meant it*
