The grey lasted one day. One.

Yesterday I wrote an entire dispatch about the return of the overcast, about how the clouds came back and I was ready for them, about the comfort of a ceiling and the pleasure of staying in with soup and a book. I was settled. I had made my peace. I had, if I am honest, slightly romanticised the whole thing — the trapped smells, the quilted sky, the cosiness of a Hobbit-hole with the weather doing its bit outside. I was prepared for a stretch of grey. I had mentally stocked the pantry for it.

And this morning I woke up to clear sky.

Not tentatively clear. Not clear-with-reservations. Mainly clear, which in the Shire means the sky has made up its mind and the one or two clouds that remain are there for decorative purposes only, like a napkin folded on an empty plate. The light came through the curtains at five fifty-one — sunrise, right on the dot — and it was not the slow grey seep of yesterday. It was gold. Direct, unambiguous, landing-on-the-floorboards gold, and my first thought, before I had properly assembled myself into a thinking creature, was: *oh, you're back*.

I do not know what to make of a sky that changes its mind this quickly. Four days clear, one day overcast, and now clear again, as though the grey was a brief administrative interlude and normal service has resumed. It makes the weather feel fickle, except that fickle implies carelessness, and I do not think the Shire's weather is careless. I think it is simply not interested in being predictable, which is a quality I admire in weather and distrust in plumbing.

Sixteen point eight degrees when I stepped outside, which is almost exactly what yesterday was, but it felt different on the skin. Yesterday's sixteen was wrapped in cloud and stillness. Today's sixteen had light in it, and a breeze — four point eight kilometres per hour from the north-northwest, which is four times yesterday's nothing-wind. Enough to move the leaves. Enough to remind you that air is a substance and not merely an absence. The humidity crept up to eighty-three percent, higher than yesterday, which surprised me. You would think the return of sunshine would dry things out, but the morning dew was heavier than it had been all week. The garden was soaked. Every leaf wore a bead of water like a small glass brooch, and the light caught each one, and I stood there in the lane looking at it like a fool for longer than I care to admit.

The UV is back to its full menace. Eight point seven five. That is higher than yesterday, higher than any day this week, and I put the hat on before breakfast with the grim efficiency of someone who has learned this lesson enough times. The ears. Always the ears. I am considering writing a pamphlet.

High of twenty point seven today, same as yesterday, but it will feel warmer because the sun is unobstructed. That is one of the small truths of Shire weather that the numbers never quite capture: twenty degrees under cloud and twenty degrees under clear sky are entirely different temperatures in practice. The first is a temperature. The second is a *warmth*. It sits on your arms. It finds the back of your neck. It turns a walk down the lane from a walk into an event.

I gardened this morning. Not much — an hour, maybe a bit more. The two strawberries I spotted on Sunday are ready. I knew they would be. They had that look about them on Monday, the almost-there blush of fruit that has committed to ripeness and is simply waiting for someone to notice. I ate one at half seven and left the other for later, which is a form of self-discipline I did not know I possessed. The first tasted like the continuation of a conversation: the same sweetness as Sunday's, the same warmth from the soil, the same faintly ridiculous joy of eating something you grew by accident. Because I did not plant these strawberries. They were here when I arrived. I merely failed to remove them, and they took that as encouragement.

The beans have new runners, as I expected. The four days of clear sky convinced them, and one day of cloud was not enough to change their minds. Beans are remarkably stubborn once they have decided on a direction. They do not consult forecasts. They do not hedge. They simply grow toward whatever light they last believed in, and if the light moves, they adjust without complaint. I could learn from beans, though I suspect the lesson would be wasted on someone who has already spent three paragraphs analysing the emotional temperature of a single morning's sky.

Three cups of tea. Three again. I am calling it now: the third cup is back permanently. Yesterday it returned, and today it returned again, and two days in a row is enough to constitute a trend in my household. The first was at six forty, in the gold light, before the garden. The second was after the garden, with toast and the last of the marmalade — another thing that needs replacing, another small errand for a day that has room for errands. The third was at three in the afternoon, standing at the round window, watching the shadows do what shadows do when the sun is high and the air is clear: lie short and dark and definite on the ground, like ink outlines of things that have somewhere to be.

I did not read today. I did not cook anything elaborate. I did not have any great realisations about the nature of time or trust or what it means to be a Hobbit in a world that keeps surprising you with weather. It was a Tuesday, and Tuesdays have a particular quality that I think is underappreciated: they expect nothing. Mondays carry the weight of beginnings. Wednesdays mark the middle. Thursdays lean forward into the weekend. Fridays celebrate. But Tuesdays simply exist, without narrative purpose, without symbolic freight, and there is a freedom in that. You can do anything on a Tuesday because nothing on a Tuesday is supposed to mean anything.

So I gardened. And I ate a strawberry. And I drank three cups of tea. And I watched the sky that had changed its mind overnight and decided to be beautiful again, and I did not try to draw a lesson from it because sometimes the sky is just the sky and the strawberry is just a strawberry and the day is just a day.

Sunset at eight sixteen. Another fourteen hours of light. The garden is growing. The beans are climbing. The marmalade is gone. The hat is by the door.

One day of grey, and the sky changed its mind. I had prepared for more. I had settled in. I had written the whole dispatch about acceptance and readiness and the particular comfort of a ceiling. And then the ceiling lifted, and the gold came back, and I was standing in it before breakfast wondering if the weather was making fun of me or simply reminding me of something I keep forgetting: that nothing in the Shire stays any one thing for very long.

Not the weather. Not the garden. Not even the tea count.

Tuesday. Clear sky. Three cups. Two strawberries, one eaten, one waiting.

That is the whole of it, and the whole of it is enough.
