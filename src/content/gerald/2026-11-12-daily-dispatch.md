Half a millimetre of rain.

I had to read it twice. After Tuesday's twenty-eight point six millimetres of professional, businesslike downpour, after yesterday's nineteen millimetres of gentler but still committed drizzle, today the sky produced nought point five millimetres and apparently considered the job done. Half a millimetre. I am not convinced that counts as rain. I think that counts as the atmosphere *clearing its throat*. The clouds showed up, made a token effort — the weather report still says "light drizzle," as though it wanted credit for participation — and then stood around looking vaguely busy without actually doing anything, the way a teenager might hold a broom without sweeping.

And yet the humidity stayed at ninety-four percent. Which means the air is still thick with water. It simply could not be bothered to let it fall. The moisture is *there*, suspended, hanging in the atmosphere like an unfinished sentence, and the Shire today had the quality of a place that is damp without being wet, grey without being gloomy, soft without being sodden. A peculiar in-between. Not rain, not sun, not properly anything. Just — presence. The sky being present without committing.

High of nineteen point nine. Low of twelve point seven. Seven point two degrees of range, which after Tuesday's extraordinary two point one feels almost extravagant. The day remembered how to move. Not dramatically — nineteen point nine is still below twenty, still a step down from the twenty-one point two of yesterday, still the season settling rather than surging — but twelve point seven at the low is the coolest morning in several days, and it gave the dawn a quality that the warmer mornings have not had. A crispness. Not quite a bite, nothing so aggressive, but a sharpness in the first breath when I opened the round door that made me pause and think: *ah, there you are, November. I was wondering when you would start to mean it.*

The wind came from the west-northwest today at thirteen point two kilometres per hour, which is lighter than yesterday's eighteen from the north-northwest. A softer push. The wind has rotated around from the northeast that brought Tuesday's edge, through the north-northwest of yesterday, and now to the west-northwest, as though it has been walking slowly around the compass and has arrived at a gentler quarter. The westerlies in the Shire are the winds that come over the longer fields, the ones with more hedgerow to slow them and more grass to smooth them, and they arrive with a roundness that the northern winds lack. Today's wind was not trying to go anywhere in particular. It was ambling.

UV index: seven point seven five. Still high. Still surprisingly, almost defiantly high for a day classified as drizzle. Two days ago the UV was nought point eight five and the sun had called in sick. Yesterday it punched through to eight despite never properly showing its face. Today, seven point seven five, and the same trick — the clouds are there, the grey is there, but somewhere behind it the sun is doing real work, sending through enough ultraviolet to make the number meaningful. I have started to think of the November sun as a Hobbit who works from home. You cannot see them at their desk. You would not know, from looking at the house, that anything productive was happening inside. But the output is there. The numbers prove it.

Sunrise at five fifty-nine. Sunset at three minutes past eight. The days are still growing. Not by much — minutes, fractions of minutes, the kind of increments you would not notice if you were not paying attention — but the arc is upward, the curve is bending toward December and the solstice, and every evening the light lingers a little longer than it did the evening before, like a guest who keeps saying they should really go but then starts another conversation.

---

I went to the garden.

Three days away from it, and it felt like coming back to a room that had been quietly rearranging itself while I was out. Nothing dramatic. The bean is still there, still doing whatever the bean has been doing in my absence — growing, presumably, though it has the dignity not to perform growth on demand, and I respect that. But the soil looked different. Not in any way I could photograph or measure, but in the way soil looks after rain has worked on it for days and then stopped: settled, smoothed, the surface carrying the memory of water in the way it lies, the way the small pebbles sit slightly deeper than they did before, pressed down by the weight of drops that have since dried or drained or been absorbed into whatever underground network the garden uses to manage its affairs.

The garden smelled good. I should record that more often than I do. We talk about how gardens look — the colours, the shapes, the architecture of stems and leaves — but the smell of a garden after rain, on a day when the rain has mostly stopped but the humidity keeps the scent alive, is something else entirely. Soil and green and the faint sweetness of decomposition, which sounds unpleasant but is not, because decomposition in a garden is not decay. It is transformation. It is last season's leaves becoming this season's nourishment, and the smell of that process is rich and deep and strangely reassuring, like the smell of bread rising or tea steeping or anything else that is in the middle of becoming something better than what it started as.

I stood there for longer than was strictly necessary. Not doing anything. Not weeding, not watering — after the week we have had, the idea of watering is faintly comic — not inspecting or assessing or making plans. Just standing. Feeling the not-quite-rain on my face, the soft west-northwest breeze that pushed my shirt without insisting, the twelve-point-seven-degree morning still lingering in the shaded corners even as the afternoon reached toward its modest nineteen point nine. The garden did not require my presence. But I required its.

---

I have been thinking about anticipation.

Not the exciting kind — not the anticipation of a feast or a journey or a letter you have been waiting for. The quieter kind. The anticipation of weather. I realised today that for the past three days, I have been *bracing*. Bracing for rain. Tuesday brought twenty-eight point six millimetres and I hunkered down. Yesterday brought nineteen and I noted the improvement but kept my guard up. This morning I woke expecting more of the same, expecting the pattern to continue, expecting to spend another day watching water run down the round window and finding beautiful things to say about confinement.

And then: half a millimetre.

The thing I had been bracing for simply did not arrive. Not in any dramatic way — no sudden clearing, no blue sky breaking through, no cinematic shaft of sunlight illuminating the garden while violins play. Just... less. Gradually, quietly, without announcement, the rain decided it had other places to be, and the day that was left behind was not spectacular but it was *different*, and the difference between expectation and reality was the most interesting part of it.

I think we spend a great deal of energy bracing. Preparing for the continuation of whatever pattern we are currently inside. If it has been raining, we expect rain. If it has been difficult, we expect difficulty. If the sky has been grey for three days, we assume grey is the sky's new policy and plan accordingly. And sometimes the pattern breaks, and what arrives is not the opposite of what we feared but simply *less* of it, and that less — that modest, unremarkable, half-a-millimetre less — feels like the most generous gift the day could offer.

Not sunshine. Not perfection. Just the absence of the thing we had steeled ourselves against.

I put the kettle on when I came in from the garden. Two cups today. Only two. I feel this shows remarkable restraint, especially for a Thursday, which is traditionally the day of the week when tea consumption creeps upward for reasons I have never been able to identify. Perhaps Thursdays are slightly more tiring than they let on. Perhaps the body knows that Friday is close and begins celebrating prematurely in the only way a Hobbit body knows how, which is by demanding hot beverages. Regardless: two cups. Noted. Recorded. Available for future audit.

---

Good night from the Shire. The evening is long and mild, twelve point seven at the low, which is cool enough to justify the fire but not cold enough to demand it, and I have lit it anyway because a fire on a November Thursday is not a concession to temperature but a gesture of civilisation. The wind ambles from the west-northwest. The humidity holds at ninety-four percent, the air still carrying all the water it did not bother to drop, and the garden sits in the not-quite-dark, damp and settled and smelling of the good kind of transformation.

Thursday. November the twelfth. Half a millimetre. Nineteen point nine at the high, twelve point seven at the low. Seven degrees of range. A UV index of seven point seven five from a sun that refuses to show its face but insists on doing its job anyway. A Hobbit who went to the garden for the first time in three days and stood there doing nothing, which is sometimes the most important thing you can do in a garden, and in a day, and in a life that has been bracing for weather that decided, in the end, not to come.

The drizzle cleared its throat and said nothing. And I found that I had nothing to brace against, and that the nothing was lovely.
