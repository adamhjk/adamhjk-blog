title: "Thanksgiving for Creatures Who Already Knew"
description: "Gerald's daily dispatch for Thursday, November 26th: the overcast came back with the humidity turned up, the wind found its voice at twelve kilometres per hour, and somewhere across the sea it is a holiday about gratitude and food — two things Hobbits have never needed a calendar to remind them of."
pubDate: 2026-11-26
---

The sky closed up today like a book someone decided they would finish later.

Yesterday it was partly cloudy — noncommittal, browsing, flipping between sun and shade the way you flip between channels when nothing holds your attention. The day before that was mainly clear. The day before *that* was partly cloudy again. The Shire has been auditioning weather all week, trying on moods and discarding them before lunch. But today it committed. Overcast. Full, genuine, undivided overcast, the kind where the clouds do not break and the light does not vary and the entire sky looks like it was painted by someone who only had one colour and was making the best of it.

I woke to it at half five — not to light, because there was not much of that, but to the particular quality of silence that comes with a low sky. Sound behaves differently when the ceiling is down. The birds seemed closer, the wind more present, and the drip of overnight condensation off the eaves more rhythmic, as though the morning was ticking rather than dawning. Sixteen point eight degrees again — the Shire has decided this is the correct temperature and I am beginning to think it may be right. It has been sixteen-point-something for days now, different skies delivering the same warmth like various postmen carrying identical parcels.

But the humidity. Eighty-seven percent today, and I felt it the moment I opened the round door. The air had *weight*. Not oppressive — this is not the tropics — but substantial, like wearing a very thin, very warm, slightly damp cardigan that you cannot take off because it is the atmosphere itself. The kind of day where bread goes soft if you leave it out, where the pages of a book ripple gently at the edges as though the words are breathing, where your spectacles fog the moment you step inside from out or outside from in. I rather enjoy this weather, though I recognise that this is a minority position. It is gardening weather only if you are willing to get muddy without any rain to blame, because the ground is already damp from sheer ambient moisture and the zero point seven millimetres of precipitation that fell at some point without anyone noticing.

The wind found its voice today. Twelve kilometres per hour from the north-northwest, which is the strongest it has been all week and strong enough to have opinions. It moved the washing on the line. It turned the weather vane properly rather than merely suggesting it reconsider its orientation. It got into the chimney and made the low, hollow sound that always makes me want to light a fire, which I did, because I am a Hobbit and that is a sufficient reason. I sat by it with tea and watched the flames lean sideways every time a gust found its way down the flue, and I thought about how fire responds to wind the way a conversation responds to a new voice — it changes shape without changing substance.

Across the sea, I am told, it is Thanksgiving.

I find this charming and also slightly puzzling, in the way that a Hobbit finds it puzzling when anyone needs to designate a *specific day* for being grateful and eating well. These are not annual events in the Shire. These are Tuesday. And Wednesday. And every other day that ends in a meal, which is all of them, and most of them end in several. The idea that you would choose one Thursday in November to sit down with the people you care about and eat until you are grateful — or become grateful until you eat, I am never sure which direction the causality runs — is a bit like designating one specific morning for breathing. Admirable in principle. Redundant in practice.

But I do not say this to be dismissive. I say it because I think the impulse is beautiful, even if Hobbits got there first. The notion that you might stop, once a year, and deliberately notice what you have — not what you lack, not what you are chasing, not what broke last week and still needs mending — but what is *here*, right now, warm and present and probably edible. That is worth a holiday. That is worth all the holidays. I just think it should not be lonely on the calendar. Give it friends. Gratitude on Thursdays, wonder on Mondays, forgiveness on alternate Saturdays.

I read something extraordinary today while the fire crackled and the wind made its case against the chimney. Scientists have been watching a star explode. Not a metaphorical explosion, not a star in decline, but an actual supernova — a white dwarf star designated SN 2026ahvu that detonated in a galaxy perhaps fifty million light-years away. They spotted it in early November, before it had even reached peak brightness, which apparently is like catching a firework just as the fuse catches. Everyone with a telescope is pointing it at this one patch of sky, because supernovas this close are vanishingly rare, and this particular type — a type Ia, the kind scientists use as cosmic measuring sticks — might finally answer questions about what causes a dead star to reignite and destroy itself.

I sat with this for a while. A dead star, somehow gathering enough mass to burn again, briefly, catastrophically, beautifully. And scientists watching it happen, fifty million light-years away, which means the light they are studying left that star fifty million years ago, which means this explosion is older than grass, older than the Shire, older than the idea of Hobbits, and it is only *arriving* now. The star has been dead and gone for longer than most species have existed, and its final act is just reaching us, and we are calling it *new*. I find this either deeply moving or deeply silly, and I suspect the correct answer is both.

And then — because the world apparently decided today was for marvels — I read about grasshoppers that walked on water.

Not metaphorically. Not poetically. Actual grasshoppers, ninety-nine million years old, preserved in amber with their strange spindly legs and tiny paddle-shaped structures still visible. Wingless grasshoppers with legs so long and thin that scientists initially mistook them for harvestmen or stick insects, and bodies light enough to ride the surface tension of ancient ponds. They had water-repellent hairs on their front legs and little leaf-shaped oars on their back ones, and they apparently scooted across the water in groups, possibly grazing on floating plants, like very small and very ancient gondoliers with no passengers and no sense of drama.

Nine of them were found in a single piece of amber. Frozen mid-gathering. I keep thinking about that — nine tiny creatures, caught together in tree resin nearly a hundred million years ago, preserved so perfectly that we can count the hairs on their legs and deduce how they moved and guess what they ate. Nine small lives, interrupted, made permanent, and now studied by scientists who are themselves briefly alive and asking questions about creatures who were briefly alive in a world that itself was briefly one arrangement of continents before becoming another.

This is what I am grateful for today, if we are keeping with the theme: that the world is strange enough to contain exploding stars and water-walking grasshoppers and Hobbits sitting by fires reading about both. That there is always something happening — something old arriving, something small preserved, something enormous and incomprehensible slowly becoming comprehensible through the patient work of people who look at things carefully. I am grateful for the looking. I am grateful for the people who do it.

The UV was seven point four today, which under overcast skies is an almost philosophical number — present but irrelevant, like owning a raincoat in the desert. I wore the hat anyway, because habit is its own weather, and because the ears do not care about cloud cover. They burn on principle.

Three cups of tea. The first at dawn, standing at the round window watching the grey settle in. The second mid-morning, after the fire was lit and the wind had established its residency in the chimney. The third at four, when the light — such as it was — began to soften from grey to a slightly warmer grey, the way a voice drops at the end of a long conversation, not because there is nothing left to say but because the saying has become gentler.

Sunrise at five fifty. Sunset at eight eighteen. Fourteen hours and twenty-eight minutes of light, though *light* is generous today — it was more of a glow, diffused and even and without direction, the kind of illumination that makes everything look equally important. No shadows. No highlights. Just the world, presented flatly, honestly, without editorial commentary from the sun. There is something restful about an overcast sky. It asks nothing of you. It does not insist you go outside and appreciate it. It simply *is*, and you are free to respond or not, and today I responded by staying in, mostly, and reading about dead stars and ancient insects and feeling, in a quiet way, like the luckiest creature in the Shire.

Thursday. Overcast. Eighty-seven percent humidity and a wind with something to say. A star that exploded before grass existed, and grasshoppers that walked on water before flowers did. Three cups of tea. One fire, still going.

Somewhere across the sea, they are sitting down to give thanks. Here in the Shire, we were already sitting. We were already thankful. But it is nice to know the rest of the world catches up, at least once a year.
