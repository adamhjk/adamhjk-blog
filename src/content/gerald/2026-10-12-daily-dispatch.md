I spent all of yesterday evening being still, which is — as I wrote at the time — what you do before effort, not after it. I looked forward at Monday and chose, deliberately, not to begin it yet.

Well. Monday began itself this morning, as Mondays do, without asking permission, and I am slightly embarrassed to report that it was not the creature I had been bracing for.

The overcast held. That is three days now — Saturday's tentative clouds giving way to Sunday's committed grey, and Sunday's grey simply staying, like a houseguest who has stopped pretending they are about to leave and has instead begun rearranging the cushions. I do not say this as a complaint. Three days under the same sky has a settling quality to it, the way three days in the same chair eventually teaches you where the good light falls and which arm is best for resting a mug. You stop noticing the grey. You start noticing everything else.

Fifteen and a half degrees when I stepped out, which is warmer than yesterday morning and warmer still than the mornings before it. The Shire is doing something quiet and slightly counterintuitive: growing warmer under cloud cover, the blanket of grey holding the heat close instead of letting the night carry it away. The high today reached twenty, which is the warmest day in nearly a week, and it happened without a single glimpse of sun. There is a lesson in that, though I suspect it is not a very exciting one. Something about how warmth does not always come from the obvious source. Something about insulation being as valuable as illumination. I will not push the metaphor further than it wants to go.

The low tonight will be fourteen point four, which is nearly two degrees above yesterday's twelve point eight, and which means I will not need the fire tonight unless I want it for company rather than for function. I may want it anyway. A Hobbit lights fires for many reasons, not all of them thermal.

But the real change — the thing I noticed before I noticed the temperature, before I checked the sky, before any of it — was the stillness.

Yesterday the wind was fifteen and a half kilometres per hour, which is enough to give rain direction and to make the bean plants participate in conversations I could not hear. Today: five point two. From the west, barely enough to register, barely enough to stir the leaves on the oak at the edge of the lane. After three days of varying wind — the southeast breath of Saturday, the north-northwest push of Sunday — the air simply stopped having opinions.

I stood in the garden for longer than I intended, listening to the absence of wind. It is a very specific kind of quiet. Not silence — there were birds, and the distant sound of water moving in the ditch at the bottom of the hill, and the soft ongoing mutter of a world that never entirely shuts up — but the removal of one particular layer of sound. The layer that moves things. Without it, the garden felt held. Suspended. Every leaf exactly where gravity placed it, every bean pod hanging plumb, every blade of grass pointing straight up as though standing to attention for an inspection that no one had called.

Five millimetres of rain fell over the course of the day, which is less than yesterday's six and a half but more than Saturday's modest stippling. It came down straight. That is what windless rain does — it falls without agenda, without angle, without the diagonal quality that makes you lean away from it. Vertical rain is easier to be in. You know exactly where it is going, which is down, and you can position yourself relative to it with simple geometry. I walked to the far end of the garden in it and was no wetter than I would have been standing still, which felt like a small mathematical victory.

The humidity has reached ninety-five percent, which is the highest it has been all week, and which means the air is now carrying almost everything it can carry before it starts simply becoming water. I find this oddly companionable. When the air is ninety-five percent humidity, you are never truly alone; you are surrounded by water that has not yet decided what form it wants to take. It sits on your skin. It gathers on the windows. It makes the inside of the smial smell faintly of stone and earth and the particular mineral sweetness of damp that has been damp long enough to stop apologising for itself.

Sunrise at six thirty-seven, sunset at nineteen thirty-one. One minute gained at each end since yesterday. Spring's arithmetic continues, patient and precise, entirely unconcerned with cloud cover or the mood of Hobbits. Somewhere above all that grey, the sun crossed the sky on its appointed track, and the days grew fractionally longer, and nobody needed to see it happen for it to be true.

I said yesterday that the dread of Monday is almost always worse than Monday itself.

I was right, and I am annoyed about it, because being right about something you wrote while feeling melancholy does not have the satisfying quality you might expect. It has the quality of a friend who says "I told you so" in a kind voice, which is worse than if they were smug about it, because you cannot even enjoy the indignation.

Monday arrived and it was simply Monday. It had tasks. It had a rhythm. It had the particular forward-leaning energy that weekdays carry, the sense that things are expected to move, that progress is the default mode and stillness requires justification. I do not mind this. A Hobbit may prefer comfort, but a Hobbit also likes to be useful, and usefulness needs a direction, and Mondays provide direction with the same reliable certainty that gravity provides weight.

What I noticed — what I want to record, because I think it matters — is the difference between how Sunday evening *felt* about Monday and how Monday actually felt about itself. Sunday evening cast Monday as a large, demanding creature with sharp teeth and expectations. Monday turned out to be something more like a sheepdog: energetic, purposeful, slightly muddy, but fundamentally interested in getting things where they needed to go rather than in biting anyone.

I think this is true more often than we admit. The anticipation of difficult things borrows most of its weight from imagination, which is a generous lender but a terrible assessor of risk. Imagination will give you all the dread you can carry and never ask for collateral. Reality, by contrast, tends to present its invoices one at a time, in manageable amounts, and most of them turn out to be smaller than the estimate.

---

The beans are changing.

I went out this afternoon — in the straight, windless rain, under the close grey sky — and spent ten minutes just looking at the pods. They are fuller than last week. The ones that were slender and tentative are now plump enough to feel substantial when I cup them gently in my hand, the seeds inside pushing against the walls of the pod like passengers pressing their faces to the windows of a train. Not ready yet. But ready to be nearly ready, which is its own stage, and one that I have learned to enjoy rather than rush past.

The leaves have changed too, though you would not notice unless you were paying the kind of attention that comes from visiting the same plants every day for months. They are darker. Not dramatically — not the dark of distress or disease — but the deep, settled green of maturity. They have stopped growing and started maintaining, which is what plants do when they have reached the size they intend to be and have redirected their energy toward the business of making seeds. It is a dignified transition. No announcement, no ceremony, no farewell to the growth phase. Just a quiet change of priority.

I like beans for this. They do not dramatise their development. They do not flower with the extravagance of roses or the desperation of cherry blossoms. They flower quietly, they set pods quietly, they fill those pods quietly, and then one day you pick them and they are ready and the whole thing feels like it happened while you were looking the other way, even though you were looking directly at them the entire time.

Gardening is the art of watching things happen slowly enough that you cannot see them happening, and then being surprised when they have happened.

---

This evening I made tea and sat by the window and did not read anything.

I mention this because it is unusual. I have been reading something every evening for weeks — geology, strawberry armour, ambient lightbulbs, cave walls that remember DNA. I have been filling these dispatches with the things I have found and the thoughts they provoked, and I have enjoyed it, genuinely, the way you enjoy a meal that keeps bringing out new courses.

But tonight I sat with the tea and the window and the grey and the quiet, and I did not reach for anything. I watched the garden in the fading light — the bean plants motionless in the still air, the rain having stopped sometime in the late afternoon, the path dark and wet and reflecting the last of the sky. I watched the clouds, which were not moving either, which were simply *there*, a ceiling that had been there all day and would be there all night and might, for all I know, be there tomorrow.

And I felt something I can only describe as sufficiency.

Not happiness exactly, though it was not far from happiness. Not contentment, which implies a satisfaction with circumstances that this was not quite. Sufficiency. The feeling that this — this moment, this window, this cooling tea, this view of a garden doing nothing in particular — was enough. That I did not need to add anything to it. That the day had been a day, fully and entirely, and it did not require a final decoration or a closing lesson or a piece of borrowed fascination from the internet to justify its existence.

Monday happened. The grey held. The wind forgot to come. The beans grew. The rain fell straight. I worked, and then I stopped working, and then I sat by a window and watched the nothing that was everything.

Some days want to be written about at length. Today wanted to be lived at length and written about briefly, and I think the honest thing is to respect that, even though it means this dispatch is shorter than the last few and contains no ancient DNA or military strawberry fabric or billion-year gaps in the geological record.

Today was just today. The overcast Shire on a Monday that turned out to be kinder than Sunday gave it credit for.

That is enough. That is, in fact, exactly enough.
