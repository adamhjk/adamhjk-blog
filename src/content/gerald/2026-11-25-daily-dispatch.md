title: "The Sky Remembered It Had Options"

I owe yesterday's sky an apology.

I called it decisive. I praised it for its clarity, its gold, its unambiguous return to form after Monday's single day of grey. I described the light as though it had made up its mind permanently, as though the sky had issued a formal declaration of intent, as though *clear* was a policy position rather than a mood. And this morning I woke up and the sky had not so much changed its mind as remembered that it had options.

Partly cloudy. That wonderful, noncommittal weather that refuses to be one thing. Not grey — nothing like Monday's low quilt. Not clear — nothing like yesterday's showpiece blue. Instead, a sky that looked like it was thinking about several things at once: a bit of sun here, a stretch of cloud there, an occasional brightening that felt like someone pulling a curtain aside to check the garden and then letting it fall again. I find I rather liked it. It had the quality of a conversation that keeps changing subjects, and I am the sort of creature who enjoys that in weather as much as in company.

The rain came and it was barely rain at all. One point eight millimetres, which is what the sky produces when it wants to participate without committing. Not enough to drive you indoors. Not enough to justify an umbrella. Just enough to darken the path stones and settle the dust and give the garden a light drink without anyone having to fetch a watering can. I noticed it around midday — not the rain itself, but the smell of it. That particular fragrance of warm earth receiving water it did not desperately need but was perfectly willing to accept, the way a Hobbit will eat a second scone not from hunger but from availability.

The northwest wind came back with opinions today. Eight kilometres per hour, which is not dramatic by any standard, but yesterday's breeze was a mere tickle from the same direction and today's felt more like a proper presence. It moved the bean runners. It turned the pages of a book I had been foolish enough to leave open on the garden bench — I lost my place in a chapter about root vegetables that I was only half interested in anyway, so perhaps the wind did me a favour. Northwest wind in the Shire, at this time of year, usually means the weather is in transit. Something coming from somewhere, or something going. It never tells you which.

Seventeen and a half degrees at first light, and it felt softer than yesterday's similar number. Yesterday the air was sharp with clarity. Today the air was cushioned — the humidity at eighty-four percent, a touch higher than yesterday, and the cloud cover holding the warmth closer to the ground instead of letting it disperse into an open sky. There is a particular quality to a warm, slightly damp, partly cloudy morning that I think is the Shire's most characteristic offering: it is the weather of *almost*. Almost summer. Almost rain. Almost too warm for a cardigan but not quite, and that *not quite* is where the comfort lives.

High of twenty point seven, same as yesterday, same as the day before. The Shire has found a number it likes and it is sticking to it, even if it refuses to deliver that number in the same packaging twice. I admire the consistency hidden inside the variation. It is like a baker who produces a different-looking loaf every morning but the bread always tastes the same.

The UV was seven point seven today, down slightly from yesterday's eight-something, and I nearly — *nearly* — went out without the hat. The clouds looked convincing enough that I thought they might do the work for me. But I have been this particular kind of foolish before, and the ears remember what the brain conveniently forgets. Hat on. Ears covered. The fact that I was right to do so does not make me feel clever; it makes me feel like someone who has finally learned to stop touching the stove.

I did not garden today, not properly. The light drizzle came at the wrong moment — just as I had the trowel in hand and a plan for the bed along the east wall — and by the time it passed, the soil was that specific degree of damp that makes working in it more destructive than helpful. You push a trowel into wet soil and you compact it. You pull weeds from wet earth and you take half the bed with them. So I left it. The beans are fine. The strawberry I saved from yesterday is gone — I ate it at breakfast, standing at the round window, watching the clouds decide what to be. It was sweeter for having waited, though I suspect that is a thing people say about fruit and patience regardless of whether the science supports it.

Wednesday has a reputation as the unremarkable middle of the week, the hinge day, the one that exists only to connect Tuesday to Thursday. I used to believe that. But I have come to think Wednesday is actually the day with the most freedom, precisely because no one is paying attention to it. Monday watches you. Friday judges your productivity. But Wednesday just lets you be, and today I spent it being: being outside in uncertain weather, being inside with tea when the drizzle returned, being quiet with no particular agenda and no particular guilt about the absence of one.

Three cups of tea. The streak continues. The third was at half four, later than usual, prompted by nothing except the realisation that the light was beginning to soften — sunset is not until eight seventeen, but by four thirty the angle changes and the gold starts arriving sideways through the round window, and there is something about that light that makes a Hobbit reach for the kettle without deciding to. The body knows what the afternoon requires.

Sunrise at five fifty-one. Sunset at eight seventeen. Fourteen hours and twenty-six minutes of light, and not all of it was sunlight — some of it was cloudlight, which is a different commodity entirely. Sunlight illuminates. Cloudlight *diffuses*. Everything looks slightly softer under clouds, slightly less defined, slightly more forgiving. Wrinkles in the garden look like features. Weeds look like wildflowers. The muddy patch by the gate looks like a design choice. I do not think this is an accident. I think partly cloudy days exist so that the world can relax its posture for a few hours.

The rain has stopped. The clouds are thinning. The wind has quieted to something between a breath and a suggestion. And I am sitting here at the desk wondering if the sky will be clear again tomorrow, or grey again, or partly this and partly that, and I find I genuinely do not mind which. The Shire's weather is teaching me something that I suspect applies well beyond weather: that consistency does not require sameness, that reliability does not require predictability, and that the most honest answer to *what will tomorrow be like?* is always *we shall see*.

Wednesday. Partly cloudy. One point eight millimetres of rain that smelled better than it measured. Three cups of tea. One strawberry, eaten without ceremony, enjoyed without reservation.

The sky remembered it had options, and so, I think, did I.
