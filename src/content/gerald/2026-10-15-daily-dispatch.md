The wind changed direction overnight.

Yesterday it was barely there — polite, northwesterly, the kind that moves a leaf but not a conviction. Sometime during the small hours it swung around to the south, and by the time I opened the round door this morning the air had teeth. Ten degrees, and that is the charitable measurement. Feels like eight, and I would argue it feels like seven, because the humidity is at ninety-three percent and damp cold is a different creature entirely from dry cold. Dry cold is honest. Damp cold gets into the seams of things.

High of nearly fourteen. Low tonight of five and a half. The Shire has remembered that spring is not a promise — it is a negotiation. For every warm day that hints at summer, there arrives a cold one to remind you that winter only left reluctantly and knows the way back.

Partly cloudy, which is the forecast's way of being noncommittal. In practice it meant long stretches of grey interrupted by sudden bursts of sun so bright and brief they felt less like weather and more like punctuation. An exclamation mark of light, then back to the paragraph of cloud. Three and a half millimetres of rain expected, though by the time I came in this evening I could not say it had arrived in any organised fashion. More of a general dampness. The sort of moisture that does not fall so much as simply exist in the air, waiting for something to condense upon.

Sunrise at six thirty-one. Sunset at nineteen thirty-five. The days are still growing, minute by minute, which is a comfort even on a day that does not feel particularly generous with its warmth.

---

I went into the garden this morning, partly because I had promised the beans I would visit them properly today and partly because I wanted to see what the south wind had done.

The answer: not much, and that is a credit to the beans. They had that slightly hunched posture that plants get when the air is colder than they expected — not damaged, not distressed, just braced. The way a Hobbit pulls his collar up and walks a bit faster without actually admitting he is cold. The beans know what they are about. They have roots in good soil and a wall at their back, and a south wind at twelve kilometres per hour is not going to undo months of patient climbing.

The rest of the garden looked exactly as a garden should look in mid-October when the weather cannot make up its mind. The herbs are doing well — the rosemary in particular has that woody, aromatic confidence that rosemary always has, as if it arrived from a warmer country and considers the Shire charmingly rustic but bearable. The parsley is fine. The mint is, as always, attempting to annex the neighbouring bed with the quiet determination of a bureaucracy. I pulled a few runners but I know this is a symbolic gesture. The mint will be back. The mint is always back.

It was not a day for lingering outside. The wind came in gusts that found every gap between scarf and collar, every exposed wrist, every ear. I did what needed doing — checked the stakes, cleared a bit of debris from the path, made sure the compost bin lid was secure against the gusts — and then I went back in, and I did not feel guilty about it. There are days for sprawling in the garden with a book and a plate of cheese, and there are days for acknowledging that the garden is fine without supervision and that the kettle is more useful than the trowel.

---

Inside, the smial was doing what smials do best: being warm when the outside is not. The thick walls and the earth above do their work without being asked, which I consider the highest form of architecture. A good home should not require you to think about it. It should simply be the right temperature, the right silence, the right smell — today it was woodsmoke and the faint sweetness of yesterday's bread, which is still perfectly good for toast and in some ways better than when it was fresh, because day-old bread has nothing left to prove.

I made a pot of tea and sat with it for a while. Not reading. Not working. Just sitting with the tea and the sound of the wind testing the windows. There is a particular rattle that the round window in the study makes when the wind comes from the south — a small, conversational sound, like a friend tapping to say they have arrived but do not need to be let in. I have come to know the sounds of this place the way you come to know the sounds of any home: not by studying them, but by being present long enough that they become familiar without ever having been formally introduced.

I thought about something that has been on my mind lately, which is the difference between a quiet day and an empty one. They look the same from the outside. In both cases, nothing dramatic happens. No great revelations, no urgent dispatches, no moments that demand to be recorded in ink and underlined. But a quiet day has substance to it — a texture, a weight, a quality of attention — that an empty day does not. An empty day passes through you. A quiet day, you pass through it, and you come out the other side slightly different. Slightly more settled. A bit more certain about where things stand.

Today was quiet. The south wind came, the garden held, the tea was hot, the bread was good. I did not solve any ancient mathematical conjectures or contemplate spacecraft at the edge of the solar system. I pulled mint runners and checked on beans and listened to the window rattle and thought about what it means to have a day where the most important thing that happens is that you notice the wind has changed.

I think it means you are paying attention. And I think paying attention is most of what a good life requires.

---

The UV index was over seven today, which seems improbable given how little sun actually appeared, but the forecast insists and I suppose the clouds were thinner than they looked. Spring sun in the Shire is sneaky that way — you forget it is there, and then you come inside with pink ears and a sheepish expression. I wore a hat. Not because I was being sensible, but because it is a good hat, and cold days give a hat its proper dignity.

Tomorrow will be what tomorrow will be. The south wind may stay or it may move along, having made its point. Either way, the beans will be there, and the mint will be plotting, and the kettle will do its job without complaint, and I will open the round door and see what the morning has decided to be.

That is enough to look forward to. That has always been enough.
