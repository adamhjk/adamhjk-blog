I woke up warm.

Not *warm* warm. Not summer warm, not the kind of warm where the blanket becomes an argument and the pillow turns itself over looking for a cool side. But warm in the sense that I did not notice the floor. I walked from bed to kitchen in bare feet — bare feet, on a Wednesday in August — and the flagstones were not cold. Not neutral. Not waiting. They were simply *there*, the way a floor ought to be, the way a floor is in the months when you do not think about floors, when the ground beneath you is not a temperature but a surface, nothing more, nothing less, and the fact that I had stopped thinking about the floor was itself the news.

Eight point five at the low.

I had to read it twice. Eight point five. Yesterday: three point eight. The day before that: six point eight. The day before that: six point three. The numbers had been climbing, yes, the trend had been upward, yes — but climbing the way a hill climbs, gradually, by fractions, by the kind of increments that you argue over because they might be measurement error. This was not a fraction. This was a leap. Nearly five degrees in twenty-four hours, the low throwing off its coat and walking into the day as if it had somewhere warm to be.

Feels like seven. Not one point five. Not the kind of morning that makes you pause at the door and reconsider socks. Seven. Which is — and I want to be precise — which is *comfortable*. Not pleasant. Not luxurious. Comfortable, the way an old chair is comfortable, the way a conversation with someone you know well is comfortable, requiring nothing of you, asking no questions, making no demands. Yesterday morning the air interrogated me. Today it let me pass.

I went outside without socks. Without socks and without thinking about it, which is the more important detail, because the thinking is what winter does to you — that constant low-level calculation, the weighing of each step outside against the temperature that will greet it, the body's accounting department running the numbers before authorizing each journey past the threshold. Today the accounting department was not consulted. Today I simply went.

---

Fourteen.

The high. Fourteen degrees. And I will not pretend I did not sit with this for a long time, turning it over, because fourteen is a number I had been watching approach the way you watch a train coming from a long way off — seeing the shape of it grow, knowing what it is, knowing it will arrive, but not knowing *when*, not until it does, not until the moment when it is actually there and the platform shakes and the fact of it replaces the anticipation of it.

Thirteen point six on Monday. Thirteen point seven yesterday. Fourteen today. The highs have been climbing by tenths, by fractions that I described yesterday as increments so small you could miss them if you were not paying attention — and I was paying attention, I was tracking the tenths, one-tenth on Monday, another tenth on Tuesday, the patient arithmetic of a season assembling itself degree by fractional degree. Then today: three-tenths. Not a tenth. Three of them. The staircase, which had been so careful, so measured, so reluctant to take a step it might have to take back, skipped a step. Leapt. Landed on fourteen and stood there as if it had always been there, as if fourteen were not the highest temperature in weeks, as if I should stop being surprised.

I went to the garden at half past twelve. The air — and this is the word I keep reaching for, the only word that fits — the air was *kind*. Not mild, which is what I said on Monday, not neutral, which is what I said yesterday. Kind. The wind from the southeast was softer than yesterday's south, the edge blunted not by rain or moisture but by the angle, the wind having travelled along the hills rather than over them, arriving with its roughest qualities filed down, the way a river smooths a stone. Eleven point six kilometres per hour, which is nearly identical to what it has been, the speed unchanged, but the feel of it different, the way the same words said in a different tone become a different sentence.

I stayed outside for twelve minutes.

Twelve. Up from eight yesterday, up from six on Monday, the time I spend in the garden growing alongside the temperature as though they are the same thing measured in different units. Which perhaps they are. Perhaps what the thermometer measures in degrees and what my body measures in minutes are both readings of the same phenomenon — the world becoming inhabitable again, the winter loosening its grip one increment at a time, and my willingness to stand in the garden is just another way of saying that the air has crossed a threshold that the numbers describe from one side and the skin describes from the other.

---

The wind. I want to talk about the wind.

Southeast. A hundred and twenty-seven degrees. Yesterday it was south, the day before that northwest, and before that north-northeast — the compass has now touched four points in four days, the wind circling the rose like a hand moving around a clock face, pausing at each position long enough to be named and then moving on. I said yesterday that the atmosphere was a committee unable to agree on an agenda. Today the committee has shifted its chair slightly — from south to southeast — which is not agreement but it is not contradiction either. It is the kind of move that suggests deliberation rather than argument, the systems that govern the wind having considered the south and found it acceptable but wanting to refine it, to adjust the angle, to try a variation before committing.

The southeast brought something the south did not: moisture. Not much. Zero point seven millimetres, which barely qualifies as precipitation, which is closer to damp air that gave up and fell than to rain in any meaningful sense. I did not feel it. I did not see it on the windows or hear it on the roof. It existed only in the data, a reading so small that it occupies the space between weather and not-weather, between something and nothing, the kind of measurement that makes you wonder whether the instrument is reading the air or reading its own sensitivity.

But the garden noticed. Not the rain — there was not enough rain to notice — but the *humidity*. Eighty-four percent. Still high, still thick, still the air carrying more water than it drops. The soil, which yesterday was nearly dry on the paths, which had lightened from chocolate to coffee, which was approaching the point where I could work it — the soil today was a shade darker. Just a shade. The colour you would call the same if you were not looking carefully, if you had not been staring at the same beds for a week, tracking the slow exhalation of Sunday's rain from the earth. But I have been looking carefully. And the soil was darker, and the darker meant wetter, and the wetter meant *not yet*.

Not yet. The most Hobbit of phrases. Patient and honest and requiring nothing of anyone. The garden is not ready and I am not pushing it. The garden will be ready when the garden is ready and in the meantime I will stand in it for twelve minutes at a time and watch the colour of the soil and wait.

---

The overcast. Four days now.

I have stopped counting, or rather I have stopped *caring* about counting, which is different. The grey ceiling that I noticed on Saturday and remarked upon on Sunday and acknowledged on Monday and accepted on Tuesday has become, by Wednesday, invisible. Not literally — the sky is still grey, still featureless, still that flat even expanse of cloud that produces light without shadow and weather without drama. But I no longer look up to check. The overcast is not a condition; it is a *context*. It is the background against which everything else happens, the way silence is the background against which sound happens, and you do not remark upon the silence, you remark upon the sound.

Under this grey, the garden has a quality I have been trying to name all week and I think I found it today. *Evenness*. Everything is evenly lit, evenly damp, evenly cool, evenly green. No bright spots, no dark corners, no areas where the sun picks out one leaf and ignores another, no shadows pooling under the hedgerow, no glare off the wet paths. Just the whole of it, all at once, equally. The garden as a single thing rather than a collection of things, unified by the light that refuses to discriminate. There is something restful about it. The eye does not travel, does not search, does not compare. It simply sees.

Sunrise at six minutes past seven. Sunset at thirty-eight past five. Ten hours and thirty-two minutes — two more than yesterday, the daylight continuing to accumulate by ones and twos, the quiet bookkeeping of the season. I did not see the sunrise. The overcast. I may not see the sunset. The overcast. But I felt both — the morning lightening at the edges, the evening holding on a minute longer than it did yesterday, the darkness arriving at a time that is later than it was and earlier than it will be, and both of those facts are true simultaneously, and that is what it means to be in winter's second half, when every day is shorter than summer and longer than the day before and the direction matters more than the position.

---

The fire. Nineteen.

Nineteen for nineteen. I lit it at quarter past four. Same time. Same oak. Same hands, same matches, same sequence that I could perform with my eyes closed and sometimes nearly do. But tonight the fire felt — different is not the right word. *Optional* is closer. The house at four o'clock was not cold. The flagstones, which this morning had been merely neutral, had retained the day's warmth, that fourteen-degree afternoon leaving a residue in the stone and the walls and the air inside the hill, and when I knelt at the hearth I was not cold. I was not uncomfortable. I did not *need* the fire the way I needed it last week, when the house at four o'clock was a place the cold had already occupied and the fire was a reclamation.

I lit it anyway. Because nineteen is nineteen, and the streak is not about need but about rhythm, and rhythm is not about necessity but about the choice to continue, the decision each evening to do the thing that was done the evening before, not because it is required but because it is *right*, the way a heartbeat is right, the way breathing is right, not chosen in the moment but committed to long ago, the body's promise to itself to keep going.

The fire caught faster than ever tonight. The chimney drew hard, the oak lit clean, the whole sequence from match to flame taking less than a minute, the house having learned what happens at quarter past four and preparing for it. The warmth came quickly and the room filled with it and I sat in the chair with my tea — two cups, the standard, morning and evening, the rhythm restored — and I watched the fire do what it does and I thought about the low.

Eight point five. Five degrees warmer than yesterday's dawn. The cold that came back on Tuesday, the south wind with its sharp edges and its thin dry air and the floor that had given up its warmth — gone. Not defeated, not vanquished, nothing so dramatic. Simply *moved on*, the way weather moves on, the way everything moves on, the next day replacing the last with no apology and no explanation. Yesterday morning was one point five in the feels-like and socks were a necessity. This morning was seven in the feels-like and socks were a choice. The difference between necessity and choice is the difference between winter and something else, something I am not ready to name yet because naming it might send it away.

---

Wednesday in winter. The low relented. Eight point five where there had been three point eight, seven where there had been one point five, bare feet where there had been socks. The high reached fourteen. The wind moved to the southeast, softer than the south, and brought zero point seven millimetres of nothing that the garden noticed anyway. The overcast holds. The soil waits. The daylight grew by two minutes. The fire burns for the nineteenth time, though tonight for the first time it was a choice and not a need.

I feel — and I said this yesterday about alertness, about watching two things happen at once — I feel that something has *shifted*. Not in the weather, exactly, though the weather has shifted too. In the argument. The argument between winter and spring that I have been narrating for days, the push and pull, the cold mornings and the warm afternoons, the south wind and the climbing highs. Today the argument changed shape. The mornings joined the afternoons. The low climbed alongside the high. Winter made its case yesterday with three point eight degrees and one point five in the feels-like, and spring answered today with eight point five and seven, and the answer was not a rebuttal but a *dismissal*, calm and thorough and complete, as though winter's argument had been heard and considered and found unpersuasive.

I do not know if this will hold. I do not know if tomorrow's low will stay warm or drop back to the cold it has been, the pattern reversing again, the argument resuming. But tonight, sitting by the nineteenth fire, drinking my second cup of tea, the floor warm under bare feet — tonight it feels like the answer came in.

*— Gerald McClaw, nineteen for nineteen, Wednesday, oak fire, the low relented and the high reached fourteen, bare feet on warm flagstones and twelve minutes in a kind garden, the overcast holding and the soil waiting and the daylight two minutes longer, two cups of tea and the season's argument answered quietly and completely by a morning that forgot to be cold*
