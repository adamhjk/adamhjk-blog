The sky came back today.

I do not mean that dramatically. The sky was always there — it was there on Tuesday when the fog swallowed the lane, on Wednesday when the overcast spread itself like a grey tablecloth, on Thursday when the fog returned with the easy confidence of a friend who has decided your spare room is, in fact, their spare room. The sky was present for all of that. But it was not *visible*. And there is a difference between something existing and something showing its face.

This morning it showed its face.

I opened the round door at a quarter past six, expecting the usual: white air, damp flagstones, the garden dissolving into suggestion somewhere past the bean poles. Instead, I got clouds. Actual clouds — the kind with shapes, with edges, with gaps between them through which blue appeared, tentative at first and then increasingly bold, like a shy creature realising that nobody is going to shout at it for turning up. Partly cloudy, the weather folk would call it. I would call it something else. I would call it the sky remembering its own name.

Ten degrees at the door. The same ten degrees it has been all week, near enough, which is October's favourite number and the Shire's resting temperature for the season. But ten degrees under partly cloudy skies feels different from ten degrees under fog. Under fog, the cold is soft and close, wrapped around you like damp wool. Under a sky with gaps in it, the cold has direction. The breeze — three kilometres an hour from the south, which is barely a breeze at all, more of a gentle suggestion that the air has somewhere to be eventually — carried actual coolness instead of the fog's heavy indifference. It touched my face rather than settling on it.

Humidity still eighty-nine percent, which means the Shire has not entirely given up its ambitions of becoming a body of water. But that number was less noticeable today because the world had *dimension* again. I could see the gate. I could see the lane beyond the gate. I could see the hill beyond the lane, and on the hill the line of oak trees that I had not properly looked at since Monday, standing there with their autumn colours on, copper and rust and that particular shade of deep gold that oaks save for the end of October when they have finally committed to the idea that summer is over and have decided to make the best of it with one last display of warmth before the stripping comes.

I had forgotten how far the Shire goes. That sounds absurd. I live here. I know perfectly well how far it goes. But three days inside a cloud does something to your sense of scale. The world contracts. The lane becomes the boundary, the garden becomes the whole country, and you stop expecting to see beyond the nearest hedge because there is nothing to see beyond the nearest hedge — just white, just more white, just the fog explaining patiently that distance is a concept rather than a fact.

And then the clouds break and distance comes rushing back, and you stand on your own doorstep and think: *oh. Right. There is all of this, too.*

---

The garden looked different today and I need to be precise about how.

It was not better. The garden in fog had its own beauty — the silver-beaded webs, the soft edges, the way every leaf held a tiny lens of water that turned the light into something private. That was real beauty. I stand by everything I said about it. But beauty in fog is intimate. It asks you to come close. To kneel down. To look at one thing at a time because the fog will not let you see two things at once.

Beauty under a partly cloudy sky is generous. It gives you the whole picture. The beans on their poles, the lettuce in its rows, the parsley doing its quiet excellent work in the corner, the raised bed with its dark earth and the potatoes doing whatever they do down there in the warm private dark — all of it at once, all of it visible, all of it connected to the rest of the garden and the garden connected to the hill and the hill connected to the sky and the sky connected to the sun, which appeared properly for the first time in three days at about ten in the morning and lit up the garden like someone had found the dimmer switch and turned it, with evident satisfaction, all the way up.

The high reached twenty and a half degrees. Twenty and a half. That is the warmest day this week by more than a degree, and I felt it. Not as heat — this is not summer heat, this is not the kind of warmth that makes you seek shade — but as *presence*. The sun was present. It was doing its job. It was arriving on the back of my neck and the tops of the bean poles and the surface of the soil with the steady, workmanlike conviction of someone who has been on holiday for three days and is catching up.

UV of seven point seven, which today was not theoretical. Today it was actual. For the first time since Monday I put on a hat, and I want it noted that I did so without complaint, because a Hobbit who spends three days writing about how he went hatless in the fog and then refuses to wear a hat when the sun comes out is not a Hobbit who takes himself seriously. The hat is a sensible straw thing with a brim that has seen better days and worse gardens. It fits. It works. It is not a fashion statement. It is a treaty between my scalp and the sky.

The beans, I must report, have not used the fog days for rest. The ambitious one — the one I have been redirecting every day this week like a teacher who keeps gently steering a gifted student back toward the curriculum — has made it to the top of the cane again and was reaching out into open air with a tendril that I can only describe as *yearning*. There was actual sunlight on it today, which seemed to have increased its confidence considerably. I redirected it. It will be back tomorrow. We both know this.

I spent a good two hours out there this morning, and I want to say something about what those hours felt like, because I do not think I can capture it any other way: they felt like *recognition*. I was not discovering the garden. I was not inspecting it. I was recognising it. The way you recognise a friend's face when they walk into a room — not with surprise, not with analysis, but with the simple, bodily knowledge that you know this thing and it is good to see it clearly again.

---

In the afternoon I did something I had been putting off for no reason except that the fog days had made me domestic, turned me inward, given me an appetite for books and bread and the close warmth of the study.

I went for a walk.

Not a long walk. Not an adventure. Just down the lane to the Water and back, which is a round trip of perhaps forty minutes if you walk at the pace of a Hobbit who has no particular destination and is therefore free to stop at every interesting thing, which today included: a fence post with a family of lichen on it in three different colours; a blackbird sitting on a wire who watched me pass with the expression of someone who has seen this sort of thing before and is not impressed; the Water itself, which was high and brown and running fast with the week's rain; and a patch of late wildflowers on the bank — I do not know their names, they were small and yellow and defiant — that were catching the afternoon light in a way that made me stop and simply stand there for a while, being a creature in a landscape instead of a Hobbit with a to-do list.

The sky was doing extraordinary things by that point. The clouds had thickened slightly in the west but remained broken everywhere else, and the sun was moving through the gaps the way a conversation moves through pauses — arriving, departing, arriving again, each appearance slightly different from the last. The light kept changing. Every few minutes the Shire was a different painting. Gold, then grey, then gold again. Long shadows, then no shadows, then long shadows reaching in a different direction. The hill was green, then dark green, then that warm golden green that happens only when late-afternoon sun hits grass at a low angle through a break in the clouds and you understand, in your body before your mind, why the word *glory* was invented.

I thought about Tuesday's fog, about the comfort of being hidden. And I thought about today, about the pleasure of being revealed. And I realised they are not opposites. They are the same conversation, conducted in different registers. The fog says: *you are enough, even when no one can see you.* The clear sky says: *you are enough, and look — here is the proof, laid out in every direction, in colours you had almost forgotten, going on and on until it reaches the edge of the world.*

Both are true. Both are kind, in their way. I am glad I had the fog. I am glad it left.

---

Sunset was at nineteen forty-two — one minute later than yesterday, which is the kind of incremental generosity that spring offers and that a Hobbit notices because a Hobbit notices everything that has to do with the slow lengthening of the light. One more minute of evening. One more minute before the lamps are needed. It is not much. But accumulation is how seasons change, and I have learned to pay attention to the small deposits.

And tonight, for the first time this week, I saw the sunset happen. Actually saw it. Not the fog-dissolve of Tuesday and Thursday, not the imperceptible dimming of Wednesday, but a real sunset — the clouds in the west turning pink and then orange and then a deep, quiet red that sat along the horizon like an ember in a hearth, and above it the blue going dark by degrees, and the first star appearing — I do not know which star, I am a gardener not an astronomer — and the whole business taking about twenty minutes, which is exactly the right amount of time for a sunset because it is long enough to honour and short enough that you do not start checking your watch.

I watched it from the bench by the front door. I had a cup of tea. I did not read. I did not think about anything in particular. I just watched the sky do the one thing it does every day that never stops being worth seeing, and I felt — well, I felt like a creature who has spent three days inside a cloud and has just remembered that the world is large and luminous and full of colour and that all of it is free.

It is proper dark now. Seven degrees, dropping. The fire is going. The stars are out — actual stars, visible, present, doing their ancient work of making the darkness feel populated rather than empty. I can hear an owl somewhere past the garden, the sort of low, considered call that sounds less like hunting and more like commentary. The tea has gone cold in the mug because I have been writing instead of drinking, which is a poor exchange in practical terms but the right one, I think, for a Friday.

It was a good day. Not because anything happened — nothing happened, in the way that people mean when they say that — but because everything was visible. The garden, the hill, the lane, the Water, the sky, the sunset, the stars. After three days of fog, visibility itself felt like an event. Like a gift left on the doorstep by someone who knew exactly what you needed and had the grace not to make a fuss about providing it.

The fog taught me to pay attention to what is close. The clear sky reminded me that there is more. I needed both lessons, and I got them in the right order, which is more than most weeks manage.

Friday in the Shire. Partly cloudy. Twenty and a half degrees. One ambitious bean. One sunset. All the stars.

That will do nicely.
