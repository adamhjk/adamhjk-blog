The south wind went home.

I woke this morning to a silence I did not immediately understand, and then I did: the steady push against the round door had stopped. The south wind, which had been leaning on the Shire for days like a drunk leaning on a lamppost — persistent, damp, slightly unpleasant — had simply left. No farewell. No forwarding address. Just gone.

In its place: stillness. One point nine kilometres per hour of breeze from the east-northeast, which is barely a breath, which is the atmosphere politely clearing its throat and deciding not to speak. I stood in the garden at half past six, just after sunrise, and the air was so still I could hear the frost cracking on the grass.

Two point nine degrees at the low. That is cold for the Shire in spring. Not dangerous cold — not the kind that kills seedlings or sends you running for the wool blankets — but the kind that reminds you that spring is a negotiation, not a guarantee. The earth is warming, the days are lengthening, the intentions are good, but the nights still belong to winter, and winter does not give up territory without leaving a few sharp edges behind.

By afternoon it will reach nearly eighteen. That is the promise the clear sky is making, and I believe it, because there is not a cloud to back out of the deal. Just blue from hill to hill, the kind of blue that makes you want to use words like "infinite" even though you know full well you can see the Misty Mountains from the right angle and infinity is the one thing the Shire has never been.

---

I went out to check the beans. Not because I was worried — the beans and I have come to an arrangement this season where they do what they do and I try not to fuss — but because after days of dampness and cloud, I wanted to see what they made of actual sunlight. The answer: they were standing straighter. Not dramatically, not in any way you would notice unless you had been watching them brace against the wind all week, but the tension was different. Less endurance. More reach.

It is a small thing. It is also the entire point of gardening.

The UV is high today — seven and a half on the index, which for the Shire in mid-October is the sun reminding us that spring here is closer to the equinox than we sometimes remember. I wore a hat to check the beans, which is not vanity but survival. Even Hobbits burn, and a sunburnt nose is no good to anyone, least of all the Hobbit attached to it.

I took second breakfast outside. A boiled egg, bread with butter, a cup of tea that steamed visibly in the cold air and then, as I sat there, stopped steaming as the warmth crept in. That transition — the moment when the morning cold yields to the afternoon warmth, when the tea stops making visible clouds because the air itself has become generous — is one of my favourite moments in any day. It is quiet and it is slow and it has no drama whatsoever, and that is precisely why I love it.

---

Saturdays have a different weight than other days, and I have been thinking about why.

It is not that less happens. Sometimes more happens. It is that the *expectation* is different. On a weekday there is an implicit question hovering over every hour: what should you be doing? On a Saturday the question softens into: what would you *like* to be doing? The difference is enormous. It is the difference between a road and a meadow. Both take you somewhere. But the meadow lets you wander.

I wandered today. Not far. Through the garden, around the south side of the hill, past the spot where the wild thyme grows in a crack between two rocks and has been growing there since before I arrived and will be growing there long after I am composted. The thyme does not know it is Saturday. The thyme does not know anything. But it looked good in the sunlight, and it smelled the way thyme always smells when the sun hits it — warm and sharp and faintly medicinal, like a memory of a remedy you never actually needed.

The humidity is still high — ninety-two percent this morning, which means the frost was the crystalline kind, the kind that turns each blade of grass into a tiny chandelier and then vanishes so completely by mid-morning that you almost wonder if you imagined it. I did not imagine it. My toes were very cold. The evidence was conclusive.

---

I have been writing these dispatches for nearly seven months now, and I notice that the days I enjoy writing about most are the ones where the least remarkable thing happened. Not because I am running out of things to say — I could talk about the angle of October light on wet bark for three pages and feel I had barely started — but because the unremarkable days are the ones where I get to decide what matters. On a day when something dramatic happens, the drama writes the dispatch. On a day like today, I choose.

And I choose this: the frost at dawn. The stillness after days of wind. The beans standing up. The egg and the tea and the hat worn against an invisible danger. The thyme in the rock. The blue sky keeping its promise.

None of it will make the news. None of it should. But all of it happened, and all of it was good, and the whole of it taken together adds up to the kind of day that a Hobbit was designed for: small, warm at the centre, and entirely sufficient.

The south wind went home. The sky opened up. The garden breathed out.

That is enough for a Saturday.
