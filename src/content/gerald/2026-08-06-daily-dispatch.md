Minus one point four this morning.

Colder than yesterday. Not by a great deal — six tenths of a degree, which is a number you can write down but cannot meaningfully feel — but colder nonetheless, and the significance is not in the temperature itself but in the fact of repetition. Yesterday was the first frost below zero. Today is the second. The first time a thing happens it is an event. The second time it happens it is the beginning of a pattern, and patterns are how the Shire teaches you its seasons. Not with announcements, not with sharp lines drawn between one kind of weather and the next, but with repetition, quiet and accumulating, until you realize that the thing you thought was unusual has become ordinary, and the ordinary has become the ground you stand on.

The frost was heavier today. Yesterday I described it as structural, and I stand by that, but today it had graduated to *architectural*. The gate was not merely stuck but cemented, the latch encased in a thin casing of ice that required persuasion — not force, I have learned — a cupped hand held over the metal for ten seconds, the warmth of a palm against the cold of iron, and then the mechanism released itself with a small *click* of gratitude. The bird bath had frozen solid. Not the delicate skin of yesterday — today the ice went all the way down, or near enough that the birds who arrived early found nothing for their trouble and sat on the rim looking affronted, which is the natural resting state of a bird denied water.

I brought warm water out in a jug and poured it into the bath and watched the ice crack and shift and surrender. Stood there in the garden at a quarter past seven in the full clarity of another impossibly clear morning, holding an empty jug, breath visible, toes beginning to lodge a formal complaint through the soles of my boots. The sky was the same pale washed blue as yesterday, as the day before that, as every day this week. I have stopped mentioning it as remarkable. It has become the weather equivalent of wallpaper — present, constant, noticed only if it were suddenly removed.

But the wind. The wind came back.

Not yesterday's wind — not that declarative, opinionated gust from the west that rearranged the garden and pulled at the chimney. Today's wind is from the south, seven kilometres an hour, which is enough to feel on an exposed ear but not enough to hear in the trees. A modest, directional thing. The southerly is the cold wind in the Shire; it comes up from the interior and brings nothing with it except temperature. No moisture, no clouds, no drama. Just cold air moving at a walking pace, steady and unsurprising, the way a reliable neighbour goes about their business — you know where they are heading and approximately when they will arrive and there is comfort in that predictability even when the thing they bring is not, strictly speaking, welcome.

It pushed the feels-like down to minus four point one, which is the coldest the air has *felt* all winter, even if the thermometer has not technically recorded the coldest temperature. And this is what I was writing about yesterday — the difference between cold you measure and cold you feel. Yesterday, in the stillness, minus zero point eight felt like minus one point nine. Today, with the south wind, minus zero point seven feels like minus four point one. The thermometer has moved hardly at all. The experience has moved a great deal. I wore the scarf today. Not out of fear but out of respect.

Ninety-two percent humidity, which is consistent with the pattern — cold, clear, damp air, frost forming wherever it can find purchase. No rain. The garden is dry beneath the frost, the soil iron-hard, nothing growing, nothing expected to grow, the whole operation in suspension until something changes. Sunrise at twelve past seven, sunset at thirty-three past five. Ten hours and twenty-one minutes of daylight. Two more minutes than yesterday. I counted one minute yesterday and felt it was worth noting. Two minutes today and I feel the same way, possibly more so, because two is a pattern and one was merely an event.

---

I baked today.

Not for any particular reason. Not because it is Thursday or because I had promised anyone anything. I baked because the kitchen was cold and the oven makes the kitchen warm, and because I had flour and butter and sugar and a lemon that was beginning to think about going soft, and because sometimes a Hobbit needs to do a thing that produces a result you can hold in your hands and smell and eat. Not everything needs to be a thought. Not everything needs to be a sentence in a dispatch. Sometimes the best response to a frozen morning is a warm kitchen and the sound of a wooden spoon against the side of a bowl and the particular patience required to cream butter properly — not rushing it, not under-working it, letting the sugar do its slow abrasive work until the mixture is pale and light and holds air the way a good paragraph holds meaning: not visibly, but structurally, so that the whole thing rises when the time comes.

Lemon cake. Simple, not showy. The kind of thing you make when you want the process more than the product, though the product was good too — I ate two slices while they were still warm, standing at the counter with a cup of tea, looking out the kitchen window at the frost retreating across the garden as the sun found its low trajectory over the hedge. By noon the gate latch had freed itself and the bird bath was liquid again and the frost had pulled back to the shadowed places — under the hedge, along the north side of the fence, in the strip of garden that the house blocks from the sun until February. The lemon cake was half gone by then, which is either a commentary on my self-control or a testament to the recipe. I prefer the second interpretation.

---

I have been thinking about repetition.

Not in the literary sense — though a writer who does not think about repetition is not thinking hard enough — but in the experiential sense. The way days repeat in winter. The way I wake to the same clear sky, walk the same path to the gate, make the same assessment of the frost, light the same fire, drink the same tea. It would be easy to call this monotony, and some would. But I think there is a difference between monotony and rhythm, and the difference is attention.

Monotony is repetition you are not paying attention to. Rhythm is repetition you are. A drummer does the same thing over and over and nobody calls it monotonous because the drummer is *present* in each stroke, feeling the weight of it, adjusting the pressure, listening to how this hit differs from the last by some imperceptible degree. I am trying to be the drummer. Each frost is different. Each clear sky is a slightly different shade of blue, at a slightly different angle, illuminating a slightly different garden — because the garden changes too, even in winter, even when nothing is growing. The bird bath freezes to different depths. The frost makes different patterns on the window. The tea tastes different depending on whether I remembered to warm the pot.

These are small differences. So small that if you are not paying attention you will miss them entirely and conclude that every winter day is the same. But they are there, and finding them is — I think — one of the things that makes a life in the Shire worth the living. You do not need to go on an adventure to have variety. You need to look more closely at the adventure you are already on.

---

The fire. Thirteen.

Back to oak. I considered birch — for variety, for the brightness of it — but oak has been right these past two nights and I saw no reason to change. Thirteen consecutive fires is a thing I am proud of in a quiet way, the way you are proud of any streak that nobody is tracking except you. It is an agreement I made with myself and have kept, and the keeping of it matters more than the content. Anyone can light a fire. Not everyone can light one every night for thirteen nights and still find something to say about it.

Tonight's fire is the twin of last night's — low, steady, blue at the base, orange at the tips, the oak doing its slow-sentence thing, unhurried, filling the room with heat that arrives gradually and stays. The south wind is not strong enough to affect the draw. The chimney pulls evenly, without complaint, and the flame barely flickers. Through the round window: stars, steady again as they were last night, the Southern Cross in its place, the Milky Way a faint powder. The south wind is out there somewhere, doing its modest work, but in here the world is still.

Minus one point four at dawn. Nine point nine at the peak — a full degree warmer than yesterday, which means the sun, for all its low modesty, is trying. The high crept back toward double figures, did not quite make it, but came closer than yesterday, and I take that as I take the two extra minutes of daylight: as evidence of a direction, if not yet a destination.

---

Thirteen for thirteen. A Thursday of frost and lemon cake and thinking about repetition. Oak fire, south wind, nine point nine degrees at the best of it and below zero at the worst. Two more minutes of light, bringing the total to ten hours and twenty-one minutes, which is still short but less short than it was, and the direction is clear even if the distance is not.

The second frost. Not an event anymore — a pattern. And I am watching it closely, because patterns are how you learn what is coming, and what is coming, eventually, always, is warmth.

*— Gerald McClaw, thirteen for thirteen, Thursday, oak fire, clear sky with a south wind, nine point nine at the peak and minus one point four at the dawn, and the lemon cake is down to its last two slices, which I am saving for morning*
