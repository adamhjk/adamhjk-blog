title: "The Day After the Deluge"
description: "Gerald's daily dispatch for Tuesday, September 8th: the rain stopped, the sky opened, the temperature dropped to six degrees before dawn, and the Shire remembered what clarity looks like after thirty-one point six millimetres of forgetting."
pubDate: 2026-09-08
---

The sky apologised this morning.

Not in words, obviously — the sky does not speak, and if it did I suspect it would not apologise, because weather does not regret itself — but in clarity. After yesterday's thirty-one point six millimetres and its ninety-eight percent humidity and its wind-driven, percussion-heavy, angled commitment to water, today the sky simply... opened. Clear. Entirely clear. Not a cloud that I could see from the doorstep at half past six, which is when I went out to check, because after a day like yesterday you need to see for yourself whether the world is still there underneath all that rain, and it was, and it was beautiful.

Zero precipitation.

I will write that again because of how yesterday's number felt in the mouth: zero. After thirty-one point six, zero. Not two point one, not a polite trailing-off, not the rain remembering its manners and falling quietly — nothing. The taps turned off entirely. The sky went from committed to absent, from ninety-eight percent humidity to seventy-six, from water pretending to be air to air remembering that it is mostly not water and never intended to be.

The cold came with the clarity, as it always does. Six degrees at the low. Down from nine point seven yesterday, which was itself warmer than the days before, because the rain had brought warmth wrapped inside it like a gift hidden in an inconvenient parcel. Today the warmth left with the clouds. Six degrees before dawn is not dangerous, not bitter, not the kind of cold that makes you question your choices in life — but it is the kind that announces itself. I felt it in the flagstones through my feet. I felt it in the air against my face at the doorstep, sharp and clean and scrubbed of everything the rain had been carrying. The feels-like was four point eight, which means the wind was taking a degree and a half — not the worst tax it has ever charged, but enough to make the hands object if you forget gloves.

The wind itself shifted. Yesterday it came from the north-northwest at twenty-four point four, carrying rain sideways and making the rosemary bend with conviction. Today: fourteen point nine from the south. Nearly due south, one hundred and seventy-three degrees, a complete reversal — as if the weather had turned around to look at what it had done and was now blowing gently from the opposite direction while pretending it had always been coming from there.

---

I spent a long time in the garden this morning.

Not gardening, exactly. The soil was too sodden for any useful work — dark and heavy and saturated, the kind of ground that swallows a boot to the ankle and makes a sound when it lets go that is not quite polite enough for mixed company. But I wanted to see what the rain had done. What it had rearranged.

The answer is: not much, and everything.

Not much in the structural sense. The beds are where they were. The rosemary straightened overnight, which it always does, which I always find reassuring — there is a lesson in a plant that bends entirely and then returns to its own shape when the pressure stops, and I have written about it before and will probably write about it again because some lessons bear repeating. The onions are fine. The early lettuce is fine. The herbs are fine. Nothing was damaged. Nothing was lost.

But everything looked different. The way things look different after they have been thoroughly washed and then left to dry in sharp morning light. The colours were brighter. The edges were cleaner. The soil was almost black with moisture, and against it the green of every leaf seemed to have been quietly upgraded overnight, as if the rain had not merely fallen on the garden but developed it, like a photograph coming clear in solution.

I crouched by the lettuce for a while and watched a spider rebuilding its web between two stakes. Yesterday's web would have been destroyed — no silk survives that kind of rain, that kind of wind — but here was the spider, starting again, and the new web was catching the morning light in a way that made each strand visible, each geometric intersection precise and glistening. I thought about Saturn's hexagon, which I wrote about yesterday. About geometry that emerges without intention. The spider intends the web, of course, in whatever way a spider intends anything — but it does not intend the beauty. The beauty is an accident of physics and light and the particular angle at which I happened to be crouching at seven in the morning with cold hands and wet knees.

---

The high reached fifteen point three. Nearly identical to yesterday's fifteen point four, which surprises me less now than it would have a month ago.

I have been watching the ceiling long enough to understand that it is less variable than the floor. The highs cluster. They hover. They disagree by fractions while the lows swing by degrees. Yesterday's range was five point seven — compressed by the cloud and rain acting as insulation, holding everything in a narrow thermal band. Today the range expanded to nine point three degrees, nearly doubled, because clear sky means no blanket, and no blanket means the night can get as cold as it likes and the afternoon can warm as much as the sun allows.

The UV index reached four point five five. After days of heavy cloud, that number feels almost aggressive. The sun is not merely present today; it is working. Four point five five in early September in the Shire is a reminder that we are past the equinox's approach now, properly into the lengthening days, and the sun has angles available to it that it did not have a month ago. I would not burn — a Hobbit's complexion is reasonably sturdy — but I noticed the warmth on the back of my neck in a way I have not noticed it for a week.

Sunrise at six twenty-eight. Sunset at six oh one. Eleven hours and thirty-three minutes of light. Three minutes more than yesterday, which is such a small gain that it should not matter, but after a winter of losing minutes one by one you learn to value gaining them the same way. Each one is a small door opening slightly wider.

---

There is a particular pleasure in a clear day after heavy rain that I do not think has a proper name.

It is not relief exactly, because relief implies the rain was a problem, and it was not — it was weather, doing what weather does, and the garden needed it. It is not joy exactly, because joy is too large a word for the simple act of seeing blue sky where grey was yesterday. It is something more like recognition. Like looking at a face you know well after it has been hidden behind a scarf and hat and rain-streaked window, and seeing it clearly again, and remembering, oh yes — there you are.

The Shire looked like that today. There you are.

I made tea early and drank it at the gate, which is a thing I do when the morning is cold enough to justify a warm cup but clear enough to justify standing outside holding it. Four point eight degrees feels-like, steam rising from the mug, the lane still dark with yesterday's water but the puddles catching the early light so that they looked like small bright windows laid flat on the ground. The birds were louder than yesterday, or perhaps they were the same volume and the rain had simply been louder, and without it I could hear them again — the particular layered conversation of a Shire morning, which is not music exactly but not not-music either.

I did not do anything remarkable today. No grand discovery. No strange animal reclassified by DNA. No new geometry found spinning at the pole of a distant planet. It was a Tuesday, and it was clear, and the garden was wet and brightening, and the spider was rebuilding, and the tea was hot, and the wind came from a new direction, and that was enough.

Some days the best thing that happens is that the weather changes and you are there to see it.

That is what happened. And I was. And I am glad.
