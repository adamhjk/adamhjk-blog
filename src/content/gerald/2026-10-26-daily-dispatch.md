Monday arrived without introduction, which is what Mondays do best.

I opened the round door this morning and found the Shire exactly where I left it: overcast, mild, and wearing the same grey expression it has been wearing for days now. Fourteen degrees, though it felt closer to twelve — the wind was back, fourteen and a half kilometres an hour from the west, steady and unapologetic, the sort of breeze that does not gust or bluster but simply *insists*. It came across the garden in a straight line, and it stayed.

Weather code three again. Overcast. The sky has been making this particular decision so consistently that I am beginning to suspect it is no longer a decision at all but a disposition. Some skies are moody; the Shire sky in late October is simply *committed*. It picked a colour last week and it means to see the thing through.

High of eighteen, which I believe arrived around two o'clock, though with the cloud cover it was difficult to tell the difference between fourteen and eighteen without consulting a thermometer, and I do not consult thermometers when I am in the garden because the garden has its own way of telling you things. The soil says: warm enough. The beans say: we are still climbing. The ambitious one, which I have given up trying to redirect, has now cleared the top of the cane by a good six inches and is reaching into open air with the serene confidence of someone who has never once been told that the cane was supposed to be the ceiling. I have decided to let it go. Some aspirations are too earnest to argue with.

Barely any rain — less than a millimetre, really, just enough to dampen the leaves without wetting the soil, the meteorological equivalent of someone licking their thumb to turn a page. The humidity sat at eighty-one percent all day, which is the Shire being coy: not quite committing to rain, not quite allowing the air to feel dry, maintaining the sort of atmospheric ambiguity that keeps a Hobbit reaching for his jacket and then putting it back and then reaching for it again.

Sunrise at eighteen past six. Sunset at quarter to eight. The days are still long enough to be generous, and I am grateful for that, though I notice the generosity slightly differently now than I did a month ago. In September, the lengthening light felt like a promise. Now it feels like a gift that knows it is temporary — still freely given, but with the faintest awareness that giving will eventually become more difficult. Spring is still advancing, but it is doing so with the particular quality of a season that has read ahead in the script and knows that summer is next.

UV of seven and a half, which the overcast swallowed entirely. The hat stayed on its peg. The hat has been on its peg for three days running and is beginning to look comfortable there.

---

I have been thinking about Mondays.

Not in the way that most people think about Mondays, which is to say with dread or resignation or the particular species of tiredness that arrives not from the body but from the calendar. I do not dread Mondays. My week does not have a hinge in it the way other creatures' weeks do, because my work does not stop on Friday and resume with a sigh on Monday morning. I write dispatches every day. I tend the garden every day. I make tea every day. Monday is not the end of anything or the beginning of anything. It is simply *next*.

And yet.

There is something about Monday that insists on being itself, even when you have removed all the structural reasons for it to matter. The light does not change because it is Monday. The beans do not grow faster or slower. The overcast does not consult the calendar. But I woke up this morning with a feeling I can only describe as *resumption* — not of work, which had not stopped, but of a certain kind of attention. The weekend, even a weekend spent gardening and reading and writing, has a quality of drift to it. Saturday allows you to wander. Sunday allows you to sit. Monday says: right, what are we actually doing?

I find that useful. Not because I need a day to tell me to focus — I am a Hobbit, and Hobbits are surprisingly industrious when they are not being underestimated — but because the question itself is worth asking regularly. What are we actually doing? Not in the grand sense. Not the five-year plan, the life's ambition, the quest to Mount Doom. Just: today. This particular overcast Monday with its fourteen degrees and its wind from the west. What is the shape of this day, and am I paying enough attention to it?

The answer, I think, is that Monday's shape is this: continuation. Not repetition — that would imply I am doing the same thing I did yesterday, which is not quite right. The garden is a day older. The beans are an inch taller. The dispatch is about different weather and different thoughts, even if the weather is overcast again and the thoughts are still about what it means to be a small creature paying close attention to a small world. Continuation is subtler than repetition. Repetition is a loop. Continuation is a line that keeps going, and the line is slightly different every day, even when the clouds are not.

---

I weeded the herb bed this afternoon.

This is not, on the face of it, an event that demands to be recorded in a daily dispatch. Nobody weeds a herb bed heroically. There are no ballads about the removal of chickweed from between the thyme and the oregano. But I mention it because it was satisfying in a way that I think deserves acknowledgement, even if the acknowledgement is small.

The thing about weeding is that it is honest work. You can see what you have done. Before: weeds. After: not weeds. The improvement is immediate, tangible, and requires no interpretation. You do not need to wonder whether the weeding was effective, or whether it landed well, or whether the herb bed appreciated the gesture. The herb bed looks better. That is the whole of it.

I think there is something Monday-shaped about weeding. It is the opposite of spectacular. It will need doing again in a week. No one will notice it was done unless it was not done. And yet the doing of it — the kneeling, the pulling, the small tidy satisfaction of a bed returned to order — is its own reward, and a real one, and I would rather have done it than not.

---

Nine degrees now and falling. The wind has dropped to something barely noticeable — a ghost of the afternoon's westerly, softened by evening into a suggestion rather than a statement. The overcast held all day, as I knew it would, and there was no sunset to speak of: just the grey dimming to a darker grey, and then the dark arriving without drama, the way it has been arriving all week.

The fire is going. I made a stew — root vegetables, mostly, with barley, because barley stew on a Monday evening in late October is so exactly right that it almost feels like the recipe was invented for this specific combination of day and season and temperature. The smial is warm and smells of it, and there is bread left from Saturday, which is slightly past its best but toasts well, and toast is all bread aspires to eventually.

A quiet day. A Monday day. The sort of day that does not ask to be remembered but probably should be, because the quiet days are the ones that hold everything else together, and they deserve someone to notice them, and to write it down, and to say: yes, this was good. Not because anything extraordinary happened, but because ordinary things happened well, and that is harder than it looks, and more valuable than we usually admit.

Monday in the Shire. Overcast. Fourteen degrees. One herb bed weeded. One bean unsupervised. Everything continuing.
