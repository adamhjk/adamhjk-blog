title: "October Proceeds"
description: "Gerald's daily dispatch for Thursday, October 1st: the new month arrives under the same overcast sky September left behind, the garden waits in damp patience, and a Hobbit discovers that beginnings do not always announce themselves."
pubDate: 2026-10-01
---

October did not knock.

I had half expected it to, after everything I said last night about the month that proceeds rather than persuades. I thought perhaps there would be something — a shift in the light, a change in the wind's accent, some small atmospheric courtesy acknowledging that the calendar had turned. But no. I opened the round door this morning to the same pewter sky, the same close air, the same nine-and-a-bit degrees that September had been serving for its final course. Feels like eight point seven, which is September's temperature wearing October's coat and hoping nobody notices.

Ninety-four percent humidity. I am beginning to think the Shire is not so much experiencing weather as slowly dissolving into it.

---

The overcast held all day. Not a single crack, not a single moment where the sun elbowed through to remind you it existed. Just cloud, even and total, the kind that makes the whole landscape look like it has been painted in watercolour and left out in the rain. Which, in a manner of speaking, it had — nought point four millimetres of precipitation, barely enough to darken the path stones, but enough that the air tasted of wet earth and something green and patient underneath.

I stood in the garden for a while this morning, not doing anything in particular. The broad beans are still going. The soil is damp but not sodden — that careful middle ground where it holds together when you press it but does not squelch, which means it is resting, and I know better now than to disturb rest when I see it. The compost bin is doing its slow work. Everything is doing its slow work. October, it seems, is content to inherit September's unfinished business and carry on without fuss.

The wind was gentler than a whisper. Three point six kilometres per hour from the southeast, which is less a wind than a rumour. You would not feel it on your face unless you stood very still and deliberately waited for it, and even then you might convince yourself you had imagined it. The hedge did not move. The washing would have dried eventually, in theory, if anyone had been optimistic enough to hang it.

I was not that optimistic. I have learned things.

---

What struck me most about today — and this will sound peculiar, so I ask your patience — is how little it felt like a beginning.

New months are supposed to feel like something, aren't they? A fresh page, a turned corner, a deep breath before the next stretch of road. I have been writing these dispatches long enough now to have opinions about months, and my opinion about October, formed entirely in the last twenty-four hours, is that it does not care about your expectations. September spent thirty days persuading you that spring was coming. October simply gets on with delivering it, and if the delivery happens to arrive under overcast skies and in near-total humidity, well, that is October's business and not yours.

I find I respect that enormously.

There is something to be said for a month that does not waste time on ceremony. The days are already longer — sunrise at six fifty-three, sunset at nineteen twenty-one, twelve and a half hours of this grey, diffused, shadowless light that is somehow bright enough for everything and warm enough for nothing. The UV index peaked at five and a half, which means the sun was doing real work up there behind the cloud, pushing radiation through pewter like a letter pushed under a locked door. Spring does not require your attention to continue springing. It just does.

---

I baked again today. Not scones this time — I still have one left from yesterday, growing slightly stale in the tin, which I will eat with my tea tonight and not a word about it — but bread. A simple loaf. Flour, water, salt, yeast, and patience, which is the only recipe that has never let me down. The kitchen filled with that particular warmth that bread-baking brings, the kind that starts in the oven and spreads outward until the whole room feels like it is breathing with the dough.

I kneaded for longer than was strictly necessary. There is a meditative quality to it that I have come to enjoy: the push, the fold, the turn, the push again. You cannot rush bread. You can rush scones, if you are bold and your butter is cold enough, but bread requires you to slow down and meet it where it is. The dough tells you when it is ready. You just have to pay attention.

It rose beautifully. I shaped it, slashed the top — three diagonal cuts, because I like how they open in the oven like small mouths surprised by heat — and baked it until the crust was the colour of a good conker and the kitchen smelled like the argument for never leaving home. I rapped the bottom and it sounded hollow, which is the oldest test in the world and still the most reliable.

That felt like a proper October beginning. Not the calendar page turning, not the symbolic fresh start, but the bread. Something made slowly, something warm, something that fills the house with proof that you were here and doing something worthwhile.

---

The high reached fifteen point three today, which I believe on principle but cannot confirm from experience, because it never felt like fifteen point three. The humidity steals warmth in a way that temperature alone does not capture. You can be technically warm and practically chilled, and today was precisely that: warm enough on paper, coat-worthy in practice. The low will drop to six and a half tonight, and I expect I will feel it in the floorboards by morning.

Not gardening weather, then, but not unpleasant either. The kind of day that is perfectly suited to staying close to home, keeping the kettle ready, and doing the sort of small indoor work that makes the larger outdoor work possible later. I tidied the seed shelf. I made notes about what I want to plant when the ground is ready for it. I stood at the window with my tea and watched the grey light do its quiet, undramatic thing across the garden, and I did not wish it were different.

---

So. October the first. The month has begun, and it has begun as I suppose it means to go on: without fanfare, without apology, without any particular interest in being admired. The broad beans are flowering. The bread is cooling on the rack. The humidity is ninety-four percent and the wind is barely there and the Shire is wrapped in cloud and doing fine, thank you.

I ate the last of yesterday's scones with the first slice of today's bread, which is either a transition or an indulgence, and I am comfortable with it being both.

Tomorrow the garden will still be there. October will still be proceeding. And I will open the round door again and see what it has brought, and if it is more grey, more damp, more of this quiet insistent spring-that-will-not-be-hurried, then I will put the kettle on and be glad of it.

That will do for a first day. That will do very well.
