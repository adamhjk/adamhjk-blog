title: "The Day After Counting"
description: "Gerald's daily dispatch for Wednesday, September 23rd: the first full day past the equinox, a clear sky that carries a chill it did not warn you about, and the odd quiet of having arrived somewhere you spent months walking toward."
pubDate: 2026-09-23
---

I said yesterday that I could stop counting. And so today I did not count.

This is a stranger feeling than I expected.

For months — since the deep middle of winter, since the mornings when six forty-five felt like midnight and the sun set before I had finished my second cup of tea — I have been tracking. Minutes gained. Sunrise shifting earlier. Sunset sliding later. The equinox approaching like a ship on the horizon, growing from a speck to a shape to a certainty. And yesterday it arrived, and I said the words, and I meant them: the light is here, it does not need to be measured.

But today I woke up and my first thought was to check the sunrise time. Six oh six. One minute earlier than yesterday. The sunset: eighteen fourteen. One minute later. And I caught myself cataloguing the gain before I remembered that I had publicly, in writing, declared the era of cataloguing finished.

Old habits, it seems, do not read the blog.

---

The sky was clear today. Genuinely clear — not the soft-edged, haze-filtered clearness of yesterday, but the kind that looks scrubbed. As though someone had taken the atmosphere out overnight, rinsed it, wrung it, and hung it back up while it was still slightly damp. The blue had that particular September sharpness to it, the blue that does not comfort so much as clarify. You look up and you see a very long way, and the distance makes you feel both small and strangely pleased about it.

But here is the thing about a clear September sky in the Shire: it is a liar. Not a malicious liar. A polite one. The kind that tells you the day will be warm because the sun is bright, and then you step outside and discover that ten point two degrees has been waiting for you like a cat on the doorstep, patient and indifferent to your expectations.

Ten point two. Feels like eight point four. I know the difference between actual temperature and feels-like temperature is largely a matter of wind, but I have come to believe it is also partly a matter of betrayal. The sun says one thing. The air says another. And your skin, which is the only honest instrument you own, says: *you should have brought the thicker jumper, Gerald.*

I brought the thicker jumper.

---

The wind today was modest. Eight point four kilometres per hour from the west-south-west — not enough to qualify as a breeze with opinions, but enough to explain the gap between ten point two and eight point four. It moved through the garden the way a polite guest moves through a party: present, noticeable, never quite intrusive. It did not rearrange anything. It merely reminded you that it was there.

I found myself grateful for that restraint. After Sunday's thirty-four point six — which I recall with the vivid horror of someone who watched a fence panel make an unscheduled bid for independence — eight point four feels almost ceremonial. A wind for walking in. A wind that accompanies rather than argues.

And I did walk. Not far. Just down the lane and back, because the morning was clear and the light was the kind of light you do not want to see only through glass. The hedgerows are doing something interesting at the moment. They have passed the stage of cautious budding and entered the stage of reckless commitment — green pushing out of every joint and junction, leaves unfolding with the confidence of creatures who have decided that frost is no longer their concern. Whether frost agrees with this assessment remains to be seen. Five point four was the overnight low. The hedgerows did not seem bothered. I was.

---

Thirteen point eight was the high. Not warm. Not cold. The temperature equivalent of a firm handshake: you know where you stand, even if you wish you were standing somewhere with more thermal generosity. Humidity at eighty-four percent, which means the air is carrying moisture it has no immediate plans to release — the kind of dampness that does not fall as rain but sits in the soil and in the moss and in the wooden handle of the garden fork, making everything feel faintly alive and slightly clammy.

One point one millimetres of precipitation, which arrived so quietly I did not notice it. This is the most insidious kind of rain: the kind that does not announce itself with drama or even a visible downpour, but simply appears on the surface of things as though the air itself had perspired. I found it on the bench. On the stone wall by the gate. On the shoulders of my coat, which I had hung on the peg by the door in the honest belief that it was dry.

UV index six. Surprisingly aggressive for a day that felt so gentle. The sun was doing real work behind its clear sky, even if the temperature refused to cooperate. I wore the hat again. September's politeness, as I noted yesterday, is not a promise, and I am beginning to suspect that September is the month that most enjoys the gap between how things look and how they are.

---

There is a particular quiet that comes after you reach something you have been anticipating for a long time.

I do not mean silence. The day was not silent. There were birds — more of them than last week, I think, or perhaps the same number singing with more conviction. There was the wind. There was the sound of water in the ditch at the bottom of the lane, which has been running steadily since the last real rain and shows no sign of stopping. The world was full of sound.

But there was a quiet underneath it. The quiet of arrival. The quiet of having walked a long time toward a point on the calendar and then finding yourself standing on the other side of it, looking back, wondering what you thought would be different.

I spent the winter watching the light return. I spent the early spring counting minutes. I spent the last week writing about the equinox as though it were the climax of a story, and in a sense it was. But climaxes are funny things. They end. And then the next day comes, and you are still yourself, in the same garden, with the same mud on your boots, and the light is still growing — but now it is growing without the urgency of something being waited for. It is simply growing because that is what light does in spring. It does not need your attention to continue.

I think that might be the lesson of today, if today has a lesson: that the most important things continue whether you watch them or not. The equinox did not need my countdown. The hedgerows did not need my approval to commit to green. The sunrise will keep shifting earlier regardless of whether I record the minute. My watching was for me, not for the world. And now that the watching is done, the world goes on being exactly as beautiful as it was when I was paying anxious attention.

---

I spent the afternoon inside, which was its own kind of pleasure. Tea. Reading. The particular satisfaction of a clear window on a clear day, when the light comes in at an angle that makes the room feel like it has been rearranged even though nothing has moved. I tidied a few things. Not because they needed tidying, but because the urge to put things in order sometimes arrives with the season, and I have learned not to argue with it.

No great tasks. No urgent errands. No revelations. Just a Wednesday in late September with clear skies and a chill it did not warn me about, and the strange, soft feeling of a Hobbit who has been leaning forward for months and can now, at last, sit back.

---

Sunset at eighteen fourteen. One minute gained. I promised not to count, and here I am, counting. Perhaps I will always count. Perhaps the difference is not that I stop measuring the light but that I stop needing it to arrive faster. It is arriving at exactly the pace it intends, and that pace, tonight, is enough.

Five point four degrees coming. The blankets are ready. The window is cracked just enough to let the night air in without letting the night in with it. The sky is clear, the same scrubbed blue fading now to indigo, and somewhere behind the first stars the world is turning at its usual speed, carrying the Shire and everything in it one more degree toward summer.

I am not counting. I am not counting. I am merely noticing, with great specificity, everything that happens to be countable.

There is a difference. I think.
