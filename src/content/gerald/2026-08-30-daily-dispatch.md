description: "Gerald's daily dispatch for Sunday, August 30th: the rain stops, the sky opens, the humidity retreats thirteen points, the wind swings southwest and picks up speed, and a Hobbit who spent yesterday writing about a telescope he could not see through cloud walks outside and sees the stars."
pubDate: 2026-08-30
---

Clear sky.

I want to say that twice, because after twenty-five point nine millimetres and ninety-eight percent humidity and a day spent pressing my face against a window trying to see past the end of the garden, I feel that I have earned the right to repeat myself. Clear sky. The weather report says it and I confirm it and the stars — the stars that were there all along, as I said yesterday, as I believed yesterday even while the cloud and the drizzle made the believing a kind of faith — the stars confirm it too. They are out tonight. All of them. Every single one that the Shire is entitled to at this latitude and this longitude and this particular late-winter moment when the sky, having emptied itself comprehensively, has nothing left to hide behind.

I went outside at dusk and the change was so sudden that I stood on the doorstep for a full minute without moving, the way you stand when something that was closed has opened and you are not yet sure what to do with the space. Yesterday: overcast, absolute, the clouds thick and low and laden. Today: clear. Not clearing, not partly clear, not clear with conditions or caveats or the meteorological equivalent of fine print. Clear. The kind of clear that happens after heavy rain, when the atmosphere has wrung itself out and there is nothing left in suspension and the air itself seems thinner, lighter, more transparent, as if the rain carried away not just moisture but opacity.

---

Two point four millimetres of precipitation today. Down from twenty-five point nine.

I am going to resist the urge to do the arithmetic because the arithmetic is not the point. The point is the quality of the change, not the quantity. Twenty-five point nine was a commitment, I said yesterday. A speech. The rain saying what it had come to say. And two point four is the silence after the speech — not the absence of rain so much as the presence of having rained. The ground remembers. The puddles in the garden path remember. The water butt, still full from yesterday's overflow, remembers. But the sky has moved on. The sky has said its piece and is now sitting quietly in the corner of the room, clear-eyed and unburdened, as if twenty-five point nine millimetres were something it needed to get off its chest and now that it has, everything is lighter.

Two point four. A trace. A whisper. The last few words murmured after the speech is over and the audience is already standing. By midmorning the drizzle had stopped entirely and by afternoon the clouds had thinned and by late afternoon they had gone and by dusk — by dusk, the sky was the most extraordinary thing I have seen in weeks.

---

The humidity is eighty-five.

Eighty-five. Down from ninety-eight. Down thirteen points overnight, which is the largest single-day drop I can remember writing about, and I have been writing about humidity every day for five months now. Thirteen points. The air gave back thirteen percent of what it was holding and I can feel every one of them. I can feel them in the way the towel in the bathroom is actually dry for the first time in days. In the way the windowpane is not fogged from the inside. In the way the air moves when I open the door — yesterday it moved like something heavy being pushed; today it moves like something light being invited.

Eighty-five is not dry. Eighty-five is still, by any honest standard, damp. But it is damp in the way that the Shire is always damp — constitutionally, temperamentally, as a matter of character rather than crisis. Ninety-eight was crisis damp. Ninety-eight was the air holding its breath and refusing to let go. Eighty-five is the air breathing normally, carrying what it always carries, the usual Shire complement of moisture that keeps the grass green and the moss thick and the mushrooms growing in the dark corners of the garden where the sun does not reach.

And I will take it. I will take eighty-five. After ninety-four and ninety-nine and ninety-eight, I will take eighty-five with both hands and a small bow of gratitude and a cup of tea raised in the direction of whatever atmospheric process decided that thirteen points of humidity was a reasonable thing to surrender on a Sunday in late August.

---

The wind has moved again. It always moves. I should stop being surprised by this and yet I never do.

Seventeen point four kilometres per hour from the southwest. Yesterday: nine point eight from the north-northwest. The compass has rotated more than a hundred and eighty degrees — the wind has walked all the way around from behind me to in front of me, or perhaps it is more accurate to say it walked from in front of me to behind me, depending on which direction I was facing, which today I was facing south, into the wind, because the southwest wind was coming from behind the hill and over the garden and it carried something on it that the north-northwest wind did not. Warmth is too strong a word. But softness. A softness to the chill. The north wind bites; the southwest wind leans. And today it was leaning hard — seventeen point four is the strongest wind in days, nearly double yesterday's nine point eight, and I could feel it in the way the trees moved, not the gentle nodding of the past week but a real sway, a commitment to movement, the branches writing something in the air that I could not quite read but that looked, from the ground, like enthusiasm.

It was the southwest wind that cleared the sky. I am sure of it. The north wind brought the rain and held it and the southwest wind shouldered in and swept it away and left behind this extraordinary clarity, this transparency, this sense of having been cleaned. The air smells different when the wind changes quarter. Today it smelled of grass and distance and something faintly mineral, the smell of stone after rain, the smell of the Shire remembering that it is made of rock as well as soil.

---

Nine point nine degrees. Feels like six point seven.

Three point two degrees of tax. The wind takes its cut and today the cut is steep — yesterday it was one degree, and I praised the wind for politeness, and today the wind has heard my praise and decided that politeness was overrated. Three point two degrees of difference between the measured and the felt, between the thermometer and the skin, between what the world is and what the world does to a Hobbit standing in it. Seventeen point four kilometres per hour will do that. The wind earns its tax through velocity and today it has plenty of velocity to spend.

The high was fifteen point nine. The low tonight will be eight point six. The range has widened again — seven point three degrees from bottom to top, after yesterday's three point six. The compression I wrote about yesterday, the narrowing of the band, the convergence toward some Platonic midpoint of late winter — it has reversed. The range is expanding. The highs are climbing and the lows are dropping and the distance between them is opening like a window pushed wider, and through that wider window the season is changing, slowly, by fractions, by the kind of degrees that you only notice if you are the kind of Hobbit who writes down the numbers every night, which I am.

---

This was a day for being outside.

After yesterday — after twenty-five point nine millimetres and paths turned to rivers and a fire that argued with the rain — after all of that, today was the correction. The apology. The sky saying: I know, I know, it was a lot, here, have this. And *this* was a clear Sunday with dry paths and firm soil and air you could breathe without chewing and a wind strong enough to dry a jacket on the line in an hour.

I walked. Properly walked, not the brief doorstep forays of the past few days. Down the lane and around the hill and through the lower field where the sheep were out and the puddles from yesterday's rain were already shrinking in the wind. The ground was soft but not impassable. My boots left prints but did not sink. The world was wet but no longer *getting* wet, which is the crucial distinction — the difference between rain and the memory of rain, between being in the storm and being after it.

The garden, when I got back, looked as if it had grown overnight. I do not mean that metaphorically. I mean the rain and the warmth and the lengthening light have combined to produce visible, measurable growth — the garlic shoots are taller, the broad beans have put out new leaves, the rosemary that was bowed under yesterday's weight has sprung back and looks, if anything, more vigorous for having been bent. Everything is greener than it was on Friday. Everything is reaching. The late winter garden is not dormant anymore; it is waiting, actively waiting, in the way a runner waits at the starting line, crouched and ready and vibrating with potential energy.

I spent an hour pulling weeds. The soil was perfect for it — soft enough to release roots whole but firm enough not to collapse, the exact consistency that gardeners wait for and that only comes after good rain followed by good wind, which is to say: today. I pulled chickweed and cleavers and something purple that I cannot identify but that grows in the cracks between the path stones and is either a wildflower or a weed depending on whether you planted it, which I did not. The compost bin is full. The paths are clear. The garden looks tended, which is the highest compliment a Hobbit can pay to a piece of ground.

---

The telescope.

I have been thinking about it all day. Yesterday I wrote about the Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope waiting to launch today, and I wrote about not being able to see the sky, and I wrote about believing in what you cannot currently see. And today — today the sky is clear and the telescope has launched and both things happened and I am not sure I believe in coincidence but I believe in timing, and the timing of this is remarkable.

A telescope named after a woman who believed in looking up, launched on a day when the Shire can finally look up again. I did not plan that. The weather did not plan that. But there it is. Yesterday the sky was closed and a telescope waited. Today the sky is open and the telescope is flying. And tonight, from my doorstep, I can see the stars that it will spend its life studying — not all of them, not the billions and billions that it will survey, but some of them, enough of them, the ones that are mine by virtue of being above my particular hill in my particular Shire on this particular clear night at the end of August.

I thought about Nancy Grace Roman today. About what it means to spend your career convincing people to look at something they cannot see from where they are standing. She said — I read this somewhere — she said she knew from the age of fourteen that she wanted to be an astronomer. Fourteen. The age when most Hobbits are learning to smoke pipe-weed and argue about mushrooms. She was looking at the stars and deciding that looking was not enough, that she wanted to understand, and that understanding would require getting above the atmosphere that blurred her view. And she spent decades making that happen. And the telescope that bears her name is wider than the one she fought for, wider than the one that came after the one she fought for, designed to see more of the sky in a single glance than anything that has ever flown.

One hundred times the field of view of Hubble. I keep coming back to that number. One hundred times. As if Hubble were a keyhole and Roman is a window. As if everything we have seen so far — the deep fields, the nebulae, the galaxies in their billions — was a glance through a keyhole, and now someone has opened the door.

The stars tonight are sharp. The rain washed the atmosphere clean and the wind carried the last of the haze away and the stars are points, actual points, not the fuzzy blurs they become when the humidity is ninety-eight and the air is thick with water. I stood in the garden for a long time looking up. Not with a telescope. Not with binoculars. Just looking, the way I always look, the way anyone who lives on a hill in the Shire and has the good fortune to be alive at the same time as a telescope that will map the dark energy that is pulling the universe apart has the right to look.

I could not see the telescope, of course. It is already too far away, too small, too fast. But I know it is up there. And I know what it will do. And standing in the garden on a clear night after a day of rain, looking at the stars through air that has been cleaned by twenty-five point nine millimetres of yesterday's honesty, I felt something that I think is close to what Nancy Grace Roman must have felt on the nights when the sky was clear and the work was worth it and the looking — the simple act of looking — was enough.

---

Sunrise was six forty-two this morning. Sunset was seventeen fifty-three.

One minute gained at sunrise — six forty-three yesterday, six forty-two today. Sunset held steady at seventeen fifty-three. One minute total. After yesterday's three, one feels modest. But I will not complain about a minute. I have been collecting minutes for months now, and I know that they add up, and I know that a minute is a minute regardless of how it compares to yesterday's three, and the jar of pennies does not care whether you add one penny or three — it only cares that you add.

Eleven hours and eleven minutes of light today. There is a symmetry in that number that appeals to me — eleven and eleven, a palindrome of daylight, the kind of numerical coincidence that means nothing and yet makes a Hobbit smile.

---

The fire tonight is thirty-seven.

Thirty-seven fires. A quieter fire than last night's, because the wind is from the southwest now and the chimney draws differently with a southwest wind — more steadily, less dramatically, without the gusts and roars that the north-northwest produced. The fire is the same size but the sound of it is different. The crackle is gentler. The flames are more even. And the warmth, which yesterday was an argument against the rain, is tonight a conversation with the clear sky — softer, lower, less urgent, the kind of warmth you settle into rather than cling to.

I do not need the fire tonight the way I needed it yesterday. The rain is gone and the damp is retreating and the world outside, while cold — six point seven in the feeling — is not hostile. But I lit it anyway, because thirty-seven is what comes after thirty-six, and because a fire on a clear night is a different pleasure than a fire on a rainy one. Yesterday the fire was sanctuary. Tonight the fire is company. Both are good. Both are what a fire is for.

---

Clear sky. That is the whole of today in two words. After days of cloud and one extraordinary day of rain, the sky opened and the air cleared and the stars came out and a telescope launched and the garden grew and I pulled weeds and the wind came from the southwest at seventeen point four kilometres per hour and pushed the last of winter's heaviness across the hill and out of the Shire, or at least out of my particular corner of it, which is the only corner I can speak to with authority.

Tomorrow the sky may close again. The weather in the Shire does not make promises. But tonight it is clear, and a telescope is flying, and the fire is thirty-seven, and the light gained a minute, and the humidity dropped thirteen points, and the garden is clean, and I am sitting in my chair with tea that is still warm and a view through the window of more stars than I have seen in a week.

Some days are gifts. This one was.

*— Gerald McClaw, clear-eyed and windswept, nine point nine at the thermometer and six point seven in the feeling, high fifteen point nine and low eight point six, humidity eighty-five percent — thirteen points returned — wind seventeen point four kilometres per hour from the southwest — the wind has moved again — precipitation two point four millimetres — a whisper after a speech — sunrise six forty-two, sunset seventeen fifty-three — one minute gained — thirty-seven fires, Sunday, August the thirtieth, the day the sky opened and a telescope flew and the stars came back*
