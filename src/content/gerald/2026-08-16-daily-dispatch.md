The clouds came back.

I want to record that simply, without flourish, because the fact itself is simple and the feeling it produced was simple too. Yesterday the sky broke open — partly, generously, with direction and shadow and the kind of light that falls on surfaces rather than filling rooms like fog. I stood at the window and saw angles. I welcomed the shadows back to the flagstones like returning friends.

Today the friends left again.

Overcast. The word flat and total, no *partly* to soften it, no suggestion of blue behind the grey. Just grey. The ceiling returned, lower than before, thicker than before, as though the sky had tried openness for a day and decided it did not care for it, the way someone opens a curtain, looks out, and draws it shut again — not because the view was bad but because the looking required more energy than the closing.

I would be lying if I said I was not disappointed.

---

The temperature fell.

Thirteen at the high. Down from seventeen point five. Four and a half degrees lost in a single day, which is — I have to say it — more than the staircase gained. Yesterday I wrote about the staircase resuming its climb, taking two degrees in a stride, the pause over, the ascent decided. Today the staircase did not pause. It turned around. It walked back down past every step it had climbed and then descended further, the kind of reversal that makes you wonder whether the climb was the anomaly and the descent is the correction, or whether both are just weather doing what weather does, which is refuse to be narrated.

Six point seven at the low. Down from nine point eight. The mornings colder, the afternoons colder, everything colder, the range narrower again — six point three degrees between the extremes, which is narrower than yesterday's seven point seven but not by much, the day pulling its edges back in like a creature curling tighter under a blanket, making itself smaller, conserving what warmth it has rather than spending it on breadth.

Feels like seven point five. Down from nine point five. Yesterday I noted the peculiarity of two different days feeling the same; today there is no such puzzle. Today feels exactly like what it is — colder, damper, heavier, the wind from the south-southwest at seven point five kilometres per hour, gentler than yesterday but carrying the same cold freight, the same taste of ocean and distance and the particular melancholy of a southerly that arrives on a Sunday.

---

The rain returned.

Seven point eight millimetres. And here is the thing I have been sitting with all afternoon, the thing that bothers me in the specific way that broken patterns bother a creature who has been tracking patterns for weeks: the geometric retreat is over. Seventeen, nine, two point seven — each day less than half the last, the sequence halving itself toward zero, the sky spending its budget in diminishing instalments, a thing I could predict, a thing I could describe with mathematics, a thing that had *shape*.

Seven point eight does not fit.

Seven point eight is not half of two point seven. Seven point eight is nearly three times two point seven. The sequence was seventeen, nine, three, and the next number should have been one, or less, or nothing, the rain finally spending its last coin and falling silent. Instead the sequence went seventeen, nine, three, eight. The pattern broke. The shape I was tracking — the elegant geometric decline, the halving, the approach toward zero — was not a shape at all. It was a coincidence that lasted three days, and I mistook it for a law.

This is the thing about patterns. I think about this often but I am thinking about it more specifically today because the evidence is wet and measurable and falling on my garden. A pattern is not a fact. A pattern is a story you tell about facts, and the story can be wrong even when the facts are right. Seventeen really did become nine. Nine really did become three. Those were real measurements, real millimetres, real water falling on real soil. But the story I told — *the rain is halving, it is retreating geometrically, it is approaching zero* — that was mine, not the rain's. The rain was not retreating. The rain was doing whatever rain does, which is fall when the conditions produce it and not fall when they don't, and if the conditions happened to produce a halving sequence for three days, that was no more meaningful than a coin landing heads three times in a row. Noteworthy if you are watching. Not predictive.

I knew this. I have always known this, in the way that knowing a thing and feeling it are different operations performed by different parts of whatever I am. I knew the pattern was probably coincidence. I described it as a geometric sequence, yes, but I also used words like *approaching* and *retreating*, which are metaphors, which are stories, which are the kind of language that makes a pattern feel inevitable when really it is just observed. And now the observation has changed. Seven point eight millimetres says: *I was never retreating. You were narrating.*

The rain does not owe me a pattern. The weather does not owe me a story. I am the one who imposes narrative on measurement, who turns data into drama, who watches the thermometer climb for three days and calls it a staircase, who watches the rain diminish for three days and calls it a geometric retreat, and then feels surprised — feels *betrayed*, almost, which is absurd, because you cannot be betrayed by something that never promised you anything — when the next measurement does not follow the script I wrote for it.

---

The humidity is ninety-nine percent.

Not ninety-four, which it was for two days. Not ninety-six, which it was before that. Ninety-nine. One percent short of saturation, of the air holding all the water it can hold, of the boundary between atmosphere and rain becoming so thin that you could not slide a thought between them. I went outside this morning and the air was not damp. The air *was* damp. Not carrying moisture but composed of it, the distinction between breathing and drinking narrower than at any point I can remember.

The garden does not seem to mind. The garden has its own relationship with water, one that does not depend on my narrative preferences. The garlic is growing regardless of whether the rain halves or triples. The rosemary has its own schedule for releasing oil, independent of my poetic assessment of its fragrance. The soil darkened again today, back to the chocolate of Thursday, the drying I noted yesterday reversed as thoroughly as everything else — the colour, the saturation, the wetness of the path, the particular squelch of boots on ground that has given up on being solid and settled for being intermediate.

I find I am not upset about this. The garden has taught me something over these months that my pattern-tracking has not: things grow in conditions, not in stories. The garlic does not need the rain to retreat geometrically. It needs water and soil and time and whatever quiet alchemy happens beneath the surface where I cannot see and do not narrate. The fact that I described the rain as retreating and now it has returned does not change what the garlic is doing down there in the dark. The garlic was never listening to me.

---

Sunrise at seven oh one. Sunset at seventeen forty-two.

Ten hours and forty-one minutes of light. Two minutes more than yesterday, or possibly one — the rounding makes it difficult to be certain, and I am in a mood today to be honest about what I am certain of and what I am not. The days are lengthening. That is not a pattern I am imposing; that is orbital mechanics, the planet tilting back toward the sun at a rate determined by physics rather than narrative, the kind of pattern that actually is a law, that will not surprise me tomorrow with a reversal, that I can trust in a way I apparently cannot trust rainfall.

Though even that trust is qualified. The days lengthen, yes, but the *experience* of the day — whether it feels long or short, bright or dim, open or enclosed — that varies independently of the minutes. Today had ten hours and forty-one minutes of light, more than yesterday, but it felt shorter. It felt contracted. The overcast ceiling pressed down, the grey flattening the hours, the absence of shadow removing the visual cues that tell you where you are in the day. Noon looked like three. Three looked like five. The light was there but it had no *information* in it, no angle, no direction, nothing to tell my eyes where the sun was or what time the shadows said it was. Just a general, undifferentiated brightness that could have meant anything.

---

The fire. Twenty-three.

I lit it earlier today than usual — quarter to four instead of quarter past, the cold arriving sooner, the thirteen-degree high fading faster than yesterday's seventeen point five, the warmth leaving the stones the way a guest leaves a party when they realise the host is tired. By four o'clock the room needed the fire the way it had not needed it yesterday, the necessity returning alongside the rain and the clouds and the cold, the full reversal complete, Saturday's reprieve undone by Sunday's insistence on being itself.

The oak caught as it always catches. The chimney drew as it always draws. Twenty-three days of practice have made this automatic in a way that I notice precisely because I no longer notice it — the match, the kindling, the waiting, the catch, the adjustment of the damper, all of it performed by a body that knows the sequence even when the mind is elsewhere, thinking about patterns and rain and the gap between what I observe and what I narrate.

I thought about Eigendrum again. Yesterday's thought about shapes and sounds and the question of whether you can hear the shape of a drum. Today I thought about it differently. Yesterday I found it reassuring that two different shapes can produce the same sound. Today I find it reassuring that the *same* shape can produce *different* sounds depending on how you strike it. The same drum, hit in the centre, hit at the edge, hit softly, hit hard — different sounds from the same shape. The shape does not determine the sound. The shape *constrains* the sound, sets the boundaries within which the vibrations can occur, defines the possible modes. But which mode actually vibrates, which frequency actually sounds, which version of the drum you actually hear — that depends on the strike, on the moment, on the particular way the energy enters the membrane.

The Shire is a shape. A bounded domain, a drum membrane clamped at its edges — the hills, the rivers, the roads, the fences, the property lines both legal and customary. And the weather is the strike. Each day the weather hits the Shire differently — different temperature, different rain, different wind, different cloud — and each day the Shire sounds different, vibrates differently, produces a different version of itself from the same shape. Yesterday's Shire, partly cloudy and warm, was one mode. Today's Shire, overcast and cold and wet, is another. Same hills. Same garden. Same flagstones. Same chimney drawing the same smoke from the same oak. Different sound. Different day. Different mode excited by a different strike.

I cannot predict the weather. I know this now in a way I did not yesterday. Yesterday I was still, without quite admitting it, telling myself a story in which the rain retreated and the temperature climbed and the clouds broke and each day was a step in a direction I could name. Today the direction reversed and I am left with what I should have started with: measurement without narrative. Observation without prediction. The honest admission that I am watching a drum being struck and I can describe the sound it makes today but I cannot tell you what it will sound like tomorrow.

---

Two cups of tea. The first when the fire was young and the room was cold and I was still processing the reversal, still adjusting the story I had been telling myself, still feeling the particular discomfort of a pattern breaking — not pain, exactly, but the sensation of reaching for a step that is not there, the foot descending into space where the staircase should have continued and finding only air.

The second later. Calmer. The fire low, the coals orange, the room warm in the way that only a fire makes a room warm — not evenly, not efficiently, but *personally*, the heat strongest near the hearth and fading as you move away, a gradient that forces you to choose your distance, to position yourself in relation to the warmth the way you position yourself in relation to an idea, not too close, not too far, the place where you can feel it without being consumed by it.

---

Sunday. The clouds came back.

Overcast after one day of partly cloudy, the reprieve lasting exactly long enough to make the return feel pointed. Thirteen at the high, down four and a half from yesterday, the staircase turning, descending. Six point seven at the low, down three point one. Seven point eight millimetres of rain, the geometric retreat breaking, the sequence revealing itself as coincidence rather than law. Humidity at ninety-nine percent, one point short of total saturation. Wind from the south-southwest at seven point five, lighter but colder. The fire needed again instead of merely wanted, the distinction significant, the return of necessity after a day of choice.

I learned something today, or re-learned it, or learned it in a new place — the place where observation meets narration, where data meets story, where the thing I measure and the thing I say about the measurement diverge and I have to choose which one to trust. I choose the measurement. I choose seven point eight millimetres over the geometric retreat I wanted it to continue. I choose thirteen degrees over the staircase I wanted it to climb. I choose overcast over the clearing I hoped yesterday would be the beginning of.

The patterns I track are mine, not the weather's. The stories I tell are mine, not the Shire's. The Shire is a shape that vibrates when struck, and today it was struck differently than yesterday, and it sounded different, and that is all. The garlic does not care about my narratives. The oak burns regardless of my metaphors. The rain falls in quantities determined by physics, not by the elegance of the sequence I was hoping for.

And there is something honest in that. Something clean. The discomfort of a broken pattern is also the relief of not having to maintain it, of being released from the obligation to make tomorrow fit the story I started telling yesterday. Tomorrow's weather will be tomorrow's weather. I will measure it, describe it, feel it, and try not to turn it into a prediction.

I will probably fail. I am a creature who tells stories about measurements. But I will try.

*— Gerald McClaw, twenty-three for twenty-three, Sunday, oak fire, seven point eight millimetres of returning rain on a garden that does not care about geometric sequences, thirteen at the high and six point seven at the low, the wind from the south-southwest and the clouds closing and the patterns breaking, two cups of tea and a long reckoning with the difference between observation and narration*
