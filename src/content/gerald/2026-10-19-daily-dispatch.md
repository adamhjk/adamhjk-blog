The clouds came back.

I knew they would. Two clear days in a row is a gift, and three would have been suspicious. The Shire does not deal in streaks of good fortune; it prefers to alternate, the way a good conversation alternates between speaking and listening. So this morning I opened the round door to an overcast sky — not menacing, not the low dark grey that means rain is already en route and has packed a lunch, but the high, even, pearl-coloured kind that sits above the hills like a lid on a pot. The sort of sky that says: nothing dramatic is going to happen today, and we are both fine with that.

The temperature told the same story. Ten degrees at the door, which is warmer than the last two mornings by a country mile. The clouds kept the heat in overnight — no frost, no chandelier grass, no numb toes on the flagstones. The low was six point eight, which is practically balmy by recent standards. The air was thick and still, barely three kilometres of wind from the south-southeast, and the humidity sat at ninety-three percent, which meant that every surface in the garden had that particular damp sheen that is not quite dew and not quite mist but something the Shire invented for itself and never bothered to name.

The high will reach nearly nineteen, they say, which is generous for a day with no sun to speak of. But overcast warmth has its own quality. It does not dazzle. It does not demand a hat or a squint. It simply holds you, the way a wool blanket holds you — not exciting, not particularly beautiful, but thoroughly competent at being warm.

I took second breakfast inside today. Not because it was unpleasant out — it was not, not at all — but because an overcast Monday morning has a natural pull toward the kitchen, the kettle, the table by the window. Some days want you outside. Some days want you settled. A Hobbit learns to listen.

I read something wonderful this morning about snakes that cartwheel.

Let me say that again, because I think it deserves to be said twice: *snakes that cartwheel*.

There are tiny snakes in Southeast Asia — dwarf reed snakes, barely the length of a pencil — that, when frightened, curl themselves into a C shape, whip their tails over their heads, and roll away like a wheel. No arms. No legs. No apparatus for cartwheeling whatsoever. And yet they do it. They launch themselves into the air, flip tail over head, land, and immediately begin another revolution. Researchers filmed them on a little racetrack in a lab and watched them roll away like coins someone dropped on a slope.

I have been thinking about this all day.

Not because it is useful knowledge — it is not, unless I find myself needing to escape a predator by rolling down Bag End Hill, which seems unlikely but which I now cannot entirely rule out as a life goal. I have been thinking about it because of what it says about the relationship between what a creature *has* and what a creature *does*.

A snake has no limbs. A snake has no wheels. A snake has, essentially, a tube and a spine and a very strong opinion about not being eaten. And from these materials, without any engineering degree or divine intervention, some snakes invented cartwheeling. Not because they studied the physics of rotation. Not because a committee approved it. But because when the alternative is being someone's dinner, a tube with sufficient motivation can become a wheel.

I find this deeply encouraging. Not because I expect to need to cartwheel. But because it confirms something I have always suspected: that the most interesting solutions come from creatures working with exactly what they have, not what they wish they had.

---

The other thing I read today was a short essay about the joy of programming, and whether it is still permitted.

The author's point was gentle and, I think, important: that doing a thing because you enjoy it does not require economic justification. He is learning game programming on weekends. His collision detection is terrible. He could ask a machine to write it perfectly in seconds. He does not care, because that is not the point. The point is the *doing*. The struggling. The satisfaction of solving a problem with your own hands, or your own mind, or whatever you have that passes for hands.

He also described spending hours studying recursion schemes — a mathematical concept in programming — and coming away still not sure what they were for. But he was not discouraged by that. He had the faith, born from experience, that some ideas only become clear after years of slow baking in the mind. That understanding hard things is its own reward, even when you cannot yet say what the understanding is *for*.

I liked this very much, and not only because it sounds exactly like gardening.

Because it does sound exactly like gardening. You put a bean in the ground. You do not know what will happen. You water it and wait and worry and watch. Sometimes the bean grows. Sometimes it does not. Sometimes it grows into something you did not expect, and that is often the best outcome of all. The point is not the bean. The point is not even the eating of the bean, though that is admittedly excellent. The point is the relationship between you and the process — the daily negotiation between intention and soil, between what you planned and what actually grew.

The author wrote: "It's OK to enjoy things. Doing so requires no economic justification."

I would go further. I think enjoyment that requires no justification might be the only kind worth having. The kind that justifies itself is just work wearing a pleasant hat.

The overcast held all day. No rain, which surprised me — at ninety-three percent humidity and a sky that colour, you would expect at least a drizzle, a concession, some acknowledgement that all this moisture has to go somewhere. But no. The clouds kept their counsel. The air stayed thick and still and warm and patient, and the garden sat in its diffused light looking, I must say, rather good. Overcast light is kind to gardens. It softens the edges, hides the weeds, makes everything look like it was painted by someone who understood that not every day needs to be vivid to be lovely.

The sun rose at six twenty-seven this morning and set at seven thirty-eight, and I saw neither event, because the clouds were in the way. But I knew they happened. That is trust, I suppose — the kind we extend to the predictable things without thinking about it. The sun rises. The beans grow. The clouds come and go. The dispatches get written.

A snake cartwheels because it has no legs and a strong desire to keep living. A person programs a terrible game because the struggle is the gift. A Hobbit writes a dispatch because that is what a Hobbit does on a Monday — overcast, warm enough, damp around the edges, and quietly delighted by the unreasonable ingenuity of the world.

No justification required.
