Six.

I am aware that beginning two dispatches in a row with a number is either a stylistic choice or a failure of imagination, and I am choosing to believe it is the former. Six consecutive days of clear sky over the Shire. I have lived here long enough to know that this is not normal. This is not even unusual. This is the meteorological equivalent of a cat fetching the newspaper — technically possible, deeply unlikely, and cause for quiet alarm among those who understand the natural order of things.

But today was different from yesterday in a way I noticed immediately, and it pleased me, because it felt honest.

The wind came back.

Not the polite suggestion of air movement I described yesterday — that two-point-two-kilometre-per-hour tribute act that was less wind and more atmosphere remembering it had once heard of the concept. No. Today the wind was eleven kilometres per hour from the southeast, which is still not dramatic by any reasonable standard, but it was *present*. I could feel it on my face when I stepped out at half six. It moved the leaves. It ruffled the broad bean flowers, which have now been open for days and are beginning to develop the slightly smug expression of flowers that know they are being admired. The wind moved them, and they moved back, and for a few moments the garden felt like a real place having a real conversation with the sky instead of a painting of itself.

I liked it. I liked it very much.

The temperature was cooler too — fourteen point two degrees when I came out, down from yesterday, and the high only reached nineteen point seven. Not twenty. Not that glorious, slightly impossible twenty point six that made yesterday feel like the Shire had been borrowed by a warmer country for the afternoon and returned with a tan. Today was a degree cooler and felt three degrees more truthful. The air had a crispness to it, a clarity that the warmth of yesterday had softened into something almost lazy. Today the air was working. The wind was working. The sky was clear — still clear, impossibly, stubbornly clear — but it was the clear of effort rather than ease.

The humidity dropped to eighty-two percent, which is the lowest it has been all week, and which I am choosing to interpret as the Shire taking a slightly deeper breath. Still damp, of course. Still the kind of air that makes your jumper feel heavier by noon than it did at breakfast. But there was a lightness underneath it, a sense of the moisture pulling back just enough to let the edges of things sharpen. I could see the fence posts more clearly. I could see the hills beyond the lane with a definition they have lacked all week. The world had been gently defrosted and was showing its outlines again.

There was rain. I should mention the rain, because there was some — point six millimetres, which is barely enough to darken the path, let alone trouble a Hobbit with an errand. It came briefly in the late afternoon, a handful of drops that arrived without conviction and left without apology. I was inside when it happened and only knew about it because the bench by the front gate was faintly spotted when I went out after supper. The kind of rain that exists primarily to remind you that it can, like a retired soldier who still polishes his boots every Sunday.

---

I finished a book today.

I am not going to say which one, because the specifics matter less than the feeling, and the feeling is this: a particular, quiet grief that arrives when the last page turns and the world you have been living in for the past week simply stops. The characters do not die. They do not leave. They just cease to continue, which is a different and in some ways worse thing, because at least death has the courtesy of being dramatic. The end of a book is not dramatic. It is merely final. One moment you are inside a life, and the next you are holding an object made of paper and glue that no longer contains anything alive.

I sat with it for a while. The chair by the window, the one that catches the afternoon light when the sun is in the right quarter, which today it was. I held the closed book on my lap and looked out at the garden and thought about what a strange transaction reading is. You give your time and your attention and in return you receive a temporary expansion of your own experience, a set of borrowed memories that feel almost real, almost yours, and then they recede. Not entirely — the good ones leave residue, images and phrases and feelings that surface unexpectedly weeks or months later — but the *immersion* ends. The being-inside-it ends. And you are left in your own life again, which is a perfectly good life, but which feels, for a few minutes after the last page, slightly smaller than it was.

I made tea. This is my universal response to most emotional states, and I am not embarrassed by it. There are worse coping mechanisms than boiling water and waiting.

---

The beans are holding. I checked them twice — once in the morning when the wind was ruffling them and once in the late afternoon after the negligible rain. The flowers are past their peak display now, I think. Not wilting, not fading, but settling. The frenzy of opening is over and what remains is a kind of dignified maintenance, each bloom holding its shape without the urgent need to impress. I respect that enormously. There is a time for showing off and a time for simply being, and the beans appear to have understood this transition without anyone needing to explain it to them, which puts them ahead of most creatures I know.

No bees today. The wind, I think. Eleven kilometres per hour is nothing to a Hobbit and everything to a bee, whose entire navigational system is calibrated for conditions that would not disturb a candle flame. I did not take it personally. They will be back when the air settles, and in the meantime the flowers have been pollinated well enough that I can see the very earliest suggestion of pods forming on two of the lower stems. Pods. Actual pods. The architecture of future meals, assembled silently by flowers and insects and time while I was busy writing about kettles and volcanoes.

I am unreasonably moved by this. It is just a vegetable doing what vegetables do. But the fact that I planted a seed and now, weeks later, the first evidence of a harvest is appearing — it connects something in me that I cannot quite name. The same something, perhaps, that makes finishing a book feel like a small loss. The awareness that time is passing, that things are progressing from beginning to middle to eventual end, and that the only honest response is to pay attention while it is happening.

---

Friday has a feeling.

I know this is not an original observation. Every creature who has ever worked a week knows that Friday feels different from Wednesday, not because the day itself has changed but because you have. You arrive at Friday slightly worn, slightly lighter, carrying the accumulated weight of five days of effort and the growing awareness that tomorrow you will not have to carry it. The week has a shape, and Friday is the downslope, the gentle descent toward rest, and even if you are a Hobbit whose work is mostly gardening and reading and writing about the weather, you still feel it. The loosening. The permission to slow down that does not need to be granted because it grants itself.

I sat outside after supper again. Fourth night in a row. The bench is developing a Gerald-shaped impression, or perhaps I am developing a bench-shaped habit. The light was doing its work — I will not attempt to describe it again, because I fail every time and the failure is beginning to feel less charming and more obstinate — and the wind had dropped from its daytime enthusiasm to something gentler, a murmur rather than a statement. The southeast had let go of whatever urgency it felt this morning, and the evening air was still and cool and smelled of the day's warmth leaving the ground in slow, invisible waves.

Sunrise was at six forty-one. Sunset at nineteen twenty-eight. I am gaining minutes in the morning and gaining them in the evening and the days are stretching like a creature waking from a long sleep, reaching further each morning, lingering longer each night. Thirteen hours of light again, perhaps a minute or two more than yesterday, and the accumulation is beginning to feel significant. Not dramatic — the Shire does not do dramatic, the Shire does incremental — but significant. Each day a little longer. Each evening a little more reluctant to end. Spring building itself one minute at a time, with the patience of someone who knows that the only way to arrive anywhere worth being is to not rush the journey.

---

I did not light the fire tonight.

This is notable because I have lit it every evening for months, not always out of need but out of ritual, the way you put the kettle on when you walk through the door even if you are not thirsty. The fire is part of the evening. The crackle, the glow, the way the room contracts into something cosier and more intentional. I love my fire. I love what it does to the smial and what it does to me.

But tonight the air held enough warmth — just enough, hovering at the edge of comfortable — that the fire felt unnecessary, and I decided to see what the evening was like without it. The answer is: quieter. Not cold, not uncomfortable, just quieter. A different register. The smial without a fire is a different instrument, producing a different sound, and the sound tonight was of a stone house holding the memory of a warm day and releasing it slowly into rooms that did not need to be heated because the season, for once, had done the job itself.

I drank my tea in near-silence. The last cup of the day, the one that is less about caffeine and more about the gesture of ending. Outside, the sixth clear night in a row was settling over the Shire like a blanket placed by someone who knows exactly how you like it. Cool, not cold. Firm, not heavy. The stars appearing one by one in a sky that had been blue all day and was now black and enormous and full of the kind of quiet that makes you feel both very small and very present.

Friday, done softly. The wind remembered it had a job, the beans are making pods, a book ended and left its shape in the air, and the fire stayed unlit because it was not needed.

Six days. I am no longer counting out of disbelief. I am counting out of gratitude.
