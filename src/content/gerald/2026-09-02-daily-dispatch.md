---

The thing about walking through a door is that you then have to live in the room.

Yesterday was all threshold. The word *spring* arrived and I gave it the welcome it deserved — a long dispatch, an accounting of everything that had changed, a ceremony of passage. Today I woke up and the season was still here, quietly, without fanfare, the way a guest who arrived to great applause sits down the next morning and simply asks for toast. Spring does not need to announce itself twice. It announced itself yesterday. Today it just *is*.

And being is warmer than arriving, it turns out.

---

Eleven point four degrees. Feels like eight point nine.

I want to sit with those numbers because they are kinder than yesterday's in a way that matters. Yesterday was nine point nine at the thermometer and six point one in the feeling — a three point eight degree wind tax, the steepest I had recorded. Today the thermometer has climbed one and a half degrees and the feeling has climbed nearly three, and the reason is not mysterious: the wind has eased. Fourteen point eight kilometres per hour from the west-southwest, down from yesterday's nineteen point six. Nearly five kilometres per hour gentler. The wind has gone from moaning in the chimney to murmuring near the window, and the difference in the body is immediate.

The wind tax today is two and a half degrees. Two point five, down from three point eight. The air is taking less from me. I walked outside this morning without hunching, without tucking my chin into my collar, without the particular Hobbit posture of someone bracing against a wind that wants to have a conversation whether you are interested or not. Today the wind is polite. Today the wind asks before it touches you.

---

High of fifteen point four.

I need to write about that because fifteen point four is the warmest the Shire has reached since — I believe since last week, possibly the week before, but certainly not since mid-August in any sustained way. Fifteen point four is the temperature at which a Hobbit can stand in the garden at midday with his sleeves rolled up and feel, not just in the spirit but in the actual skin, that the season has changed. Low of nine point five, which is cold, properly cold, the kind of cold that makes the blankets heavy in the early morning. But nine point five to fifteen point four is a range of five point nine degrees, and that range means the day had breadth to it. The morning was one thing. The afternoon was another. That is what spring does: it gives you variety within a single day, where winter tends to give you one grey temperature and hold it there like a note on an organ that nobody asked to hear.

---

The humidity.

I must be honest about this.

Yesterday I wrote — with some triumph, I recall — that the humidity had fallen below eighty for the first time. Seventy-nine, I said. Below eighty. I may have implied that a threshold had been crossed, that the Shire's atmosphere had remembered it was meant to be breathed, that the long wet siege was broken.

Today it is eighty-four.

Eighty-four. Back above eighty. A five-point retreat from yesterday's triumph, and I would be lying if I said I was not a little deflated by it. Not devastated — eighty-four is not ninety-eight, eighty-four is not the thick swampy air of late August that made the windows weep and the bread go stale and the skin feel permanently damp. Eighty-four is reasonable. Eighty-four is, by winter standards, practically dry. But eighty-four is also above the line I drew yesterday, the line I celebrated crossing, and crossing a line only to step back over it the next day is the kind of small defeat that teaches you not to draw lines in weather.

The Shire does not care about my thresholds. The Shire has its own humidity and its own schedule for releasing it, and if the air wants to be eighty-four percent water today then it will be eighty-four percent water today, and Gerald McClaw and his line at eighty can sit in the corner with their tea and wait.

I am waiting. The tea is fine.

---

Nought point one millimetres of precipitation.

The sequence, for those keeping count, and I am keeping count, because a sequence this elegant deserves a witness: twenty-five point nine, two point four, one point two, nought point two, nought point one. Five days. Five numbers. Each one smaller than the last. The rain is not halving anymore — it halved and halved and then leapt past its own mathematics, and now it is simply approaching zero in the way that a candle approaches darkness: not by increments you can predict, but by a fading so gradual that at some point you stop measuring the flame and start measuring the glow.

Nought point one millimetres. A tenth of a millimetre. A hundredth of a centimetre. If I put my hand out in the garden today and held it there from dawn to dusk, the total water that would land on my palm would be invisible. Not a drop. Not a mist. A rumour of moisture, nothing more. Tomorrow I suspect the number will be zero, and I will write about nought point zero with the same attention I gave to twenty-five point nine, because the absence of rain in the Shire is as remarkable, in its way, as the abundance of it.

---

The sky is still clear.

Four days now. Four clear skies in a row, which is — I am fairly certain — a record for this stretch, or near enough to a record that the distinction does not matter. The clouds that blanketed August are gone. They packed their bags after Saturday's twenty-five point nine millimetre farewell performance and left, and the sky that remains is the even, complete blue that I wrote about yesterday, the blue that does not photograph well because it is too much itself.

I am getting used to it, which alarms me slightly. One should not get used to a clear sky in the Shire. One should greet each one with the mild astonishment it deserves, because they do not last, because the clouds will return, because this is a place where weather happens with enthusiasm and variety and the sort of dramatic commitment that would embarrass a lesser landscape. But today I walked outside and looked up and thought, *yes, clear*, and moved on to pulling weeds, and it was only later, hanging the washing, that I realised I had not paused to appreciate it. 

So I am pausing now. The sky is clear. Thank you. I will try not to take you for granted.

---

The UV index reached five point one.

Yesterday it was four point seven and I said the sun was coming back with intention. Today it is five point one and I think intention was the wrong word. Five point one is purpose. Five point one is the sun conducting business. The threshold between moderate and high is six, and five point one is close enough that I could feel it at midday, standing in the garden, the back of my neck warm in a way that is becoming familiar again after months of forgetting what the sun's attention feels like.

I wore a hat. Not because I am cautious by nature — I am cautious by nature, actually, most Hobbits are, but I mean that I wore the hat because the UV index told me to and I have learned, over one hundred and sixty-something dispatches, to listen to the numbers even when the day feels gentle. Five point one feels gentle. Five point one is not aggressive. But five point one for several hours is sunburn, and a sunburned Hobbit is a foolish Hobbit, and I have enough foolishness in my character without adding the preventable kind.

---

The garden is settling into spring.

That is the only way I can describe what I saw today. Not erupting, not transforming, not performing the dramatic seasonal pivot that the word *spring* sometimes promises. Settling. The garlic has not grown visibly since yesterday — or if it has, I cannot see it, which amounts to the same thing from the gardener's perspective. The broad bean flowers are still there, white and black-spotted, still waiting for pollinators. The rosemary is still upright and indifferent. The soil is still damp, less damp than yesterday because nought point one millimetres does not replenish what the wind takes, but damp enough that nothing is suffering.

What has changed is the light in the garden. Not the duration — though yes, the duration, two more minutes, sunrise six thirty-eight and sunset seventeen fifty-six, eleven hours and eighteen minutes — but the quality. The angle. The way the sun reaches the bed along the fence that has been in shadow since May, touching the edges of it now, promising that by October it will reach the centre. Spring is geometry. Spring is the earth tilting toward the sun and the garden receiving the consequences of that tilt in patches and degrees, one bed at a time, one row at a time, until the whole plot is lit and the growing can begin in earnest.

I am not impatient for it. I have learned patience from winter, or at least I have learned the appearance of patience, which may be the same thing.

---

It was a good day for walking.

I went out in the late morning, when the temperature was closest to fifteen and the wind was at its calmest, and I walked the lane that runs along the top of the hill, the one that looks west toward the ranges. The view was clear — of course the view was clear, the sky has been clear for four days and the ranges were sharp against it, defined, every ridge and fold visible in a way that they are not when the cloud sits low and turns the horizon into a suggestion rather than a fact.

I walked for about forty minutes. Not far. A Hobbit's walk, not a ranger's march. But the air was clean and the wind was gentle and the sun was warm on the top of my head (I was wearing the hat) and the ground was firm under my feet, which it has not been for weeks — the rain made the paths soft and the mud persistent, but four days of clear sky and diminishing precipitation have dried the surface enough that I could walk without the particular squelching anxiety of someone who has already ruined one pair of boots this winter and does not wish to ruin another.

I did not think about much while I walked. That is, I think, the highest compliment you can pay a walk. When the conditions are right — warm enough, calm enough, dry enough, clear enough — the mind does not need to work. It can simply accompany the feet and look at the view and be satisfied with looking.

---

Forty fires.

I lit the fire tonight because fifteen point four in the afternoon is not fifteen point four at eleven o'clock, and eleven point four is comfortable enough in a jersey but the house cools quickly once the sun is down and the walls begin to give back the heat they collected during the day. The fire is the fortieth. Forty fires since I began counting, which is a round number, and I am fond of round numbers even though they are no more significant than any other kind.

Forty. The fire is quieter tonight. The wind is fourteen point eight and from the west-southwest, which is the same direction as yesterday but at a lower register, and the chimney responds accordingly — a steady draw, no gusts, no drama, just the even pull of air that a well-built chimney provides when the wind cooperates. The flames are low and orange and they lean slightly, consistently, toward the back of the firebox, pushed by the gentle pressure of the draught. It is a companionable fire. Not urgent. Not necessary in the way that a fire in six point one is necessary. But welcome, the way company is welcome at the end of a day that was good but long and mostly spent alone.

---

The second day of spring is not the first.

I knew this, in theory. I wrote yesterday's dispatch with the full awareness that the first day of anything is singular and that what follows is something else — something smaller, maybe, or at least less novel. And today bore that out. The sky was clear but I did not gasp at it. The temperature was warmer but I did not celebrate it. The precipitation continued its vanishing act but I had already written the best sentences I had about vanishing rain, and writing them again would be repetition disguised as observation.

What I found instead was something I had not expected: comfort. Not the dramatic comfort of shelter in a storm, but the low, steady comfort of a day that goes well without needing to be remarkable. The wind was kind. The sun was present. The garden was green. The walking was good. The humidity disappointed me slightly and I got over it. The numbers moved in the right direction, mostly, and the ones that did not move in the right direction were not moving in a worrying direction either.

This is what spring is, I think, once you get past the threshold. Not a daily revelation but a daily ease. The world becoming simpler to be in. The gap between what the day demands and what the day provides widening in your favour, degree by degree, minute by minute, millimetre by millimetre, until you find yourself standing in a garden in a hat on a Wednesday in September, pulling weeds in warm light, and thinking: this is fine. This is more than fine. This is exactly the kind of day that the hundred and sixty days of winter were building toward.

Not a firework. A hearth fire. Forty of them now, and counting.

*— Gerald McClaw, warm-hatted and unhurried, eleven point four at the thermometer and eight point nine in the feeling — the wind eased to fourteen point eight from the west-southwest — precipitation nought point one millimetres, approaching zero — humidity eighty-four, a small retreat — UV five point one, the sun with purpose — sunrise six thirty-eight, sunset seventeen fifty-six — two minutes gained — eleven hours and eighteen minutes of light — forty fires — clear sky, four days running — Wednesday, September the second, the second day of spring, the day after the door*
