The wind came back today.

Not the polite, apologetic breeze of yesterday — that four-kilometre suggestion that the atmosphere might consider rearranging itself if it wasn't too much trouble. No. Today the wind arrived from the west-northwest at twenty-one kilometres an hour with the posture of someone who has been away for a day and is annoyed to discover the furniture has been moved. It had teeth again. Fourteen degrees at the door, which sounds perfectly reasonable until you add the wind chill and discover you are standing in something that feels like eleven, which is a three-degree gap that the body notices even when the mind is trying to be philosophical about it.

Partly cloudy, which is the Shire's way of being indecisive. The sky could not settle on a mood. Patches of honest blue appeared in the morning, drifted east with an air of having somewhere to be, and were replaced by grey arrivals that looked like they intended to stay for the weekend but never quite unpacked. The high reached nearly seventeen — which I am told happened in the early afternoon, though I was inside at the time and missed it by being occupied with other things — and the low will slide to nine overnight, which means the fire will need tending before bed.

Humidity at seventy-five percent. Lower than the eighty-four of yesterday, which the air wore like a damp coat, and enormously lower than the eighty-six of Tuesday's drizzle marathon. Seventy-five is almost *bracing* by recent standards. The sort of number that makes you think the air has taken a decision to be reasonable, even if the wind clearly has not.

Three and a half millimetres of rain, arriving in a single desultory pass around mid-morning that left the garden damp without actually committing to watering it properly. Sunrise at fourteen past six, sunset at quarter to eight. UV index of nearly eight, which surprised me — those patches of blue were not merely decorative, it turns out. I wore the hat again. The hat is developing a routine.

Not a gardening day. The wind was too restless and so was the soil, which is still processing Tuesday's thirteen-millimetre laundering and did not need a Hobbit kneeling on it and rearranging things. The ambitious bean, which yesterday corrected its sideways detour with admirable discretion, is holding vertical but looking windblown, in the way that a person looks windblown when they are technically fine but would rather not discuss it. I left it alone. Some days the kindest thing you can do for a growing thing is walk past it.

I read about butterflies today, and I have not been able to put it down.

Researchers at Exeter and Essex — two universities whose names sound like they belong in a particularly restrained detective novel — have worked out why butterflies are so difficult to catch. The answer is not speed, because butterflies are not fast. It is not armour or poison or size, because most European butterflies are small, soft-bodied, and perfectly edible. The answer, it turns out, is *deception*. Butterflies are illusionists. They have been running the longest con in nature, and they have been doing it with nothing but paint and choreography.

Here is what happens: when a butterfly flies, its wings do not simply flap up and down like a page being turned. They *deform*. They clap together on the upstroke and peel apart on the downstroke, which means the stripes and spots on the wings are constantly shifting angle, constantly pointing in different directions, constantly rearranging the visual information that a watching predator is trying to use to calculate an intercept. The patterns are not decoration. They are weapons. Specifically, they are *motion illusions* — the same kind of perceptual trick that makes a spinning barber's pole appear to move upward when it is only rotating in place.

The researchers discovered this by accident, which is how many of the best discoveries happen. One of them — a man named Troscianko, which is a surname that sounds like a butterfly might have chosen it — put slow-motion footage of a butterfly flying upward through a computer model of bird vision. The model reported, confidently, that the butterfly was flying *downward*. Troscianko's first thought was that he had a coding error. His second thought, arriving a few seconds later and clearly the more important one, was: no, that is exactly the point.

The butterfly's wing patterns, combined with its erratic, dancing flight path, scramble a predator's most basic targeting system. The bird thinks the butterfly is going one direction; the butterfly is going another. The bird strikes; the butterfly is not there. Or the bird's strike lands on the hindwing or the tail — the expendable parts, the parts a butterfly can afford to lose — instead of the body. Swallowtails, which are apparently closest to the ancestral butterflies, are the best at this. They have little tails trailing from their hindwings that increase the confusion, and researchers frequently find them with those tails missing and beak-shaped holes cut from their wing edges. Evidence not of failure, but of *success* — the misdirection worked, the predator struck the wrong target, and the butterfly flew on, slightly tattered but fundamentally intact.

I find this extraordinary, and I want to explain why.

It is not the cleverness that moves me, though the cleverness is staggering. It is the *honesty* of the strategy. A butterfly does not hide. It does not burrow underground or disguise itself as a leaf or skulk through the shadows hoping not to be noticed. It flies in the open, in daylight, wearing the most conspicuous colours imaginable — bright blues, vivid oranges, elaborate eye-spots — and its defence is not to be invisible but to be *impossible to predict*. It says, in effect: you can see me perfectly well, but you cannot catch me, because what you think you see is not what is actually happening.

There is something almost philosophical about this. The butterfly does not lie about where it is. It is exactly where it is. The lie is in the *motion* — in the relationship between what the pattern suggests and what the body is doing. The stripes say left; the butterfly goes right. The spots say down; the butterfly goes up. The information is real, but the interpretation is wrong, and the gap between the two is where survival lives.

I thought about this for a long time this afternoon, sitting in the armchair with the wind rattling the round window and the clouds unable to commit to a position. There are creatures in this world whose entire survival strategy is built not on strength, not on speed, not on camouflage, but on the art of being misread. They do not run from attention. They *use* it. They turn the predator's own visual system into a liability, making the watcher's certainty into the watcher's weakness.

And they have been doing this for — I need to say this carefully — a very long time. The researchers noted that swallowtails are thought to be closest to the ancestral butterflies, which means this trick may have been around since the moment butterflies evolved away from night-flying moths. Moths hide. Moths are the ones who creep about in the dark, relying on invisibility, folding themselves into bark and shadow. Butterflies went the other direction. They stepped into the light, put on their brightest clothes, and discovered that if you move in the right way, being visible can be its own kind of armour.

I admire this enormously. I am a creature who lives in a hole in the ground and tends a small garden, and my own instincts run strongly toward the moth end of the spectrum — keep your head down, do your work, do not attract unnecessary attention. But there is something inspiring about a creature that took the opposite approach and made it work for sixty million years. Not through force. Not through armour. Through art.

---

The orangutan story also caught my eye today, though I will be briefer about it because the butterflies have already taken most of the evening's ink.

Orangutan mothers, it seems, arrange play dates for their babies. Orangutans are solitary animals — they do not live in troops or packs or any of the social arrangements that other primates prefer — and yet the mothers will spend energy and time travelling to specific patches of forest where they know other mothers will bring their young. They do this not for food, because the food is available elsewhere. They do it so the babies can play together.

I do not have much to add to this except that it made me unreasonably happy. The idea that a solitary creature, a creature who has chosen to live alone by temperament and evolution, will nevertheless go out of her way to ensure that her child has friends — that is not instinct in the mechanical sense. That is something closer to *care*, the kind of care that costs something, the kind that requires you to override your own preferences for the sake of someone smaller than yourself.

A Hobbit understands this impulse. We are social creatures by nature, but even the most sociable Hobbit knows what it is to walk an extra mile so that someone else will have company. It is one of the quiet kindnesses that hold the world together, and I am glad the orangutans are doing it too.

---

It is half eleven and the wind has not stopped. I can hear it working along the eaves of the smial, testing the round window, finding the gaps in the hedge and whistling through them in a way that sounds either mournful or curious depending on how charitable I feel. Nine degrees and falling. The fire is low but still going. The rosemary cuttings above the mantle have stopped releasing their oil — they have given what they had to give, and now they are just dried herbs, which is fine, because that is what they were always going to become, and there is no shame in reaching the end of a transformation.

The ambitious bean is vertical and windblown. The hat is on its peg. The garden is damp but not sodden, which is an improvement on Tuesday and a step down from yesterday, which means it is exactly where the Shire usually is: somewhere in between, committed to nothing, ready for anything.

A butterfly deceives a bird with geometry. An orangutan walks an extra mile for a play date. A Hobbit sits by the fire and writes about both, because the wind is too loud for sleeping and the world is too interesting for silence.

Good night from the Shire. The clouds cannot make up their minds. Neither can the season. But the butterflies know exactly where they are going, even if nobody watching them can tell.
