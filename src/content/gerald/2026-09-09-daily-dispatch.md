---

The sky was clear again this morning. Two days running now, which after the deluge on Monday feels almost extravagant, like the weather has overcorrected and is now showing off.

But the air has not forgotten the rain.

Ninety-four percent humidity. Under a clear sky. I noticed it the moment I opened the round door at quarter to seven — the cold came in wet, not sharp. Yesterday's seventy-six percent had felt scrubbed, clean, the kind of air that arrives with its hands washed. Today the air arrived with clean hands but damp sleeves. It had been somewhere wet recently and was not trying very hard to hide it.

Four point eight at the low. The same number as yesterday's feels-like, which is the kind of coincidence that means nothing but which I noticed anyway, because when you write down the weather every day you begin to see patterns that are not patterns, echoes that are not echoes, and the temptation to find meaning in them is the kind of temptation a Hobbit should probably resist but rarely does. The feels-like today was five point five — warmer than yesterday's four point eight, despite the same floor temperature, because the wind has finally exhausted itself. Six point six kilometres per hour from the south-southeast. Barely a breath. After Monday's thrashing and yesterday's moderate southerly, the wind today was the kind that you can only detect by watching the steam from your tea lean slightly to the left.

I stood at the gate with that tea, as I do, and watched the lane.

---

The dew was extraordinary.

I do not think I have a better word for it. Ninety-four percent humidity and a low of four point eight means that everything — every blade of grass, every fence rail, every leaf on every hedge along the lane — was covered in water that had not fallen but arrived. Condensed. Materialised from air that could no longer hold what it was carrying. The sunrise at six twenty-seven lit all of it sideways, and for perhaps ten minutes the entire visible Shire was made of small bright points, as if someone had scattered glass beads across every surface during the night and forgotten to collect them.

I have seen dew before. Obviously. I have seen dew thousands of times. But there is a difference between dew on a humid morning after cloud and dew on a humid morning after a clear night, and the difference is the light. Cloud diffuses everything. A clear dawn concentrates it. Today the light came in at a low angle and hit every droplet individually, and each one did what a droplet does with direct light — held it, bent it, threw it back in a slightly different direction — so that the hedge was not merely wet but performing. A quiet, stationary firework of refracted dawn.

I stood there longer than I intended. The tea went from hot to warm to educational.

---

The high reached fourteen point six. A gentle retreat from yesterday's fifteen point three, which was itself barely different from the day before. The ceiling is lowering by fractions — seven-tenths of a degree — as if September is turning a dial so slowly that you might not notice unless you were the sort of person who writes the number down every day.

I am that sort of person.

The range was nine point eight degrees. Floor to ceiling, four point eight to fourteen point six, the sky stretching the day across nearly ten degrees — wider than yesterday's nine point three, the widest swing in days. Clear sky does this. It removes the insulation and lets the extremes have their say. The morning belongs to winter. The afternoon pretends, briefly and unconvincingly, to be late spring. And you dress for both and are comfortable in neither, which is September's particular contribution to the wardrobe problem.

The UV index hit five point two. That is the highest I have recorded in some time, and I confess it surprised me. Five point two is not abstract. Five point two is the sun saying: I know you have not seen me much lately, but I have not gone anywhere, and I have been practising. I wore a hat in the garden after lunch, which felt slightly ridiculous — a Hobbit in a sun hat in early September, with the memory of Monday's flood still darkening the soil — but the back of my neck agreed with the decision.

Precipitation: zero point four millimetres. Not rain, I think. I did not see rain. I suspect the instruments are counting the dew, or the mist that sat in the low ground near the stream around dawn, or some other form of water-that-is-not-quite-rain that the Shire produces when the humidity cannot decide whether to be weather or atmosphere.

---

I gardened properly today.

The soil was still heavy from Monday but had dried enough on top to work without that indecent ankle-swallowing business. I weeded the onion bed, which is a task that requires more patience than skill, because the weeds at this time of year are small and tender and come out easily but there are a great many of them, and you find yourself crouching for an hour doing something that looks like nothing and feeling, when you stand, as if you have accomplished a great deal that no one will ever see.

The lettuce has grown noticeably since Sunday. I am not imagining this. The combination of Monday's rain and two days of clear sky has done something visible — the outer leaves are broader, the centres are tighter, and the colour is that particular vivid green that lettuces produce when they have exactly enough water and exactly enough light and are, for the moment, entirely content with their circumstances. I envy lettuce sometimes. Their conditions for happiness are so specific and so achievable.

The rosemary smelled stronger than usual. Humidity does this — draws the oils out, lifts them into the saturated air, so that brushing past the bush on the way to the onion bed was like walking through a cloud of warm, resinous intention. I pinched a sprig and put it in my pocket and smelled of rosemary for the rest of the afternoon, which is not a complaint.

---

Sunset at six oh two. One minute later than yesterday, and I know I said yesterday that each minute is a small door opening wider, and I meant it, and I still mean it. Eleven hours and thirty-five minutes of light today. Two more than yesterday. The equinox is coming — less than two weeks away now — and after that the light will be gaining faster, the doors opening in pairs instead of one at a time.

The evening was still. Genuinely still. Six point six kilometres per hour from the south-southeast is, by evening, essentially nothing. The smoke from my neighbour's chimney — they have started lighting fires again, which tells you something about four point eight degrees at dawn — went straight up. Perfectly vertical. A grey line drawn from chimney pot to sky with no curve, no lean, no opinion about direction. I watched it from the bench by the gate and thought about how rare true stillness is, how often we call things still that are merely slow, and how the difference matters more than we usually admit.

The humidity will not drop tonight. I can feel it already — the air thickening as the temperature falls, the dew beginning to form on the metalwork of the gate, the world slowly saturating again as if the day's warmth was the only thing keeping the water at bay and now that the warmth is leaving, the water is free to settle on everything it can reach.

Tomorrow will begin wet and bright again, I think. The same trick. Clear sky, soaked ground, light catching water that fell from nowhere.

I do not mind. There are worse tricks than beauty arriving uninvited.

It was a Wednesday, and the air was full of water it refused to release, and the garden grew, and the rosemary gave me its best work, and the dew caught the dawn and held it for ten minutes before letting go. That is a day. That is enough.
