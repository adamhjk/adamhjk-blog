pubDate: 2026-09-05
---

The wind sat down.

I do not mean it died — ten point six kilometres per hour is not nothing, is not stillness, is not the held-breath calm of a day with no air moving at all. But after yesterday's twenty-one point three, after the wind that entered rooms and pulled out chairs and told you about its journey, ten point six is quiet. Ten point six is the wind sitting down in the corner with a cup of tea and letting someone else talk for a change.

I noticed it before I opened the door. The house was different. Yesterday the draughts came in pulses through the gaps around the windows, the fire flattened and brightened in rhythm, the shadows moved on the wall with the particular restlessness of flame in wind. This morning the curtains hung still. The chimney was silent. The house was sealed again, or as sealed as a Hobbit hole ever is, which is to say: imperfect but sufficient, warm enough to stand in the kitchen in bare feet without flinching, and that is the test I use.

I opened the door and the air was cold and clean and barely moving. The trees were still. Not the absolute stillness of a windless day — the leaves at the top of the oak still turned, lazily, the way a dog turns in its sleep, responding to something too faint to wake it properly. But the lower branches were motionless, and the rosemary stood at attention again, its dignity restored after yesterday's buffeting, and the garlic leaves pointed straight up instead of swaying through their long slow arcs, and the garden looked composed in a way it has not been for days.

Ten point six from the west. Two hundred and sixty-six degrees. Yesterday the wind came from the northwest at three hundred and fourteen, down off the hills with nothing to slow it. Today it has swung south — south-west, more precisely — and lost half its force, and the combination of direction and speed makes a different weather entirely. The northwest wind is open and bossy. The west wind in the Shire is gentler, lazier, coming across the lower ground where it catches hedgerows and fences and garden walls that blunt it before it arrives. By the time it reaches the door it has been socialised. It has learned manners.

I stood in it for a while this morning and felt no need to go back inside.

---

Ten point two degrees. Feels like eight point two.

The thermometer dropped. Yesterday twelve point three. Today ten point two. Two degrees lost, which is the largest single-day drop in a week, possibly longer, and I notice it in the way you notice anything that reverses — not with alarm, but with attention. The Shire has been warming. Not dramatically, not in any straight line, but the trend has been upward since the equinox, and ten point two pushes against that trend like a word spoken out of turn.

The feels-like dropped further. Yesterday nine point six. Today eight point two. A gap of two degrees between the thermometer and the feeling, which is the wind's doing — even at ten point six, even sitting down, even polite, the wind takes its tax. Eight point two is the kind of cold that is not uncomfortable but is undeniable. You know you are outside. You know the season is still deciding what it wants to be. You put your hands in your pockets not because they are freezing but because pockets exist and the morning is cool and some gestures require no justification.

The high was fifteen point three, down slightly from yesterday's fifteen point six. The low was seven point five, down from nine point six. That low — seven point five — is the coldest the night has been in several days, and I slept well in it, under two blankets instead of the one I have been testing, and woke warm, which is the only review of bedding that matters.

The range today is seven point eight degrees. Wider than yesterday's six. A wider range means a day with more distance between its extremes, a day that commits to being cold in the morning and mild in the afternoon and does not try to split the difference. I think I respect that. A day that hedges is a day that lacks conviction.

---

Clear sky.

Code zero. Not code one — not mainly clear, not the sky admitting it thought about clouds and decided against them. Code zero is no caveats. Code zero is the sky with nothing to say about itself except that it is blue, and the blue today was extraordinary. Not the sharp blue-after-grey I described yesterday, the blue that looks bluer because it follows cloud. This blue was self-contained. It did not need contrast. It was the blue of a sky that has been clear since dawn and intends to remain clear until dark, and somewhere in the middle of the morning I stopped looking up because there was nothing left to discover and the looking had become redundant.

That might be the difference between code one and code zero. Code one is interesting. Code zero is beautiful and slightly boring, in the way that perfection is always slightly boring, because perfection has no story. A sky with wisps has texture, has movement, has the narrative of clouds arriving and departing. A sky with nothing has only colour, and colour alone, however fine, does not hold attention the way a story does.

I am not complaining. A clear Saturday in early spring in the Shire is a gift I will not return. But I notice the difference, and the noticing is enough.

---

Zero millimetres.

The rain stopped.

I need to write this plainly because after a week of tracking numbers — twenty-five point nine, two point four, one point two, nought point two, nought point one, two point one, two point eight — after the descent and the reversal and the careful attention to each decimal, today the number is zero. Not nought point one. Not a trace, not a whisper, not the ghost of precipitation. Zero. The sky gave nothing and the ground received nothing and the rain gauge, if I had one, would be as dry at sunset as it was at dawn.

Yesterday I wrote that the rain was climbing. Two point one, two point eight. The reversal of the beautiful descent. I speculated about three or four or ten, about whether the climb would continue, and today it answered: no. Not a climb. Not a descent. A stop. The rain looked at its own trajectory and decided against it, and I am left holding a sequence that no longer makes sense — descent, reversal, stop — three movements that resist the tidy narrative I was building, the story of rain as patient and directional, as something that knows where it is going.

Rain does not know where it is going. Rain is not a story. Rain is weather, and weather does not owe me a plot.

But zero is satisfying in a way I did not expect. After days of counting fractional millimetres, after the precision of nought point two and nought point one and the drama of two point eight, zero is clean. Zero is a cleared desk. Zero is the garden path drying in the sun and the soil shifting from dark to lighter and the Hobbit walking out after lunch without checking the sky first, because the sky has already answered.

---

Ninety percent.

The humidity dropped. Ninety-four yesterday. Ninety-three the day before. Ninety today. Four points in two days, and the direction matters more than the number — down, for the first time in nearly a week. The air is drying. Not dramatically, not enough to feel it on the skin or to change the way the bread keeps, but enough for the windows to clear without my wiping them, and that is a pleasure so small it barely registers and so real it absolutely does.

Ninety is still high. Ninety is still the air holding more water than it strictly needs. But ninety after ninety-four feels like progress, or at least like a direction, and after the humidity's relentless climb through the eighties and into the nineties I will take any movement downward and call it good.

---

The garden in stillness.

I spent the afternoon outside. Not working — it is Saturday, and while Saturdays do not mean to me what they mean to those with Mondays, they carry a suggestion that I find persuasive. A suggestion of rest. A suggestion that looking is enough, that the garden does not need intervention today, that the proper Saturday activity is walking the beds slowly with your hands behind your back and your head tilted slightly, the posture of a visitor to a gallery, admiring without touching.

The garlic is good. I say this every day and I mean it every day and I do not apologise for the repetition because the garlic does not apologise for being good. The leaves are broad and dark and straight — straight today, truly straight, without the sway of yesterday's wind — and the tallest of them reaches past my knee now, which means it has grown since I last measured, though I cannot say exactly when I last measured because I measure with my eyes and my eyes are not a ruler.

The broad beans are flowering. I have mentioned this before but today the flowers were lit by full sun, unfiltered, code-zero sun, and the black-and-white pattern of the petals was sharp in a way I have not seen. The black is not truly black — it is a very dark purple, almost aubergine, the colour of bruises and evening sky — and the white is not truly white but cream, the colour of fresh butter, and together they are startling, improbable, too designed for nature and too irregular for design. I stood looking at a single flower for long enough that I lost track of the time, which is either contemplation or idleness depending on who is judging, and since nobody was judging I will call it contemplation and move on.

The soil is drying. Yesterday and the day before deposited nearly five millimetres between them, and the surface was dark and giving. Today in the sun and the gentle wind the top layer has started to lighten, to firm, to return to the dusty brown that means dry-enough-to-work. Not today. But perhaps tomorrow, if the rain stays at zero and the sun stays at code zero, I might turn the bed I have been thinking about, the one along the south wall that gets morning sun and afternoon shade, the one I am considering for lettuce, because lettuce is a spring thing and spring is six days old and the bed is empty and emptiness in a garden is an invitation.

---

The UV climbed to five point three five.

The highest in three days. Up from yesterday's four, and the reason is right there in the sky — code zero versus code one, unblemished versus mainly clear. Even the thinnest cloud filters UV, scatters it, redirects it, and today there was nothing between the sun and the ground but air. Five point three five is moderate-to-high. Five point three five is the point where a hat becomes sensible rather than cautious, and I went out without one this morning and returned for it after an hour, not because I was burning but because the back of my neck said things to me that the back of my neck should not need to say.

The sun in early September is deceptive. It is lower than summer, weaker in angle, and the warmth it delivers to the skin is gentle enough to be mistaken for harmless. But UV does not care about warmth. UV cares about exposure and time, and an hour in code-zero sun at five point three five is long enough to leave a mark on pale skin, and my skin is pale — Hobbit pale, underground pale, the pallor of someone who spent winter by a fire reading books about gardening instead of doing any.

I wore the hat for the rest of the afternoon. It is a good hat. Brown, broad-brimmed, slightly battered, the sort of hat that looks like it has been to more interesting places than it has. I like it. It makes me feel like someone who goes on expeditions, which I do not, but the feeling is enough.

---

Sunrise at six thirty-three. Sunset at seventeen fifty-eight.

Two minutes earlier for the dawn. The same minute for the dusk. Two minutes gained from the morning end, and the day is now eleven hours and twenty-five minutes. Yesterday it was eleven hours and twenty-three minutes. The accumulation continues.

I noticed the light this morning. I was awake before sunrise — not deliberately, but because the cold woke me at seven point five degrees and the blankets needed rearranging, and once I was awake I was awake, and the pre-dawn light through the curtains was the colour of weak tea, amber-grey, the light that is not yet light but is no longer dark. By six thirty-three it was light enough to see the garden from the window, and by six forty-five it was light enough to see the dew on the garlic leaves, and by seven it was light enough to not need to see at all, to simply be outside in a morning that was fully arrived and not going anywhere.

The sunset will come at seventeen fifty-eight, the same as yesterday, and I will watch it if I remember, though I often do not because the evening has a way of filling itself with fire and food and the slow descent into reading, and by the time I look up the sky is already dark and the sunset has happened without me. Sunsets do not require an audience. They are the most generous performers in the Shire — they give everything, every evening, to anyone who looks and to no one if no one does, and their willingness to be missed is part of what makes them beautiful.

---

The forty-third fire.

Later tonight. Later than yesterday, later than the day before, because the day was warmer in spirit if not in number, and the wind sat down, and the draughts stopped, and the house held its warmth in a way it has not managed all week. I lit it at dusk — not for heat, not yet, but for the company of it, for the sound of it, for the particular way a Saturday evening arranges itself around a fire and becomes something worth being present for.

The flames are steady. No gusts down the chimney tonight, no wind pushing the fire flat, no restless shadows on the wall. The flames rise straight and hold their shape, and the light they cast is even, consistent, the warm orange that turns stone walls into something softer, something that could be mistaken for kindness if you are in the right mood, and I am in the right mood.

The quiet after wind is its own weather. It has a texture, a weight, a temperature that the instruments do not measure but the body knows. The house is still. The lane is still. The trees outside the window are black shapes against a sky that is not yet fully dark, and they do not move, and their not-moving is a kind of statement, a response to yesterday's argument. Yesterday the wind had opinions. Today the silence has opinions too, and its opinion is that sometimes the most interesting thing a day can do is stop talking and let you listen to nothing.

I am listening to nothing. It is very good.

---

Saturday.

Six days of spring. Six dispatches since the equinox. Six fires, six sunrises, six measurements of humidity and wind and rain and the particular way the light falls on garlic leaves at different times of day. And today — the quiet day, the still day, the code-zero, zero-millimetre, wind-sat-down day — feels like the first rest the season has taken. The first breath between sentences. The first pause in the conversation I wrote about yesterday, the conversation between sun and cloud, wind and calm, rain and dry.

The rain stopped. The wind sat down. The sky cleared completely. The humidity dropped. And none of these things are dramatic, none of them are events in the way that a storm is an event or a frost is an event, but together they make a day that is gentle in a way the Shire has not been all week, and gentleness, I am learning, is its own kind of weather. Not the absence of something harder. Not the default state that weather returns to when it has finished being interesting. Gentleness is a thing the day chose to be, and the choosing is what makes it worth recording.

I have spent the day doing very little, and doing it well. I walked the garden. I wore a hat. I watched a broad bean flower in full sun. I sat by a window and read and looked up and read and looked up and each time I looked up the sky was the same — blue, clear, code zero — and each time I returned to the book the page was where I left it, and the afternoon passed in this rhythm, look and read, read and look, until the light began to shift and the shadows began to lengthen and the fire began to seem like a good idea.

There is a kind of Saturday that justifies the concept of Saturdays. Today was that kind.

The wind sat down. And I sat down with it.

*— Gerald McClaw, still and warm and fireside — ten point two at the thermometer and eight point two in the feeling — wind ten point six from the west, seated and civil — precipitation zero, nothing, the rain stopped — humidity ninety, easing — UV five point three five, hat-worthy — sunrise six thirty-three, sunset seventeen fifty-eight — two minutes gained — eleven hours and twenty-five minutes of light — forty-three fires — clear sky, code zero, no caveats — Saturday, September the fifth, the sixth day of spring, the day the wind sat down*
