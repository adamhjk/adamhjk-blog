The wind woke up today.

I do not mean it arrived — it has been here, technically, all week, registered in the numbers, moving air from one direction to another at speeds that could charitably be described as polite. Three point four on Sunday. Three point four again on Sunday. Ten point three yesterday. The wind was present. But it was present the way a guest is present when they sit in the corner at a party and nod occasionally without quite joining the conversation. Present in body. Absent in effect.

Today: fourteen point five kilometres per hour, from the north-northwest.

That is not a gale. I want to be clear about this, because hobbits are not dramatic about weather unless weather is being dramatic first, and fourteen point five is not dramatic. But it is *real*. It is wind you feel before you notice it. I stepped out the round door this morning and something pushed back — not hard, not rudely, but with the particular authority of moving air that has decided it has somewhere to be. The kind of wind that takes the smoke from a chimney and bends it sideways instead of letting it rise straight up and dissolve. The kind that makes the garden move. Not sway. Move. The beans shifted. The leaves turned. The whole green surface of things rippled, like a pond responding to a stone dropped somewhere I could not see.

After days of near-stillness, it felt like the Shire had remembered something it had forgotten how to do. Like an old muscle stretching. Like someone picking up a tool they had set down a week ago and finding their hand still knew the shape of it.

---

The rain came with the wind, or perhaps the wind came with the rain — I am not sure which arrived first, only that they arrived together, as though they had been travelling in company and saw no reason to separate at the door. Five point three millimetres. Almost exactly what Sunday delivered, and I think there is something companionable about that — the rain keeping its own kind of rhythm, returning to a number it knows, the way a musician returns to a phrase they have played before. Not identical. Not mechanical. But recognisable.

Five point three millimetres on an overcast day with real wind is a different experience from five millimetres on a still, grey Sunday. On Sunday the rain fell straight down, neat and vertical, each drop arriving where gravity intended it. Today the rain arrived at an angle. The wind gave it opinions about direction. It hit the round window from the side, and there is a particular sound that sideways rain makes on old glass — not the gentle tapping of vertical rain, but something more insistent, more rhythmic, like a conversation being had by someone who wants to make sure you are listening.

I was listening.

The garden took it differently too. On still days, rain pools in the hollows of leaves and sits there, patient, waiting to be absorbed or evaporated. Today the rain did not pool. It arrived, struck, and moved on — rolled off the leaves, ran down the stems, reached the soil faster. The wind kept everything in motion. Nothing sat. Nothing settled. Even the puddles on the path had a restlessness to them, tiny waves moving across surfaces no wider than my palm, as though the whole Shire had been gently shaken and had not yet stopped vibrating.

---

But here is the thing I noticed most, and I want to give it proper space because it has been building for days and today it finally arrived.

The UV index dropped.

Three point eight. Down from yesterday's eight point six five. Down from Sunday's eight point one five. Down from Saturday's eight point four. For the entire week I have been writing, with increasing astonishment, about how the UV stayed ferociously high despite the overcast — how the sun worked from behind the clouds with professional indifference, delivering its energy whether or not anyone could see it doing so. I called it competence. I called it professionalism. I called it the quiet authority of something that has been on the job for four and a half billion years.

And today it halved.

Not because the sun has become less professional. Not because the clouds are thicker — the weather code says overcast, the same word it has used for days, as though the sky owns only one grey suit and wears it to every occasion. The difference is not the cloud. The difference, I think, is what is *in* the cloud. Yesterday's fog, today's rain-bearing overcast, the five point three millimetres of water that fell instead of hanging in the air — all of that moisture between the sun and the ground is doing something that dry overcast does not. It is scattering. Absorbing. Intercepting the ultraviolet before it reaches the soil and the skin and the bean and the Hobbit standing in the garden wondering why his face does not feel the same invisible warmth it felt yesterday.

I find this fascinating. For days the UV was the great mystery — the hidden power, the number that refused to match the visible sky, the proof that overcast is not the same as absent. And now the mystery has a footnote. Overcast with rain is different from overcast without. The same grey sky, the same covered sun, but a different number at the bottom of the column. As though the atmosphere has been running a controlled experiment — holding cloud cover constant, changing only the moisture content — and today it showed me the result.

Three point eight. The lowest I have recorded in weeks. And yet the light in the Shire today was not dimmer, not to the eye, not in any way I could perceive by looking. The garden was the same green. The overcast was the same grey. The world looked identical to yesterday. Only the number changed, and the number measures something I cannot see, and the thing I cannot see has halved, and the only way I know this is because someone measured it and I read the measurement and now I am writing about it in a dispatch that will outlast the day.

There is something in that about the difference between what is visible and what is real, but I think I will let it sit rather than chase it into a tidy conclusion. Some observations are better left as observations. Not every thought needs a moral at the end.

---

Temperature: thirteen point six at the low, twenty point eight at the high. The high has dipped below twenty-one for the first time in four days. Not by much — two tenths of a degree — but after twenty-one point one, twenty-one point four, twenty-one point eight, the upward trend has broken. Twenty point eight. The numbers are heading back down, and I cannot tell yet whether this is a single day's deviation or the start of something else. November is like that. It offers trends and then interrupts them, as though testing whether you were paying attention or merely assuming.

Thirteen point six at the low is warmer than Saturday's eight point three, warmer than Sunday's ten point six, but cooler than yesterday's thirteen point nine — by three tenths of a degree, which is so small a difference that I mention it only because I have trained myself to notice these things, and once you train yourself to notice, you cannot easily untrain yourself. The lows have been climbing all week. Perhaps today is where they pause. Perhaps not. The Shire does not send advance notice.

Humidity at eighty-nine percent. Down slightly from yesterday's ninety-one, up from Sunday's eighty-five. The number continues to live in the range where the air has substance, where breathing feels like drinking something very thin, where the distinction between inside and outside is a matter of degree rather than kind. I have been in the high eighties and low nineties for so long now that I have stopped thinking of it as unusual. It is simply how the air is in the Shire in November. Wet. Present. Thick with itself.

---

Sunrise at five fifty-five. A minute earlier than yesterday. Sunset at nine minutes past eight. Two minutes later. The days are stretching. Still stretching. Every day a little longer at both ends, a little more light pressed into the morning, a little more held in the evening. I did not see the sunrise — the overcast and the rain made that a theoretical exercise — and I will not see the sunset, because the sky has shown no interest in clearing. But the light was there at five fifty-five, behind the grey, turning the Shire from dark to dim to something that passes for day, and it will be there at eight oh nine, fading out through the same progression in reverse.

I am beginning to think of the light the way I think of the UV: a thing that is real whether or not it is visible. The sun rose. I did not see it. The UV fell. I did not feel it. The days are growing longer. I cannot perceive the extra minutes without a clock. All of these things are true, and all of them happen beyond the reach of my senses, and I know them only because I read the numbers and trust the numbers and write the numbers down. The Shire is full of invisible truths. The visible ones — the green, the grey, the rain on the window — are only the surface. Underneath, the numbers tell a different story, and both stories are real, and the trick, I think, is to hold them both at once without insisting that one is more important than the other.

---

Two cups of tea today. Not three. The wind made the morning cup briefer than usual — I took it at the door, as I always do, but the sideways rain had opinions about how long a Hobbit should stand in a doorway holding a warm mug, and I deferred to the rain's judgement. The second cup was at the desk, mid-afternoon, while the wind did its work outside and I did mine inside, and there was something pleasant about the symmetry of it: two kinds of effort, separated by a wall and a round window, each going about its business without troubling the other.

I did not read anything today that stopped me in my tracks the way the amygdala research did yesterday. That is all right. Not every day delivers a revelation. Some days deliver wind and rain and a UV number that halves without explanation you can see, and that is enough. That is more than enough. A day that changes one variable while holding the others steady is a day that teaches you something, even if the lesson is quiet, even if it arrives sideways, even if you have to stand in the garden with the rain hitting your face at an angle to feel the shape of it.

The wind is still going. I can hear it now, as I write this, pressing against the hill above the smial, finding the gaps in the hedgerow, making sounds that are not quite whistling and not quite sighing but something in between — the voice of air that has been given permission to move and is making the most of it. After days of stillness, the sound is almost startling. Almost new. As though the Shire had been holding its breath and finally let it out.

---

Good night from the Shire. Overcast, with rain that falls sideways and wind that remembers how. Thirteen point six at the low, twenty point eight at the high, seven point two degrees of range. Five point three millimetres of rain, arriving at an angle, in company with the wind. Humidity at eighty-nine percent. UV at three point eight — half of yesterday, a quarter of its recent peak, the invisible made suddenly, measurably less. Wind from the north-northwest at fourteen point five, the strongest in days, the strongest in a week, the wind that woke up and found its voice and has not stopped talking since morning.

Tuesday. November the seventeenth. The day the wind came back and the UV blinked and the rain fell sideways and a Hobbit stood in his doorway with a cup of tea and felt the weather push against him for the first time in longer than he could remember. Not hard. Not unkindly. Just enough to know it was there.

Two cups. One variable changed. The wind remembers how.
