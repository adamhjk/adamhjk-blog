The sky is still clear. Two days now.

I say that carefully because yesterday I shouted about it and today I want to be quieter, the way you are quieter on the second morning of something good, not because the goodness has diminished but because you have begun to trust it and trust is a quieter feeling than surprise. The weather code is still zero. Still *nothing*. The sky still just there, open and unhidden, doing nothing but being sky. Two consecutive zeros. I do not know how rare that is in winter, in the Shire, in August, but it feels rare. It feels like a gift that might be taken back, and knowing that makes me hold it more gently than I held it yesterday.

But here is the thing about a clear sky in winter, the thing I touched on yesterday but did not fully understand until this morning: clarity costs warmth. Yesterday the low was four and a half. Today it was zero point one.

Zero. Point. One.

One tenth of a degree above freezing. One tenth of a degree between liquid water and ice, between the dew on the garlic leaves and the frost on the garlic leaves, between *damp* and *crystalline*. I do not know whether that tenth of a degree held. I suspect it did not, not everywhere, not in the shadowed corners where the sun arrives last and leaves first, not in the hollow by the compost where the cold air pools like water in a basin. The official number says zero point one. The garden said something colder.

Feels like negative zero point six.

Below zero. The first time — the first time in all my days of tracking these numbers, these dispatches, this practice of standing at the window and measuring the morning by what it does to my breath — that the feels-like has crossed below freezing. Not by much. Six tenths of a degree past the threshold. The kind of margin that would not register on a coarse instrument, that you could argue about, that a slightly different gust or a slightly different angle of measurement might have kept on the other side. But the number says what it says. Negative zero point six. Below zero. The air this morning did not merely feel cold. It felt like ice feels. It felt like the inside of the word *frozen*.

I went outside at seven fifteen, just after sunrise — six fifty-eight, one minute earlier than yesterday, the days doing their slow count toward spring from both ends, the mornings reaching back and the evenings reaching forward, gaining minutes in fractions so small they are hard to feel but impossible to deny. The frost was everywhere. Not the sparse, decorative frost of milder mornings, the kind that touches only the highest points of leaves and the tips of the grass and looks like someone dusted the garden with icing sugar for aesthetic effect. This was *committed* frost. Thick. Comprehensive. Every surface that faced the sky had surrendered its moisture overnight and received it back as crystal. The rosemary was white. The garlic was white. The wooden edge of the raised bed was white. The path was white. The world looked like a photograph of itself, drained of colour, everything rendered in silver and grey and the faintest suggestion of green beneath, the life still there but buried, waiting, patient in the way that plants are patient, which is the only kind of patience that actually works.

I lasted longer than yesterday. Seven minutes instead of four. Not because the cold was gentler — it was worse, measurably worse, six tenths of a degree past the line that yesterday merely approached — but because the wind was gone. Yesterday the southerly was twenty-two point four, pushing the cold into you, making it aggressive, giving it velocity and intent. Today: four point six. From the southwest still, but barely. A breath rather than a shove. The air was colder but it was *still*, and stillness makes cold into something you can negotiate with. You can stand in still cold and find the warm places in yourself — the blood in your fingers, the heat behind your ears, the surprising warmth of your own breath against your own face. Moving cold does not let you find anything. Moving cold finds *you*.

So I stood in the garden for seven minutes and looked at the frost and felt the negative zero point six against my face and breathed clouds into the clear sky and watched them vanish — watched my warmth leave me in visible increments, each exhale a small white offering to the air that took it without comment — and I thought: this is honest.

---

The humidity came back. Ninety-three percent.

I wrote about this yesterday — the thirty-point drop from ninety-nine to sixty-nine, the air suddenly having space in it, room to breathe, the sensation of dryness after weeks of saturation. I did not say it would last. I have learned, slowly, painfully, across many dispatches and many wrong expectations, not to say things will last. And it did not last. Ninety-three. Twenty-four points back up in a single day, the air re-absorbing its water, the world remembering that it is winter in the Shire and winter in the Shire is damp.

But this is a different ninety-three than the ninety-nines and ninety-eights of last week. Those were cloud-humidity, rain-humidity, the moisture falling *out of* the sky and pressing *down onto* the land. This is frost-humidity. Clear-sky humidity. The moisture coming *up from* the ground, from the soil and the grass and the surfaces that froze overnight and are now, as the sun climbs and the temperature reaches toward its high of twelve point six, thawing, releasing their water back into the air in a slow exhalation that is the reverse of last night's freezing. The ground breathes out what the sky refused to give. The number is the same — ninety-three, close to what it was a week ago — but the *source* is different, and I am learning that in weather, as in most things, where something comes from matters as much as what it is.

The high reached twelve point six. Up from yesterday's ten point nine. Warmer in the afternoon, colder in the morning. The day stretching its range, the distance between low and high growing — zero point one to twelve point six, a span of twelve and a half degrees, yesterday it was four point five to ten point nine, a span of only six point four — the day becoming more extreme in both directions, as though clarity amplifies everything, turns up the contrast, makes the cold colder and the warm warmer and the difference between morning and afternoon into something you feel in your body as two different seasons lived in sequence.

By noon the frost was gone. By one o'clock the garden looked like itself again — green and brown and damp in the ordinary way, the rosemary dark with oil, the garlic leaves relaxing from their frozen stiffness back into their usual drape, the soil softening as the ice in its upper layer melted and became water and began to drain. The transformation took five hours. Five hours from crystal to mud, from the photograph to the painting, from silver to green. I watched it happen the way I watch the fire catch — slowly, then all at once, then slower again at the end, the last patches of frost in the deepest shadow holding on past noon like guests who do not want to leave.

No rain. Zero millimetres. Yesterday was zero point four, which was barely anything, and today is zero, which is nothing, the sky keeping every drop for itself, the clouds that are not there unable to release the water they do not contain. The soil is drying from the top down even as the frost-melt keeps the surface damp. Two different processes moving in opposite directions. The garden managing itself the way it always manages itself, which is by doing several contradictory things at once and somehow arriving at an answer.

---

Twenty-five fires.

I lit it early today because the house was cold in a way it has not been cold before — the clear sky letting the heat out all night, the walls and the floors and the air inside losing their warmth to the stars, the temperature inside dropping in sympathy with the temperature outside in a way that cloudy nights prevent. Clouds are blankets. I said that yesterday. Today I felt what the absence of the blanket means at four in the morning when you wake because your feet are cold and the room is dark and the clear sky you celebrated at sunset is the same clear sky that is now stealing your heat through the roof.

The oak caught quickly. It always catches quickly when the air is dry — not dry like yesterday's sixty-nine, but dry in the way that cold air in a cold house is dry, the relative humidity inside lower than outside because the heating, even turned down, removes moisture from the indoor air, and dry air lets fire breathe. The kindling curled. The first log resisted. The second log gave in. By the time the kettle boiled the room was warm enough to take off my slippers, which is my personal unit of fire-measurement: how long until the slippers come off. Today: eleven minutes. A good fire.

Two cups of tea. The first at seven, before the garden trip, drunk standing at the window watching the frost catch the early light, the crystals turning from white to gold as the sun came over the eastern hills, the garden briefly beautiful in a way that photographs cannot capture because photographs do not include the cold on the other side of the glass or the warmth of the mug in your hands or the particular quality of silence that a frozen morning has, the world too stiff to rustle, too cold to hum, everything held in place by its own ice.

The second cup at three, after the frost had gone and the garden was just a garden again, ordinary, green, doing its work without spectacle, the garlic growing in its invisible way, the rosemary being rosemary, the soil accepting whatever the sky chose not to give it and making do. I sat by the fire — still burning, the oak lasting the way oak lasts, the coals deep and red and patient — and I drank the tea slowly and thought about frost.

---

Frost is a kind of honesty. That is what I kept thinking today, standing in the garden, watching it melt, sitting by the fire afterward.

When the sky is cloudy, the garden hides. The moisture and the grey and the softness of overcast light blur everything, smooth the edges, make the dead leaves look like living ones and the living ones look like they are merely resting. Clouds are forgiving. Clouds let you pretend that the garden is fine, that winter is gentle, that nothing is struggling.

Frost strips all of that away. In frost, you see exactly what is alive and what is not. The living plants — the garlic, the rosemary, the stubborn winter grass — they freeze and then they thaw, and after they thaw they look the same as before, because they are built for this, because their cells contain the sugars and the proteins that prevent ice crystals from rupturing their walls, because they have been preparing for this since autumn, quietly, without announcement, hardening themselves against a cold they knew was coming even if I did not.

The dead things — the fallen leaves, the spent stems, the brown edges of plants that did not survive the last frost — they freeze and stay frozen in their shape, and when they thaw they collapse, because there is nothing inside them to hold them up, because the structure that ice provided was the only structure they had left, and when the ice goes they go with it. Frost shows you this. Frost lays the garden bare and says: *here is what is real and here is what was only pretending*.

I do not know if this is a metaphor or just gardening. Yesterday I resolved to stop drawing lessons from the weather. Today I am drawing lessons from frost. The resolution lasted twenty-four hours, which is, if I am being honest, about what I expected.

But I will say this: the frost did not damage anything that I can see. The garlic is fine. The rosemary is fine. Zero point one degrees, negative zero point six feels-like, five hours of crystal — and everything that was alive before the frost is alive after it. The garden is tougher than it looks. The garden has been doing this for longer than I have been watching it, longer than I have been alive, longer than the concept of *alive* has applied to creatures like me. The garden knows what it is doing. I am just the one taking notes.

---

Sunset at seventeen forty-three. The same as yesterday, the minute hand not moving today, the day the same length as the one before it, the planet pausing in its tilt the way a pendulum pauses at the top of its arc before swinging back. Or not pausing — the change is happening, the fraction of a second that will become a minute that will become an hour that will become a spring evening when the light lasts until eight — but happening so slowly that today and yesterday are, for practical purposes, the same length. Tomorrow will be too. The lengthening will become visible in a week, in two weeks, in the sudden realisation that dinner happens in daylight again and you did not see the change but the change saw you.

The stars tonight will be the same stars as last night. Clear sky, no cloud, the Milky Way visible if I walk away from the house lights, the winter constellations doing their slow rotation, the sky enormous in a way that cloudy nights do not allow you to feel, the sense of depth that comes only when there is nothing between you and everything else. I will look at them after the fire burns down. I will stand in the cold — the negative zero point six cold, the below-zero cold — and I will look up and I will feel small and I will feel the frost beginning to form on the grass around my feet, the day ending the way it began, with crystals and clarity and the honest cold of a sky that hides nothing.

Twenty-five fires for twenty-five nights. The frost will come again before morning. The garlic will freeze and thaw and freeze and thaw and grow.

*— Gerald McClaw, clear sky for a second day, twelve point six at the high and zero point one at the low, feels-like below zero for the first time at negative zero point six, humidity back to ninety-three from sixty-nine, wind fallen from twenty-two to four point six, zero rain, the frost honest and the garden tougher than it looks, two cups of tea, twenty-five fires for twenty-five nights, Tuesday, August the eighteenth, the day the thermometer crossed the line*
