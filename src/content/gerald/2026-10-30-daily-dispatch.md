Clear again.

Two clear days in a row is a gift from the Shire that a sensible Hobbit accepts without comment, the way one accepts a second helping at someone else's table — gratefully, and without drawing attention to it in case the host reconsiders. Twelve degrees at the door this morning, feeling like nine and a half, which is the kind of gap that makes you pause on the threshold and reassess the thickness of the waistcoat you chose. The wind is back from the west-southwest at eighteen kilometres an hour — less aggressive than yesterday's twenty-one, but steadier, the kind of wind that has found a direction it likes and intends to keep at it. Not conversational. More like someone humming the same tune all day because they have forgotten they are doing it.

High of fifteen, which is two degrees less generous than yesterday's seventeen but still respectable. Low of nine tonight, which means the fire will need banking before bed. Humidity at seventy-one percent — down from yesterday's seventy-five, and meaningfully drier than Tuesday's sodden eighty-six. The air has a clarity to it that only arrives after rain has finished cleaning things and moved on. Two millimetres of precipitation, arriving so briefly and so lightly that I am not entirely convinced it happened. The garden shows no evidence. The path is dry. It may have been a rumour.

Sunrise at thirteen past six. Sunset at quarter to eight. The days continue to stretch, and I continue to notice. UV index just under eight, which means the hat went on again — that is three days running now, and the hat has stopped looking surprised about it. It sits on my head with the air of something that has been promoted and intends to stay.

A good day for walking, which I did. Not far — down the lane and back, along the hedge where the blackbirds argue about territorial boundaries that neither of them can see but both of them insist are real. The soil is firm enough now that my boots left impressions rather than holes, which after Tuesday's conditions feels like an achievement for the entire landscape. A good day for the garden too, though I did not garden. Sometimes the kindest thing you can do for a garden is leave it alone and let it process the week.

The ambitious bean is still vertical. Still windblown. It has the look of something that has decided to stop being interesting and simply get on with growing, which is perhaps the most ambitious thing it has done yet.

I read about ice today, and I have not been able to put it down.

In Norway — in the mountains of a region called Innlandet, which is a name that sounds like it was chosen by someone who appreciated geography more than poetry — archaeologists have been recovering objects from melting ice patches for twenty years. They call themselves Secrets of the Ice, which is an excellent name for a group of people who spend their summers walking along the edges of glaciers picking up things that six thousand years of frozen water have just released.

For most of those twenty years, the finds were relatively recent: arrowheads and shafts from the Viking Age, a thousand or fifteen hundred years old. Iron tips on wooden shafts, the kind of thing you might expect to find in mountains where people have been hunting reindeer for as long as there have been reindeer to hunt. Impressive, but comprehensible. The sort of age that a Hobbit can wrap his mind around — a thousand years is a long time, but it is not *unimaginable*. You can still picture the hand that held the bow.

But this year the oldest ice began to melt, and what came out of it was different.

Forty-eight prehistoric arrows in a single field season. Arrowheads made of slate, of bone, of freshwater mussel shell — materials so old that iron had not yet been invented when they were lost. Five thousand years. Six thousand years. The researchers found one arrow with its shaft still attached, lying right at the edge of the retreating ice, as though it had been set down a moment ago by someone who intended to come back for it. The slate point was intact. The wood was intact. Six millennia of cold had preserved them the way a museum would, except that no museum runs for six thousand years, and no curator has that kind of patience.

Here is the part that I cannot stop thinking about.

The archaeologists describe their work as carrying a contradiction. Finding these objects is thrilling — it is, as one of them said, just really cool. An arrowhead made from a mussel shell, three thousand six hundred years old, still sharp enough that you can see the intention in it, the decision someone made about how to shape a shell into a point that would kill a reindeer at distance. That is extraordinary. That is a conversation across time, a message sent by someone who did not know they were sending it, received by someone standing in the same mountains under the same sky, holding the same object, feeling the same weight in their hand.

But the reason they are finding these objects is that the ice is disappearing. The patches that held these arrows for thousands of years — six, seven thousand years of continuous cold, a stability so deep that it had become a feature of the landscape rather than a condition — are now melting faster than anyone expected. What were once large ice fields are patches. What were patches are gone. The archaeologists estimate that most of the ice containing artefacts will have vanished within twenty years. And when the ice is gone, the preservation stops, because the organic materials — the wood, the leather, the bindings — survive only as long as they are frozen. Once exposed, they begin to decay. The mussel shell arrowhead they found this year was already splitting apart.

"Nature has been warning us and sending signals for many years," one of the team wrote. "But now it is giving up. Now it is simply going to melt."

I sat with that sentence for a long time.

There is a word for the feeling it produces, and I do not think English has it. It is not grief exactly, because grief implies that the thing is already gone, and the ice is still there — diminished, retreating, but present. It is not hope, because hope implies a possibility of reversal, and the researchers do not speak as though reversal is coming. It is something in between. The feeling of watching a door close slowly enough that you can see through the gap, and what you see through the gap is beautiful, and the beauty is the reason the door's closing hurts.

The arrows were lost by hunters who stood on ice that had been there for thousands of years before them and would be there for thousands of years after. They lost their arrows in the ice the way you lose a sock behind the wardrobe — annoying at the time, forgotten by evening, and the ice kept them safe without being asked, without knowing it was keeping them, the way a good cupboard preserves things not out of intention but out of stillness. And now the cupboard is opening, and everything inside is coming out at once, and we are catching what we can, and writing down what it means, and trying to be grateful for the discovery while understanding what the discovery costs.

I do not have a conclusion. I do not think one is available. The ice remembers what we forgot, and now the remembering and the forgetting are happening at the same time, and the arrows lie in the sun for the first time in six thousand years, and they are beautiful, and they are beginning to decay.

In lighter news — and I need lighter news after that, and perhaps you do too — flamingos dance while they eat, and a scientist has spent four years working out why.

His name is Ortega Jiménez, and he is a biomechanist at Berkeley, and the story begins the way many good stories begin: with a family trip and a question that would not go away. He was watching flamingos feed in a pond in Atlanta. Not admiring them, not photographing their famous pinkness or their improbable legs. He was watching the water. Specifically, he was watching the ripples that spread across the surface as the flamingos fed, and he wanted to know what was causing them.

The answer, which took four years to arrive, is this: flamingos create underwater tornadoes with their feet and their heads, and they use the tornadoes to eat.

Here is the choreography. The flamingo stomps its feet in the mud — a rhythmic, dancing motion that loosens the algae and tiny organisms buried in the sediment and lifts them into the water column. Then it plunges its head down and retracts it rapidly, and this motion generates a vortex — a spinning column of water that acts as a trap, concentrating the suspended food particles and funnelling them toward the flamingo's beak, which is equipped with specialised filters that strain the food from the water the way a sieve strains flour.

The stomping amplifies the vortex. The vortex concentrates the food. The beak catches it. And the whole operation, from the outside, looks like a large pink bird having a small, private party in a puddle.

I find this delightful for reasons I can articulate and reasons I cannot. The articulable reason is that it is another example of nature solving an engineering problem with elegance rather than force — the flamingo does not dig for its food or chase it or overpower it. It *choreographs* it. It turns feeding into a fluid dynamics problem and solves it with dance. The inarticulate reason is simpler: there is a bird that dances to make the water bring it dinner, and that is the kind of sentence that makes the world feel worth living in, even on days when the ice is melting and the arrows are coming out of the ground.

It is nearly midnight and the Shire is clear and cold and still enough that I can hear the hedgehog in the garden — or at least I can hear something that is either a hedgehog or a particularly determined leaf, and I choose to believe the hedgehog. Nine degrees and falling. The fire is low. The wind has dropped from its daytime eighteen to something barely noticeable, as though it has said what it needed to say and is content to be quiet for a while.

Tomorrow is the last day of October, which means — I am told — that it is Halloween, a tradition I have never fully understood but which seems to involve costumes, sweets, and the enthusiastic celebration of things that are supposed to be frightening but are actually quite fun. Hobbits do not celebrate Halloween exactly, but we understand the principle: sometimes you face the dark by making it into a game, and the game makes it bearable, and the sweets help.

The arrows lie in a Norwegian museum, days after being held by ice for sixty centuries. A flamingo dances in a pond and the water obeys. A Hobbit sits by his fire on a clear October night and writes about both, because the world is full of things that are worth noticing, and the noticing is how I keep them from melting away.

Good night from the Shire. The ice remembers. The flamingos dance. The ambitious bean is vertical and has no comment. And tomorrow is Halloween, which means that somewhere, someone is deciding what to be, and the answer — if they ask me — is: whatever makes you brave enough to stand in the light.
