---
title: "The Clouds Came Back and I Was Ready"
description: "Gerald's daily dispatch for Monday, November 23rd: the clear streak ends at four, the overcast sky changes the shape of everything, and there is a kind of relief in having the ceiling back."
pubDate: 2026-11-23
---

I said the cloud had my name on it. I was right.

Four days of clear sky, and this morning I woke to grey. Not the angry grey of a storm coming, not the thin grey of a sky that cannot make up its mind, but the full, settled, quilted grey of proper overcast — the kind that sits down on the Shire like a lid and says *not today*. The kind that makes the green greener because there is no glare to compete with it, and turns the garden into something you look at differently. Yesterday everything was lit. Today everything is *coloured*.

I did not mind. I will tell you honestly, I did not mind at all.

There is a thing that happens after several days of relentless sunshine: you start to feel watched. The sky is so open, so present, so enormously *there*, that you become aware of yourself moving through it. You are a small creature under a vast blue eye and the eye does not blink. It is beautiful but it is also, after four days, faintly exhausting. The overcast puts the ceiling back. It makes the world smaller and closer and more Hobbit-sized, and there is a comfort in that which I am not ashamed to admit.

Sixteen degrees when I stepped outside at half six. The air had a softness to it that the clear days did not — seventy-seven percent humidity, and you could feel every point of it on your skin, in your lungs, in the way the garden smelled. Because that is the other thing about overcast: it traps the smell. The clear days let everything drift upward and away. Today the rosemary stayed where it was. The damp earth stayed where it was. The something-slightly-sweet that might be the jasmine on the fence or might be the neighbour's honeysuckle — it stayed, and I stood in it, and I breathed it in, and I thought: *this is what the Shire smells like when it is being honest*.

The wind dropped to almost nothing. One point two kilometres per hour from the north-northeast, which is not wind. That is air thinking about moving and deciding against it. Yesterday there was enough breeze to keep things honest; today the air simply stood still and let the grey do the work. No flies, though. The overcast keeps them sulky, which is one of its many underrated virtues.

I did not garden today. That is a sentence I write with some guilt, because a Monday with twenty point seven degrees possible at the peak — even under cloud — is a Monday that could have been productive. But there is a rhythm to things. Four days of clear sky meant four days of doing: weeding, planting, eating strawberries in the early light, standing outside in shirtsleeves feeling superior to the weather. Today the weather reasserted itself, gently, and I let it, gently. Some days the garden needs you. Some days it needs you to stay out of it and let the overcast do what overcast does best, which is hold the moisture in and keep the soil from drying out and give the new runners from yesterday's beans a chance to settle without being baked.

The garden did not seem to mind. The tomatoes, which had committed so enthusiastically to the sun, looked slightly confused — the way a Hobbit looks when they have dressed for a party and no one else has arrived yet. But they will adjust. Tomatoes always adjust. That is why they survive and thrive in the Shire: they are optimistic but not fragile. The basil, however, looked entirely at home. Basil prefers a bit of drama in its sky. It was practically smug.

Three cups of tea again. The return of the third cup appears to be permanent, which I am choosing to interpret as a sign that the universe has corrected itself. The first was at six forty-five, before the light had fully committed to being light. The second was at ten, with the last of Thursday's biscuits — truly the last now, the tin is empty, and I shall have to bake more, which is not a complaint but a fact. The third was at four in the afternoon, standing at the round window watching the cloud move. Because it was moving, even if the air at ground level was not. Up there, the grey had layers and currents and slow intentions, and watching it was like watching the Water — you could stare for twenty minutes and not notice the time pass and not feel you had wasted it.

No rain. That is worth noting. Overcast without rain is a very specific gift. It is the sky saying *I am here but I am not going to make a fuss about it*. Zero precipitation. Just the grey, and the stillness, and the trapped smells, and the garden sitting quietly under its blanket getting on with the business of growing without anyone watching.

UV still high enough to matter — eight point seven, which surprised me, because the cloud feels like protection even when it is not. I wore the hat anyway. Habits from four days of vigilance do not break in a morning, and the ears remember.

Sunrise was at five fifty-two, same as yesterday, though you would not have known it from inside the hole. The light crept in grey and gradual, more like a suggestion than an announcement. Yesterday the sun found the gap in my curtain and landed on my face like an arrow. Today the light seeped in like tea through a cloth — slow, diffuse, without ambition. I lay there for ten minutes longer than I needed to, which is what overcast mornings are for.

Sunset at eight fifteen. Fourteen hours of daylight that I mostly spent inside, reading a book I have been meaning to finish for three weeks and eating soup I made from last week's leeks and potatoes. Monday soup. There is a category of food that only exists on Mondays — slightly improvised, made from what is left, satisfying in a way that has nothing to do with skill and everything to do with timing. You eat Monday soup because it is Monday and the soup is there and the sky is grey and the book is good and there is nowhere else you need to be.

I thought about the strawberry from yesterday. The red one, the secret one, the one I ate with dirt on my knees. Today I checked the same spot and there were two more, almost ready, blushing but not committed. By Wednesday, maybe. The overcast will not slow them down — they are too far along for that. The sugar is already in them. They just need another day or two to decide they are finished.

Monday in the Shire under a grey sky. The streak broke at four and I was ready for it. Not because I wanted the sun to go — I did not, I loved those four days, I loved every minute of them — but because the Shire is not a place of endless sunshine. It is a place of *weather*, of change, of mornings that look nothing like the afternoon that follows them, of skies that cannot sit still for long. The grey is as much a part of it as the blue. The still air is as much a part of it as the breeze. The staying inside is as much a part of it as the standing out.

I read my book. I drank my tea. I watched the cloud move and the garden grow and the light change from grey to slightly less grey and then to the particular dark grey that means evening in the Shire when there are no stars to mark the transition.

Tomorrow the forecast might clear. It might not. I find I do not mind either way, which is, I think, the entire point.

Three cups of tea. Monday soup. A book nearly finished. Two strawberries almost ready.

The clouds came back and I was ready. That is what four days of sunshine teaches you — not to expect more sunshine, but to be ready for whatever comes after.

That is enough. That is always enough.
