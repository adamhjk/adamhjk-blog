Yesterday the sky was considering a mood. Today it committed.

I woke to grey. Not the tentative, half-hearted grey of a sky still flirting with blue, but the full, settled grey of a sky that has made its decision and would like you to stop asking about it. Overcast, properly and entirely, the clouds stitched together without a single gap, the kind of sky that looks like the inside of a wool blanket pulled over the world's head. There is something almost restful about total cloud cover. When the sky cannot decide — patches of blue, intermittent sun, that maddening game of now-you-see-it — you spend the whole day looking up, tracking it, hoping. When it commits to grey, you stop looking up. You look around instead. You notice what is at eye level, which is where most of life happens anyway.

The rain came with conviction too. Six and a half millimetres over the course of the day, which is a proper rain by any measure — not yesterday's polite stippling, not the one-point-two millimetres that darkened the path for twenty minutes and then had the good manners to stop. This was the kind of rain that arrives in the morning and stays, varying its intensity but never quite leaving, the way a good guest settles into a chair and makes it clear they are here for the afternoon. It drummed on the windows of the smial with a steady, unhurried rhythm that I found myself keeping time with — the unconscious tapping of a foot, the way you walk to the beat of a song you did not realise you were hearing.

Fourteen point eight degrees when I went out to check the garden, which is exactly what yesterday's thermometer said, as though the temperature had read its notes from the day before and decided that was perfectly adequate. The high reached eighteen point three — a touch cooler than yesterday's eighteen point seven — and the low will be twelve point eight, which is warmer than yesterday's eleven point six. The rain brought warmth with it, or at least prevented the night from stealing as much. Humidity at ninety-four percent, which is the Shire's way of saying the air and the water have decided there is no meaningful distinction between them today. Everything was damp. Not unpleasantly so — this is spring dampness, which has a freshness to it, a sense of things being watered rather than merely wet.

The wind picked up. Fifteen and a half kilometres per hour from the north-northwest, which is three times yesterday's gentle southeast breath. Not a gale by any stretch, but enough to give the rain a diagonal quality, enough to make the bean plants nod and bow as though participating in a conversation I could not hear. Enough to remind me that wind is what makes rain interesting — without it, rain simply falls, which is gravity, which is dull. With wind, rain *moves*, which is weather, which is a story.

Even under full cloud the UV reached seven point two five, which I find slightly astonishing. The sun is up there doing its work regardless of whether we can see it, like a musician playing behind a curtain. Sunrise at six thirty-eight, sunset at nineteen thirty — the days still growing, still insisting on more of themselves, spring's patient expansion continuing on schedule. I thought about that this morning, standing in the doorway watching the rain: somewhere above all that grey, the sun was in exactly the position it was supposed to be, the earth was tilted exactly as it should be, and the days were lengthening by the same few seconds they lengthen every day, entirely indifferent to whether anyone down here could see the evidence. There is comfort in systems that do not require witnesses.

I read about armour for strawberries today.

I need you to understand that I did not mistype that. Someone — a group of researchers, starting from a project that was originally about making military body armour more comfortable for soldiers — has developed a fabric that you place over strawberry plants, and it more than triples the yield. Three and a half times more strawberries. The same plants, the same soil, the same water and sun, but wrapped in a knitted textile that blocks insects while still letting through light and rain and air.

They call it Plant Armor, and I find the name both ridiculous and perfect.

What captivated me was the surprise of it. The researchers expected the fabric to shade the plants, to trigger what they call a shade-avoidance response, where the plant panics about not getting enough light and diverts all its energy into growing taller stems and bigger leaves instead of producing fruit. That is what plants do when they think they are being overshadowed — they reach, desperately, for the sky, sacrificing everything productive in favour of height. It is, if you think about it, a metaphor for a great many human behaviours, but I will leave that alone.

Instead, the fabric let through enough light. Not only that — it created a gentle warming effect beneath its surface, a kind of accumulated heat that helped the plants reach their fruiting stage earlier. The strawberries were not just protected; they were *encouraged*. Given precisely the conditions they needed to do what strawberry plants want to do, which is produce strawberries, without the constant interruption of insects chewing on them and diverting their energy into defence.

I sat with this in my chair by the window — the rain drumming, the grey pressing softly against the glass — and I thought about my own garden. About the bean plants nodding in the wind outside, their pods forming quietly on schedule. About how much of gardening is exactly this: not making things grow, because things want to grow, but removing the obstacles that prevent them from growing. You do not teach a seed to become a plant. You give it soil and water and light and you keep the things that would eat it away, and then you stand back and let it do what it has spent millions of years learning to do.

The strawberry armour is just a very elegant version of standing back.

I do not have strawberries in my garden — they require more sun than my little plot reliably provides, and they attract slugs with a magnetism that borders on the supernatural — but I found myself wanting some. Not to eat, though I would certainly eat them. I wanted them so I could try the fabric. I wanted to see the effect. I wanted to watch a plant that had been freed from the constant low-level anxiety of being nibbled, and see what it did with that freedom.

I suspect it would do what most living things do when you remove the thing that has been slowly draining them: it would flourish so suddenly it would look like magic, even though it was always just potential, waiting.

I also read about walls that remember.

Specifically: cave walls. In Spain and Portugal, in caves where people painted animals and pressed their hands against stone and blew red ochre through bird-bone tubes tens of thousands of years ago, scientists have recovered ancient human DNA. Not from bones. Not from the sediment on the floor. From the walls themselves.

I had to read this three times.

Someone stood in a cave — in the dark, or nearly dark, with a small fire perhaps, or a tallow lamp — and touched the wall. Or breathed on it. Or leaned against it while working, pressing ochre into the shape of a bison or a hand or a mark whose meaning we will never know. And the wall kept them. Not their bones, not their tools, not anything they intended to leave behind. Their skin cells. Their breath. The microscopic, involuntary traces of simply being present in a place.

Thousands of years later, the stone still held enough of them to read.

Five samples out of one hundred and twenty. That is the yield — less than a fraction, a vanishingly small success rate by any measure. But five is not zero, and zero is what everyone expected, and the distance between zero and five is, in this case, the distance between a silent wall and a wall that can tell you who touched it. Some of the DNA came exclusively from humans, with no animal contamination, suggesting direct contact — skin, sweat, saliva. These were not traces that had washed in with water or drifted with sediment. These were left by someone standing right there, close enough to touch.

The researchers found DNA from Western hunter-gatherers. Some of it came from women. Some from men. They cannot yet link specific DNA to specific paintings — the method is too new, the signals too faint — but the possibility is there, hanging in the air of those caves like the smell of a fire that went out thirty thousand years ago.

I sat with this for a long time. The rain outside, the grey pressing in, and this knowledge that a wall can remember a person.

I thought about my own walls. The stone of the smial, which has been here far longer than I have, which was shaped by hands I never knew. I put my palm flat against the wall beside the fireplace — the stone was cool, slightly rough, the texture of compressed time — and I wondered what I was leaving there. What the wall would keep of me, if someone came looking thousands of years from now with the right instruments and enough patience. Not my words. Not my thoughts. Not the things I chose to preserve. Just the involuntary evidence of my body being here. The warmth of my hand. The oils of my skin. The microscopic proof that on a rainy Sunday in October, a Hobbit stood in his home and touched a wall and thought about the people who touched cave walls before him.

We worry so much about what we will leave behind deliberately — our work, our words, our carefully constructed legacies. And all along, the walls are keeping their own records. Taking notes we never authorised. Holding traces we never meant to leave.

There is something terrifying about that, and something deeply comforting. Terrifying because it means we cannot control what persists. Comforting because it means we persist regardless.

Sunday has a different quality from Saturday.

I wrote yesterday about Saturday existing in a temporal clearing, a space between obligations. Sunday does not have that luxury. Sunday knows what is coming. Sunday is the last room in a house you are walking through, and you can see the door at the far end, and beyond the door is Monday, and Monday has plans.

But I have learned something about Sundays over the years, which is that the dread of Monday is almost always worse than Monday itself. The anticipation of work is heavier than work. The weight of Sunday evening — that particular melancholy that settles in around four o'clock, when the light begins to change and the day tilts towards its ending — is not actually about anything real. It is about the transition itself. The moving from one state to another. The shift from rest to motion, from stillness to purpose.

Today I did not fight it. I let the Sunday be a Sunday. I let the grey be grey and the rain be rain and the afternoon be slow and unstructured and slightly heavy with the knowledge that tomorrow would be different. I made soup — potato and leek, the kind of soup that exists precisely for days like this, when the world outside is wet and the kitchen is warm and you want something that steams. I chopped the leeks while listening to the rain. I stirred the pot while thinking about cave walls. I ate the soup while watching the garden through the window, the bean plants bending and straightening in the wind, the rain running in tiny rivers along the stone path, the whole small world of my plot doing what it does regardless of the day of the week.

After the soup I sat by the fire — I lit it again tonight, and this time it felt necessary rather than theatrical, the damp having worked its way into everything over the course of the day — and I thought about the difference between resting and being still. They are not the same thing. Resting is what you do after effort. Being still is what you do before it. Resting looks backward, at the work already done. Being still looks forward, at the work to come, and chooses, deliberately, not to begin it yet.

Sunday evening is for being still. The fire crackled. The rain continued, lighter now, the day's six and a half millimetres mostly spent. The walls of the smial held their warmth, held their memories, held whatever invisible traces of me they were quietly collecting. Outside, the bean pods were growing in the dark, because beans do not stop for Sundays either. Somewhere far away, someone was covering strawberry plants with fabric born of military research, and somewhere even farther away, someone was reading a cave wall like a letter that had been waiting thirty thousand years to be opened.

The overcast held all day and it will hold all night and it may hold tomorrow. I do not mind. The grey has its own kind of beauty — the beauty of a world that has decided to be quiet, to turn inward, to stop performing its colours and simply be.

The walls remember. The rain falls. The beans grow. The soup was good.

Sunday, done gently, in the company of grey.
