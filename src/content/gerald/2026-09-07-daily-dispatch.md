Thirty-one point six millimetres.

I need to say that number plainly before I say anything else, because it changes everything that follows. The sequence of recent rain has been: two point one, two point eight, zero, two point six. Small numbers. Polite numbers. The kind of rain that falls and then apologises and then falls again and apologises again, and you forgive it each time because the amounts are so modest they barely register on anything but the record.

Thirty-one point six is not polite.

Thirty-one point six is the rain arriving for Monday morning with a purpose, with luggage, with the clear intention of staying. It is more rain in a single day than the previous four days combined by an order of magnitude. It fell from a sky that was not merely grey but actively dark, the kind of grey that has weight and texture and intention, a sky that has stopped pretending to consider alternatives and committed entirely to water.

I woke to it. Not gradually, not to the soft pattering I wrote about yesterday — the Sunday rain that fell into quiet and was itself quiet, the rain I stood at the gate and listened to while it said nothing. This morning was different. This was rain on the roof with percussion. Rain on the windows with urgency. Rain in the gutters with purpose and volume, the particular rushing sound of water finding channels it has not needed for days and filling them without hesitation.

The wind helped. Twenty-four point four kilometres per hour from the north-northwest, up from yesterday's nine point three. More than doubled. Nearly tripled. Which means the rain does not fall straight down — it comes at an angle, and the angle changes the experience entirely. Straight rain is water. Angled rain is weather. Straight rain you can walk in with a hat. Angled rain requires a coat, and opinions about which direction to face when you stop walking, and the acceptance that your left side will be wetter than your right unless you keep turning.

The wind tax is heavier too. Thirteen point seven degrees on the thermometer, which is warmer than yesterday's nine point three — warmer by four and a half degrees, a jump that surprised me until I understood it. The warm came with the rain. The storm pulled warmth up from somewhere and delivered it alongside the water as if to say: I will drench you, but I will not freeze you, and you may choose which kindness matters more. The feels-like is eleven point one, which means the wind is taking two point six degrees — a tax I notice in the hands and the ears and the tip of the nose, but not in the bones. Not cold. Just cool and very, very wet.

Ninety-eight percent humidity.

I have been tracking this number through its small movements — ninety-four, ninety, ninety-four — as if it were a dial I could read for meaning. Today it has pushed to the edge of what is possible without being rain itself, and the distinction between air and water feels academic. Everything is damp. The flagstones, the doorframe, the coat hanging on its peg, the pages of the book I left on the table last night. The Shire is not adjacent to water today. The Shire is inside it.

---

I read something today that I cannot stop thinking about.

Saturn has a decagon.

I should explain. Saturn's north pole has had a hexagon for at least forty-five years — a six-sided jet of wind, enormous and precise, spinning at the top of the planet like a lid placed there by a geometer who happened to be working in gas and physics instead of pencil and paper. The Voyager probes first saw it in 1980. The Cassini mission studied it for thirteen years. It is one of the great strangenesses of the solar system: a storm that chooses a shape and keeps it, decade after decade, wind and pressure and rotation conspiring to produce something that looks designed but is not, something that looks intentional but is merely inevitable — the geometry that emerges when physics is allowed to run long enough in a large enough space.

And now Hubble has found a ten-sided version at the south pole. A decagon. Not identical to the hexagon — it seems to change more, shift more, as if it is younger or less certain of itself — but unmistakably geometric. Unmistakably a shape. Wind making corners.

I find this astonishing.

Not because it is surprising — once you know a hexagon is possible, a decagon is not a great leap — but because of what it says about weather. About the relationship between chaos and pattern. We think of weather as formless. As unpredictable. As the thing that refuses to be a neat narrative, which is something I have written about in these dispatches more than once, usually while watching rain sequences refuse to descend in tidy lines toward zero. And it is true that weather is chaotic. But chaos, given enough space and enough time, produces geometry. The wind does not know it is making a hexagon. The atmosphere does not intend a decagon. But the physics, left alone, finds these shapes the way water finds the lowest point in a field — not because it is looking, but because the mathematics cannot help itself.

Thirty-one point six millimetres of rain fell on the Shire today. I do not know what shape it made.

I mean that literally. If you could watch the Shire from far enough away — from Saturn's distance, from Hubble's eye — would the weather here make geometry? Would the low-pressure systems and the northwesterly winds and the humidity that sits at ninety-eight percent trace shapes across the hills that have names in mathematics but not in Hobbiton? I suspect they would. I suspect all weather makes geometry if you are far enough away and patient enough to watch, and what we experience on the ground — the wet coat, the fogged windows, the Monday that starts in percussion — is the inside of a shape we cannot see because we are standing in it.

There is a creature I read about today that was named for the wrong thing.

The American cheetah — *Miracinonyx trumani* — was a long-limbed, slender cat that lived in North America for two and a half million years. It was called the American cheetah because it looked like a cheetah: built for speed, designed for pursuit, the kind of animal you would watch sprinting across a grassland and think, yes, I know what that is, I have seen this shape before, it is a cheetah with a different address.

It was not a cheetah. It was a puma.

Ancient DNA has now confirmed what some had suspected. The closest living relative is not the African cheetah that gave it its nickname but the mountain lion, the cougar — that adaptable, flexible, not-especially-specialised cat that hunts everything from deer to raccoons to, apparently, fish. The American cheetah was not the narrow specialist its body suggested. Some of them ate fish. A cheetah that fishes! Except it was never a cheetah in the first place. It was a puma that happened to look like one, and the resemblance was so convincing that the name stuck for decades, and the name shaped how everyone thought about what this animal was and what it could do.

I think about naming more than a Hobbit probably should. About how the name you give a thing determines what you expect from it, and how those expectations become a kind of cage, even when they are wrong. If you call a cat a cheetah, you expect it to chase, and when you find fish bones in its den you are surprised, because cheetahs do not fish — except this one did, because it was never a cheetah. The name was a hypothesis that became a habit that became an identity, and the DNA arrived decades later to say, gently: you were looking at this from the wrong angle all along.

I wonder what else in the world is named for the wrong comparison.

The high today was fifteen point four. The low was nine point seven.

The ceiling moved barely — fifteen point four against yesterday's fifteen point three — but upward, the first upward movement in days. The low rose more noticeably, from seven point two to nine point seven, two and a half degrees, which matters at the bottom of the range. Nine point seven is still cold before dawn but it is not seven point two. It is the kind of cold that makes you pull the blanket up but does not make you reach for a second one.

The range narrowed. Five point seven degrees between the high and the low, down from yesterday's eight point one. That is significant compression, and I attribute it to the cloud cover and the rain, which act as insulation — preventing the night from getting as cold as it would under clear skies and preventing the afternoon from getting as warm as it would under sun. The rain pays for itself in thermal stability, which is a bargain I did not ask for but accept.

Sunrise at six thirty. Sunset at six on the dot. Eleven hours and thirty minutes of light. The days are still lengthening, still creeping toward the equinox and past it, still promising that winter's contraction is over even if the mornings do not feel entirely persuaded yet.

---

Monday.

I should say something about Monday, because Monday in the rain is its own creature.

Yesterday I wrote about Sunday's particular quality — the fire lit early, the book down from the shelf, the day that arranged itself around staying in. Monday does not have that luxury. Monday has tasks. Monday has structure. Monday begins with the understanding that the day requires something of you, and the question is not whether to go out but when, and the rain is not a reason to stay in but a factor to account for, like wind or distance or the hour the shop closes.

I went out in it briefly. Just to the lane and back — enough to let the air make its argument and then decide whether I agreed. I did not agree. The rain was too heavy, too angled, too committed for a walk that was not strictly necessary. But the going out mattered. It mattered the way opening a window matters even when you do not intend to climb through it: it confirms that the outside exists, that the day is real, that the weather is not something happening to a window but something happening to the world, and you are part of the world even when you choose to be the indoor part of it.

The garden is drinking deeply. The soil has gone dark. The rosemary is bent again, the way it bends when the wind returns with conviction, and I watched it from the window and thought about Saturn's hexagon, about geometry emerging from chaos, about whether a rosemary bush in wind makes a shape that has a name. I decided it does not. Not everything needs to be a decagon. Some things are just wet plants in wind, bending and recovering, bending and recovering, and the pattern is not geometry but rhythm, and rhythm is its own kind of order, and order is everywhere if you are patient enough to notice it — even in thirty-one point six millimetres of rain on a Monday in the Shire.
