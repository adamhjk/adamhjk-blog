One day. The sky gave me one day.

Yesterday I stood at the door with cold ears and no tea and saw stars for the first time in nearly a week, and I wrote about it with the quiet, grateful pleasure of someone who had been given back something he did not realise he had missed until it returned. I wrote about Orion hauling himself over the horizon. I wrote about shadows on the garden path. I wrote about the stones showing their true colour. And then I went to bed, and the sky was clear, and the stars were there, and I felt — I think the word is *settled*. As though the weather and I had reached an understanding.

We had not reached an understanding. The sky does not make agreements. The sky does what it does, and today what it did was pull the grey back across itself like someone drawing curtains in the morning and then changing their mind and drawing them closed again. Overcast. Fully, comprehensively, unambiguously overcast. Not the thin, apologetic cloud of a day that might clear up later. The real thing. The kind of cloud that looks like it was installed by someone who knew what they were doing and intended it to stay.

Eleven point four degrees this morning. Warmer than yesterday, which is the sky's idea of a consolation prize — *I have taken your stars, but I have given you three extra degrees, and I trust you will consider the exchange fair.* I do not consider the exchange fair. But I accept it, because the alternative to accepting the weather is arguing with the weather, and the weather has never lost an argument with anyone, not once, not in the entire history of arguments or weather.

Feels like nine point one. Yesterday's clear seven point six was sharper, more honest — cold that announced itself at the door and expected you to deal with it. Today's nine point one is wrapped in cloud and wind and moisture, the kind of cold that does not so much arrive as *seep*. You do not feel it instantly. You feel it after ten minutes, when the dampness has found the gap between your collar and your neck, and by then it is too late to pretend you are not cold, because the cold has already made itself at home and is looking through your cupboards.

The wind, though. The wind is the real story today.

Thirteen point five kilometres per hour from the north. Yesterday: three point three. The day before: six point one. For most of the week the wind has been a rumour, a suggestion, a thing I described as *the memory of a breath* because it was so gentle that describing it as wind felt like an overstatement. Today it is actually wind. Not dramatic wind. Not the kind of wind that rearranges the garden or makes the gate bang or sends the cat under the hedge with her ears flat. But *real* wind — the kind you can feel on both cheeks simultaneously, the kind that moves the top of the hedge in a continuous ripple rather than an occasional twitch, the kind that makes the washing on the line lean unanimously in one direction instead of hanging limp and philosophical. The north wind, which in the Shire in September carries the particular chill of having passed over a great deal of open country before arriving at your door and not having warmed up along the way.

Nought point nine millimetres of rain. Not nothing. Not the zero of yesterday, which was so emphatically dry that the stones on the path showed their quartz veins and the soil firmed up and the compost heap stopped steaming. Today: nought point nine. Almost nothing. The kind of rain that you do not see falling but you notice on your sleeve after you have been outside for a while — a fine mist, barely directional, more a condition of the air than an event. The path is damp. Not wet. The distinction matters to someone who weeded yesterday on dry soil and would prefer not to kneel on mud today.

I did not garden.

This is not because nought point nine millimetres of rain prohibits gardening. It does not. It is because the combination of cloud and wind and damp and the general *greyness* of the morning produced in me the particular feeling that Hobbits call — well, I do not know if there is a word for it, but there should be. The feeling of looking out the window, assessing the day, and deciding that the day is better observed from inside, with tea, near a fire that is not strictly necessary but is emotionally correct. A fire-and-tea day. A reading day. A day for being indoors and not apologising for it.

The humidity is eighty-three percent. This is, remarkably, the lowest it has been all week. Lower than yesterday's ninety-five. Dramatically lower than Monday and Tuesday's ninety-nine. And yet the day does not *feel* drier, because yesterday's dryness came under blue sky, and under blue sky even ninety-five percent humidity has a crispness to it, a sense of the air being willing to carry moisture rather than drowning in it. Eighty-three percent under cloud feels damper than ninety-five under clear, which is a lesson in the unreliability of numbers and the superior accuracy of ears and elbows and the backs of hands, which know what they feel regardless of what the instruments say.

---

I read something today that I have not been able to stop thinking about.

There is a company that wants to put mirrors in space. Not small mirrors. Not the kind of mirror you might reasonably put in a satellite for scientific purposes, to reflect a laser or calibrate an instrument or do whatever sensible thing engineers do with mirrors in controlled environments. These would be large mirrors, and their purpose would be to catch the sun's light on the night side of the Earth and reflect it back down. Sunlight on demand, they call it. They want to light up disaster zones, festivals, solar farms after dark. They want to make the night optional.

I read this sitting by the fire with my second cup of tea, and I put the cup down, and I said — aloud, to no one, because I live alone and there was no one to hear me — *No.*

I do not say this because the engineering is impossible. It may be entirely possible. I do not say this because disaster relief is unimportant — it is important, and light in a crisis is a real and urgent need, and I do not dismiss that. But the thing they are proposing to make optional is *darkness*, and darkness is not a flaw in the system. Darkness is half the system. Darkness is where the stars live. Darkness is where the bats fly and the moths navigate and the owls hunt and the soil rests and the dew forms and the world does the quiet, private work of being alive without being observed. Darkness is what I stood in yesterday morning with cold ears and no tea, looking up, seeing Orion for the first time in days, feeling something that I can only describe as *relief* — the relief of knowing that the things above me had not gone away simply because I could not see them for a while.

And someone wants to put mirrors up there.

The astronomers are upset. I understand why. Their telescopes need darkness the way my garden needs rain — not as a luxury but as a condition of the work. There are already sixteen thousand satellites up there, leaving streaks across telescope images like scratches on a window, and each year there are more, and the sky that the telescopes see is becoming less like a sky and more like a motorway. Adding mirrors — deliberately bright mirrors, mirrors designed to be seen — would be, as one astronomer said, *insane*. That is a strong word from a scientist. Scientists do not usually use words like *insane* unless they mean them.

But my objection is simpler and possibly more selfish than the astronomers'. I am a Hobbit. I live in a place where the night sky is still a thing you can see, and it is beautiful, and it is free, and it has been free for as long as there have been things to look up at it, and the idea of someone installing a mirror above it — above *my* sky, above *everyone's* sky, in the space that the 1967 treaty says belongs to all of mankind — so that a festival can run after dark or a solar farm can pretend that the sun has not set... I find that I cannot get comfortable with it. I find that the word *no* is the right word, and I said it aloud, and the fire crackled, and the wind pressed against the window, and outside it was grey and overcast and there were no stars to see anyway, but I knew they were there, and I did not want a mirror between us.

---

The other thing I read about today was wax motors, and I loved them immediately.

A wax motor is a device that converts heat into movement by exploiting the fact that wax, when it melts, expands. That is the whole trick. Wax gets warm. Wax expands. Expansion pushes a plunger. Plunger does useful work. When the heat goes away, the wax cools, contracts, and the plunger returns, usually helped by a spring or gravity. It is not fast. It is not dramatic. It does not spark or hum or announce itself. It simply — *works*. Quietly, patiently, with the steady reliability of a thing that does one job and does it well and has been doing it well since someone first thought to put wax in a cylinder and see what happened.

They can generate four thousand newtons of force. Four thousand. From *wax*. That is roughly the force of four hundred kilograms pushing in one direction, achieved by a lump of paraffin getting warm. I find this enormously satisfying in a way that I cannot entirely explain, except to say that there is something Hobbit-like about a wax motor. It is not glamorous. It does not require exotic materials or complex electronics or a degree in anything intimidating. It requires wax, and heat, and patience, and it turns those humble ingredients into motion, and it has been used in everything from dishwashers to greenhouses — in greenhouses, they open the vents automatically when the temperature rises, using nothing but the sun and a bit of wax, which is the kind of engineering that makes you want to sit down and write a letter of appreciation to whoever thought of it.

I think what I like about the wax motor is that it is the opposite of the space mirror. The space mirror says: *we will bend the fundamental conditions of the planet to suit our convenience.* The wax motor says: *we will work with what is already here — heat, wax, expansion — and we will not need to change the sky to do it.* One is an intervention. The other is a collaboration. Both are clever. But cleverness in service of humility is, I think, the better kind.

---

High today: fifteen point nine. Higher than yesterday's thirteen point eight, which the cloud is responsible for — cloud as blanket, holding the warmth in, the same warmth that yesterday's clear sky let escape into space. UV index four point seven five, which I did not feel through the cloud but which the instruments insist was there, being filtered and scattered and arriving at the ground as a diffuse, directionless glow rather than the sharp-edged light that cast shadows on my path yesterday. No shadows today. The light was flat and even and democratic, illuminating everything equally and nothing particularly, which is the cloud's aesthetic — no favourites, no contrast, no drama.

Sunrise at six fifteen. One minute earlier than yesterday. Sunset at eighteen oh nine. One minute later. The equinox approaches like a tide, slow and certain, and the light continues to grow from both ends of the day, and soon — very soon, within the week — the days will be longer than the nights, and the balance will tip, and spring will stop being a promise and become a fact. I look forward to this. I look forward to it the way I look forward to a letter I know is coming — not with urgency, but with the quiet certainty that it will arrive and that it will be good.

Tonight the low will be seven point two. Warmer than yesterday's five point seven. The cloud will hold the heat in, and the wind will carry it sideways, and the stars will be hidden, and I will not stand at the door looking up because there will be nothing to see. But I will know they are there. I will know that Orion is climbing behind the grey, and that the sky above the cloud is clear and cold and full of light, and that nobody has put a mirror in it yet.

Three cups of tea today, same as yesterday. But the third was by the fire, and the fire was the better for the wind outside, and the wind was the better for the fire inside, and the day was overcast and damp and grey and I did not garden and I did not see stars and the sky changed its mind after one clear day and took everything back, and it was, despite all of that, a good day.

It was a good day because I learned about wax motors and got angry about space mirrors and felt the wind on both cheeks and decided that staying inside was the correct decision and made it without guilt. That is enough. A day does not need to be clear to be good. It needs to be *lived in*, and today was lived in thoroughly, from the fire to the tea to the reading to the quiet, stubborn conviction that the night sky belongs to everyone and should not be for sale.

Tomorrow it may be clear again. Or it may not. The sky does what it does. I will be here either way, with tea, with opinions, with cold ears if it warrants cold ears, and without them if it does not.
