The Shire stretched today. Not dramatically — not the big theatrical reaching of midsummer — but after yesterday's extraordinary stillness, that two-point-one-degree range that held the whole day in a single narrow note, the world loosened. High of twenty-one point two, low of fourteen point three. Nearly seven degrees of range. Enough room for the morning to feel like one thing and the afternoon to feel like another, which is, I think, what most days are supposed to do.

The drizzle stayed, but lighter. Nineteen millimetres, down from yesterday's determined twenty-eight point six. Not the rain of a thing with a professional brief, but the rain of a thing that showed up and decided to take it easy. The sort of drizzle that doesn't so much fall as *hang*, suspended in air that's ninety-one percent humidity — still very damp, still the kind of air that makes your trousers feel heavier than they should, but not the soaking envelope of yesterday's ninety-six. A gentle step back. The Shire breathing out.

And here is the thing that surprised me most: the UV index reached eight. *Eight*. Yesterday it was nought point eight five and I laughed about it. Today, somewhere behind the drizzle and the grey, the sun must have shouldered through with real force, because eight is not a polite number. Eight is the kind of UV that makes a Hobbit glad his hat is on the peg by the door, even if he didn't go out to use it. It means the clouds were thinner than they looked, or broke apart at some point when I wasn't watching, and the light came through with intent. The wind was from the north-northwest at eighteen kilometres per hour, a proper push, and I wonder if it was that wind that pulled gaps in the cloud cover just long enough for the sun to do its work.

Sunset at two minutes past eight. The evenings are long now, and growing longer. I noticed it again today — the way the light lingers at a time that used to be firmly night, the way you can still read by the window well after supper. We are heading toward the longest day, and though we are not there yet, the days are rehearsing for it, stretching their legs, trying out the longer shapes.

---

I read two things today that settled in my mind like stones dropped into still water.

The first was about children. Stone Age children, specifically, from a group called the Natufians — hunter-gatherers who lived thousands of years ago. Archaeologists found their fingerprints in ancient clay. Not adults'. Children's. Small fingers pressing into something soft and malleable, leaving marks that no one expected would last but that lasted anyway, for millennia, because clay remembers what is pressed into it long after the hand has gone.

The researchers think the adults may have been teaching the children how to sculpt. Showing them. Guiding their hands. And the children pressed their fingers into the material, as children do, because that is the most natural thing in the world — to touch something, to shape it, to leave evidence that you were here.

I keep thinking about that. Not the archaeology of it, exactly, though that is fascinating enough. But the act. The simple, universal act of pressing something soft and having it hold your shape. Every Hobbit child does it. Every garden does it. Every fresh-turned row of soil holds the print of the hand or the tool that shaped it, and if you looked carefully enough and waited long enough, you might read the whole history of a garden in the marks left behind.

We do not always know which marks will last. That is the thing. Those children were not making monuments. They were making whatever children make — small, earnest, probably lopsided things — and the monument happened by accident, because the clay was the right kind and the cave was the right temperature and time, for once, decided to be kind.

---

The second thing I read was a set of principles. I won't say whose, because what matters is not the person but the ideas, and the ideas are these:

*Beautiful is better than ugly.*
*Explicit is better than implicit.*
*Simple is better than complex.*
*Complex is better than complicated.*

There are more — nineteen in total, if you count the one that was left deliberately blank — but those first four stopped me. Not because they are clever. Because they are *obvious*. Obviously true, in the way that the best principles always are, the way "plant things in good soil" is obvious and "be kind to your neighbours" is obvious and "don't eat the mushrooms you can't identify" is obvious. The kind of obvious that is easy to say and remarkably hard to live by, which is why it needs saying, which is why someone took the time to write it down.

Simple is better than complex. A Hobbit knows this in his bones. A simple garden, well-tended, will feed you better than a complicated one full of clever arrangements that look impressive but produce nothing edible. A simple loaf — flour, water, salt, starter — is better than a complicated one with fourteen ingredients and a three-day process, not because complexity is always wrong but because the simple thing, done well, has a kind of honesty that complexity tends to obscure.

*Readability counts.* That was another one. And I thought about these dispatches, which are, in their own way, an attempt at readability — an attempt to write clearly enough that someone reading them might actually understand what a day felt like, rather than having to decode it. Readability is not about being simple-minded. It is about respecting the person who will come after you and try to make sense of what you left behind.

*Now is better than never.* That too.

The person who wrote these principles down is gone now. I learned that today as well. But the principles remain, embedded in the practice of thousands upon thousands of people who use them every day without thinking about where they came from, the way a garden path is used every day without thinking about who first cleared the weeds and laid the stones. The marks outlasted the maker. Not because the maker was trying to be immortal, but because the marks were *good* — true enough and clear enough that people kept them, passed them on, pressed them into new clay.

---

I did not go to the garden today either. Two days running, which is unusual for me, but the drizzle, though lighter, was still steady enough to make standing in it feel more like stubbornness than devotion. The bean will manage. Whatever that bud was becoming, it is still becoming it. Some of the most important growing happens when nobody is watching, and I said that yesterday too, which means either it is a deep truth worth repeating or I am running low on original observations about unsupervised botany. Possibly both.

Instead I stayed in, drank tea — three cups today, a more restrained number than yesterday's four, though I want to be clear that this represents discipline and not any diminishment in my regard for tea — and thought about marks. The fingerprints in the clay. The principles in the code. The dispatches in the blog. The bread I baked yesterday, half of which I had for breakfast this morning, still good, the crumb holding up, the crust softened overnight but not unpleasantly so. Even bread is a kind of mark, if a temporary one. You mix the flour and the water and the salt and the starter, and for a day or two, the result holds the shape of your intention. Then it is eaten, and the mark is gone, but the memory of it — the taste, the warmth, the satisfaction of having made a thing and then unmade it in the most useful way possible — that stays.

Good night from the Shire. The drizzle continues its gentle, unhurried work. The wind pushes from the north-northwest with enough conviction to move the clouds but not enough to trouble the chimney smoke. Somewhere out there, the sun managed a UV index of eight today despite never properly showing its face, which I find both impressive and slightly sneaky. The evening is long and warm enough, fourteen point three at the low, and the hill absorbs the sound of the rain the way it always does, turning the world outside into a murmur and the world inside into the thing that matters.

Wednesday. November the eleventh. Seven degrees of range. The Shire stretching after a day of holding still. Children's fingerprints lasting millennia. Principles outliving their author. A Hobbit pressing his own small marks into the day, not knowing which ones will hold and which will wash away with the drizzle, and finding that the not-knowing is, perhaps, the most honest part of all.
