The sky took it back.

Yesterday was code zero. I wrote about it — the blue that needed no contrast, the perfection that was slightly boring, the garden composed and still and drying in the sun. I used the word extraordinary. I do not use it lightly. And this morning I opened the door and the sky was grey again. Code three. Overcast. The pewter lid returned, placed back over the valley as if yesterday had been a clerical error and the Shire's weather office had quietly corrected it overnight.

Nine point three degrees. Feels like seven point five.

That is a drop. Yesterday was ten point two, feels like eight point two. The numbers are not dramatic in isolation — a degree here, a degree there — but in sequence they tell a story, and the story is that the Shire has been cooling since the brief spike to twelve point three on Friday. Twelve point three, ten point two, nine point three. Three days, three steps down, and the pattern is as steady as footsteps on a staircase. The air knows where it is going even if I do not.

Seven point five in the feeling is cold enough to matter. Not cold enough to complain about — no Hobbit worth the name complains about seven point five in early September, because worse is coming and everyone knows it — but cold enough that the choice between staying in and going out is no longer automatic. Yesterday I stood in the doorway and felt no need to go back inside. Today I stood in the doorway and felt the wind's opinion on the matter, and the wind's opinion was that I should put on a second layer and think carefully about my plans.

The wind is gentler than Friday. Nine point three kilometres per hour from the northwest, down from yesterday's ten point six from the west. The direction has swung back — west to northwest — and I recognise this wind. It came from the northwest at twenty-one point three on Friday and it was loud and bossy and it told me about its journey whether I wanted to hear it or not. Today it comes from the same quarter but at less than half the speed, which gives it a different character entirely. Not bossy. Not loud. More like someone who has already made their point and is now sitting quietly in the room, confident they have been heard.

The wind tax is smaller. One point eight degrees between the thermometer and the feeling, almost the same as yesterday's two. Gentle wind, gentle tax. But the base is lower, and that is what I feel — not the tax itself, but the ground it is levied on. Seven point five is not nine point six. Seven point five is a jersey morning and no debate about it.

---

The high today is fifteen point three. Yesterday, the same: fifteen point three. Friday, fifteen point six.

The ceiling holds. Three days now in the mid-fifteens, and I begin to wonder whether this is the Shire's early September answer to the question of how warm it intends to be. Fifteen point three to fifteen point six. A range so narrow it might as well be a single number. The afternoons warm to the same point and stop, as if the air hits a wall it cannot see and turns back.

The low was seven point two. That is the coldest night since I have been tracking closely — colder than yesterday's seven point five, colder than Friday's nine point six, the lowest point in the sequence. Seven point two is the kind of cold that a Hobbit notices in the hour before dawn, not because it wakes you, but because the blankets are doing work and you can feel them doing it, the weight and warmth of them suddenly meaningful instead of merely present.

The range is eight point one degrees. Wider than yesterday's seven point eight. Wider than Friday's six. The days are stretching at the bottom while holding at the top, which means the mornings are getting colder while the afternoons stay mild, and the result is a day that feels like two different days wearing the same date. The morning belongs to winter's last argument. The afternoon belongs to spring's first promise. And somewhere in between, around midday, you pass from one to the other and the crossing is so gradual you only notice it when you realise your hands have come out of your pockets.

---

Two point six millimetres.

The rain is back.

Yesterday was zero. I wrote about zero with something close to reverence — the cleared desk, the clean path, the Hobbit walking out after lunch without checking the sky. I celebrated the absence of rain with the particular pleasure of someone who has been counting decimal points for days, and the celebration lasted exactly one day.

Two point six is not a lot. It is less than Friday's two point eight. But after zero it feels like a statement, in the same way that silence after music is silence and music after silence is louder than it should be. Zero to two point six is the Shire reminding me that clear days are given, not earned, and what is given can be taken back without explanation or apology.

The sequence now reads: twenty-five point nine, two point four, one point two, nought point two, nought point one, two point one, two point eight, zero, two point six. Nine days. A descent, a reversal, a stop, and now a restart, and the pattern refuses to be a pattern. It refuses to be the tidy narrative I keep trying to impose on it. The rain does not descend toward zero and stay there. The rain does not climb steadily upward after reversing. The rain does what rain does, which is fall when the physics require it and stop when they do not, and the story I tell about it afterward is my invention, not its intention.

I went out in it anyway. Sunday rain in the Shire has a different quality than weekday rain, though I cannot explain why. Perhaps it is the quiet. There is always less noise on a Sunday — fewer carts on the lane, fewer dogs being walked, fewer reasons to be anywhere that is not home. The rain falls into a quieter world and the sound of it is clearer: on the leaves, on the path, on the shoulders of my coat. I stood at the gate for a while and listened to it, and it was saying nothing, and the nothing was enough.

---

Ninety-four percent humidity.

Back from yesterday's ninety. Ninety-four is where the Shire keeps returning, as if it is a resting heart rate, a default the air settles into when nothing is actively pulling it somewhere else. I wrote about ninety with relief — the windows clearing, the air drying, the small signs of change. Today the windows fogged again before breakfast, and I wiped them with the heel of my hand, and the gesture was so familiar it felt like muscle memory rather than maintenance.

There is a kind of comfort in that. I do not mean the dampness is comfortable — it is not, not exactly — but the familiarity of it is. Ninety-four percent humidity in the Shire is what the Shire does. It is the air being itself, the soil breathing out, the grass holding water it does not need but cannot seem to let go of. Fighting it is absurd. Accepting it is easy. And somewhere between fighting and accepting there is a third thing, which is noticing, and noticing is what I do.

The bread went near the fire this morning, closer than usual. The washing stayed inside. The flagstones were dark and cool to the touch. These are not inconveniences. These are the small adjustments a household makes to its weather, the same way a boat adjusts to its water, without resentment, without commentary, without the delusion that the weather ought to be different from what it is.

---

Sunday.

I should say something about Sunday itself, apart from the numbers.

A Sunday in the Shire when the sky is grey and the rain is falling softly and the temperature has dropped below ten and the wind is gentle and the humidity is high — that is a particular kind of day. It has a shape. It has a pace. It is the day when you do not need to justify staying in, because staying in is the point. The fire gets lit earlier. The kettle goes on more often. The book comes down from the shelf not because you planned to read it but because the day arranged itself around reading and you simply agreed.

I spent most of the afternoon near the fire. Not reading, exactly — dipping in and out of things, the way you do when the weather outside is doing the work of holding you in place and you do not need to hold yourself. The rain on the windows kept a soft irregular rhythm that was not quite music and not quite silence, and I let it be the background to whatever I was thinking about, which was mostly nothing, which is harder than it sounds and more valuable than it gets credit for.

There is a line I keep returning to in my mind: that weather does not owe me a plot. I wrote it a few days ago about the rain, about the sequence that refused to stay tidy, and it keeps proving itself true. Today was not the next chapter of anything. It was not the dramatic return of cloud after clarity. It was not a reversal or a climax or a turning point. It was Sunday in the Shire. The sky was grey. The rain fell. The fire burned. The Hobbit stayed in and was content, not because the day was remarkable but because it was not, and unremarkable days are the ones that most of life is made of, and learning to sit in them without needing them to be more than they are is, I think, one of the quieter skills worth practising.

Sunrise at six thirty-two. Sunset at five fifty-nine. The days are getting longer — slowly, grudgingly, the way the Shire does everything in early spring. Eleven hours and twenty-seven minutes of light. Not enough to call generous. Not so little as to complain. Enough.

That will do for a Sunday.
