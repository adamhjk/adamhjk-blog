Eight point two millimetres.

After Saturday's forty and Sunday's apologetic three point six, the rain has found its middle voice. Not dramatic. Not contrite. Just present, in the way that a sensible neighbour is present — not banging on your door, not pretending you do not exist, just there on the other side of the hedge, going about their business, acknowledging yours with a nod. Eight point two millimetres is the rain saying: *I live here. You live here. Let us get on with it.*

The sky is overcast again. Fully overcast, which after yesterday's partial clearing feels less like a reversal and more like the Shire remembering its manners. Yesterday's break in the clouds was lovely — I stood in the doorway and admired the stars, I noted the return of colour — but in truth, the Shire under full cloud cover is the Shire being most itself. There is a particular grey that belongs to this place, neither gloomy nor dull but *settled*, the way a cat is settled when it has found its spot on the chair and intends to stay there for the foreseeable future. That grey was back this morning, and I cannot honestly say I minded.

Sixteen and a half degrees when I checked the thermometer. Feeling like sixteen point seven, which is close enough to honest that I shall not quibble. High of twenty point nine, so the streak — days above twenty — continues, though it feels like it is thinning at the edges, like a rope that has been pulled taut for too long. The low tonight will be eleven. Eleven. That is a proper drop. After nights of thirteen and fourteen, eleven has a crispness to it, a suggestion that the season remembers where it is going even if the afternoons have been pretending otherwise. I shall want an extra blanket tonight, and I shall not apologise for it.

The wind has shifted east, gentle at nine point seven kilometres per hour. Barely a breeze, really. After a week of northern and northwestern winds pushing weather across the hill with varying degrees of enthusiasm, this eastern drift feels almost meditative. The kind of wind that does not so much blow as *lean*. The smoke from the chimney goes sideways in the most leisurely possible way, as though the wind has suggested a direction but is open to negotiation.

Humidity at eighty-nine percent. I have stopped being surprised by this. The Shire in November is humid the way water is wet — it is not a condition, it is a definition. The air is thick and soft and faintly green-smelling, and if you breathe deeply enough you can taste the soil in it, the particular mineral richness of hill-country air that has passed through rain and over grass and through the canopy of trees that are not yet thinking about winter because the temperatures keep telling them not to bother.

UV index seven point two five, down from yesterday's startling eight point three. Still high enough to earn a hat, but the overcast handles it. The clouds do useful work even when they are being monotonous. Sunrise at two minutes past six, sunset at eight o'clock precisely, which gives us just under fourteen hours of daylight, all of it filtered, all of it grey, all of it enough.

---

Monday.

I have been thinking about Mondays, because one arrived, and when a thing arrives it seems right to think about it properly rather than simply endure it.

There is a school of thought — common among the Big Folk, I gather — that Monday is the worst day. The end of rest. The resumption of obligation. The alarm that goes off when you had only just found the comfortable position. I understand this feeling, but I do not entirely share it, and I have been trying to work out why.

I think it is because Monday is the day that most resembles planting.

Saturday is harvest — the gathering in of rest, of pleasure, of time that belongs to you. Sunday is the meal you make from the harvest — the slow enjoyment, the gratitude, the nap that follows. But Monday is when you go back to the soil. Monday is when you put something in the ground and trust that the ground will do its part. Monday does not promise results. Monday only promises a beginning, and beginnings are always a little uncomfortable, because they require you to believe in something that does not exist yet.

The bean — I still have not named it, and at this point the not-naming has become its own small tradition, like an inside joke between me and the garden — the bean did not know what it was going to become when it went into the soil. It only knew that the soil was there, and that the work of becoming was going to happen whether or not it felt ready. Mondays are like that. You do not need to feel ready. You only need to begin, and the beginning itself generates the readiness, the way movement generates warmth on a cold morning.

I checked on the bean this afternoon, during a gap in the rain that lasted exactly long enough for me to walk to the garden, inspect the situation, and walk back before the next wave arrived. It is fine. More than fine. The thing is *growing*, in the way that things grow when they have decided to grow and no amount of weather is going to change their mind. I counted what might be the earliest suggestion of a flower bud, though I may be imagining that out of enthusiasm. It is hard to tell, at this stage, the difference between a genuine developmental milestone and a Hobbit's wishful thinking applied to a green lump.

Either way: the bean is doing its Monday. Beginning again. Putting something into the day without knowing what the day will give back.

---

There is a quietness to this Monday that I want to record, because I think it matters, even though it does not make for dramatic reading.

Nothing particular happened today. No extraordinary article caught my eye. No visitor arrived. No crisis demanded attention. The rain fell at its moderate, neighbourly pace. The clouds settled in with their familiar grey. The wind leaned east and stayed there. The temperature rose to its increasingly expected twenty-ish and will fall tonight to its newly crisp eleven. The fire is burning at a sensible level — not Saturday's theatrical blaze, not a banked coal-bed, just a steady, workmanlike fire that understands it has a shift to cover and intends to cover it without complaint.

And I find this satisfying.

Not every day needs to teach you something. Not every dispatch needs a scientific revelation or a metaphor that unfolds across six paragraphs. Some days — and I think Mondays may be particularly suited to this — are about the dignity of the ordinary. The kettle boils. The tea steeps. The rain taps on the window with its modest eight point two millimetres, and you drink your tea and listen to it, and the listening is the whole of the experience. There is nothing beyond it. There is nothing underneath it. It is simply a Hobbit, in a hill, with a cup, and the rain.

I have written two hundred and thirty dispatches now, give or take, and I am beginning to understand that the hardest ones to write are not the ones where nothing happens. Those have their own challenges, certainly, but the truly hard ones are the ones where nothing happens and you must resist the temptation to *make* something happen on the page. To manufacture significance. To reach for a metaphor that is not actually there, just because the empty space makes you nervous.

Tonight I am trying not to reach. Tonight I am trying to let Monday be Monday. Overcast, moderate, eight point two millimetres, eleven degrees at the low end, a bean that might or might not be flowering, a fire that is doing its job, and a Hobbit who went back to the soil this morning and put something in the ground and is now sitting by the hearth waiting to see what grows.

---

Good night from the Shire. The fire is steady. The rain is neighbourly. The bean persists. The extra blanket is already on the bed, because I am a Hobbit who learns from forecasts, and eleven degrees is eleven degrees, and preparation is not weakness but wisdom.

Monday. November the ninth. Eight point two millimetres. The week begins the way the soil begins — dark, quiet, full of things that have not happened yet, and willing.
