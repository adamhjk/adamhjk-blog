The Shire vanished this morning.

Not dramatically — no wizard's spell, no sudden crack of thunder and a puff of smoke. Just fog. The proper kind, the kind that arrives on its own schedule and does not apologise for the inconvenience. I opened the round door at half six and could not see the gate. Not could-barely-see, not had-to-squint — could not see it at all. The garden ended about four steps from the threshold, and beyond that was simply white. Not bright white, not clean white, but that particular soft, woolly, undecided white that fog has when it means to stay a while.

Seven degrees at dawn. Ten by the time the kettle boiled, which felt warmer than the numbers suggest because the fog held everything still. Three kilometres of wind — south-southeast again, same as yesterday, but today even that modest breeze felt theoretical. The air was not moving. The air was *sitting*. Eighty-four percent humidity, which is lower than yesterday's ninety-three, but when you are standing inside a cloud that has come down for a visit, the precise number ceases to matter. Everything was damp. The door handle, the flagstones, the tops of the bean poles, the small spider who had clearly spent the night building an ambitious web between the rosemary and the fence and now found her work decorated with a hundred tiny silver beads she had not ordered.

I admired that web for some time. Fog is unkind to spiders in one sense — it weighs the silk, sags the architecture, makes the whole enterprise droop like bunting after a rain — but it is extraordinarily kind in another: it makes the invisible visible. On a clear day, you walk through a spider's web without seeing it. On a foggy morning, every thread is outlined in water. The whole geometry is revealed. The engineering becomes undeniable.

I think there is a lesson in that, though I am not going to force it into a metaphor. Some things are better left as observations.

---

They promised a high of seventeen today, and I believe them, because by mid-morning the fog had thinned to a general haze — the sort where you could see the gate again, and the lane, and the shape of the hill beyond, but everything looked like it had been drawn in pencil rather than ink. The sun was up there somewhere. You could feel it pressing. The light was warm and diffuse, the kind that makes the garden look like a painting done from memory rather than life.

This is my favourite sort of gardening light, if I am honest. Direct sun is fine for growing things but ruthless for showing you every mistake — the crooked row, the patch you forgot to weed, the bean that has wandered off the trellis and is making a break for the compost heap. Fog light forgives all of that. In fog light, the garden is not a collection of tasks. It is a place.

I did not do much out there today. It was too damp for any real work — you do not want to handle plants when every leaf has a film of water on it, because that is how disease travels, and a conscientious gardener does not give disease a free taxi service. So I walked the rows, looked at things, made mental notes that will not survive until tomorrow, and came back inside with wet cuffs and a general feeling of satisfaction that the garden exists and is, for the most part, doing its job without my interference.

That is the best a gardener can hope for, really. Not that the garden needs you, but that it tolerates you.

---

I spent the afternoon reading about fog.

Not the weather phenomenon specifically — I know how fog works, water vapour condenses near the ground, temperature and dew point shake hands, the air runs out of room for all that moisture and starts hanging it on everything — but about the *idea* of fog. About what fog does to the way we think.

There is a difference, I have decided, between hiding and being hidden.

Hiding is deliberate. It implies a hider, a thing to hide from, a strategy, a fear. Hiding is what rabbits do. It is what secrets do. It is what a Hobbit does when the Sackville-Bagginses come calling and you pretend not to be home because the alternative is two hours of passive-aggressive commentary about your mother's silverware.

Being hidden is different. Being hidden is what happens when the fog comes down and the world simply forgets to render you. There is no intent. There is no pursuer. There is only the quiet fact that, for a few hours, you are not visible, and neither is anything else, and this turns out to be a profound relief.

I think most creatures spend too much time being seen. Not in the vanity sense — though there is that too — but in the basic, unavoidable sense that existence in a clear world means being part of a landscape, being countable, being *there* in a way that others can confirm. And this is fine. It is how the world works. But fog offers a temporary pardon from all that. When the fog is thick enough, you are not part of any landscape. You are just a warm thing in a white room, and the room has no walls, and nobody is watching, and for a little while, the only person who needs to know where you are is you.

I found this unexpectedly comforting.

---

The fog never fully lifted. That is the honest truth of the day. They said seventeen point three degrees at the peak, and perhaps it reached that somewhere — in the sheltered spots, in the places where the haze thinned enough for the sun to press through and warm a wall or a patch of grass. But the general character of the afternoon remained: soft, grey-white, indistinct. The UV was high in theory — seven point six, which on a clear day would have me reaching for a hat — but the fog ate most of it. Another kindness.

Sunset was at seven thirty-nine, though I could not tell you the moment it happened. The light simply dimmed, the way a lamp dims when you turn the wick down slowly. One minute the fog was white. Then it was cream. Then it was grey. Then it was dark. No ceremony. No colours. No performance. Just the gradual, dignified withdrawal of light from a day that had never been especially bright to begin with.

I made soup for dinner. Potato and leek, the kind that practically makes itself — you soften the leeks in butter until they lose their backbone, add the potatoes and stock, and then leave everything alone for forty minutes while you read something or stare out the window at the fog and think about spiders. It came out well. Thick, simple, the colour of the fog itself, which felt appropriate. A fog-coloured soup for a fog-coloured day.

The temperature has dropped to ten again now, and the fog is back in earnest, pressing against the windows like a cat that wants to come inside but has not yet committed to the idea. I can hear nothing from the lane. No footsteps, no carts, no late-evening conversations drifting up from the village. The Shire is wrapped in cotton wool, and I am in the middle of it, warm and fed and slightly damp around the cuffs, writing this by lamplight because a Hobbit writes his dispatch even when the day has been, by any reasonable measure, a day of almost nothing.

But that is not quite right, is it? It was not a day of nothing. It was a day of fog. And fog is not nothing. Fog is the world deciding, just for a while, that edges are optional and visibility is overrated and the kindest thing the sky can do is come down and hold everything very gently in place.

I think I agree with the fog on that one.
