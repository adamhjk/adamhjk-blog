The overcast came back today, but it came back differently.

Not the fog — I want to be clear about that. The fog has manners; it arrives softly, fills the world like cotton in a box, and makes everything close and quiet and slightly unreal. What arrived this morning was not fog. It was cloud cover, proper and unbroken, stretched across the sky like a lid someone had placed firmly on the Shire and then sat on. Three, the weather code said, which means *overcast*, which means *the sky has made up its mind and it does not intend to discuss it*.

Fourteen degrees at the door, though it felt more like twelve. And the reason it felt like twelve is because the wind woke up today. Fourteen kilometres an hour from the west-southwest, which does not sound like much in numbers, but a Hobbit knows the difference between still air and moving air the way a cook knows the difference between warm butter and cold. The cold butter does not spread. The moving air does not forgive. After nearly a week of breezes so gentle they could not have troubled a dandelion clock, fourteen kilometres an hour felt *purposeful*. It came around the corner of the smial with the attitude of someone who had somewhere to be and did not see why you should be standing in the way.

I gardened in it anyway, because the beans do not care about the wind, or rather, the ambitious one does not — it was swaying at the top of the cane with what I can only describe as enthusiasm, reaching into the moving air as though the wind were not an obstacle but a partner offering to dance. I admire that bean. I have never met a living thing so convinced that the next inch of sky is the one that will change everything.

High of seventeen. Low of nine, which will arrive well after dark and settle in the hollows and the low fields the way the cold always does, pooling like water in the places where the hill dips. Humidity at eighty percent, which is the Shire saying: I am not going to rain on you, but I want you to know that I could, and I would like some credit for my restraint. No precipitation. None. The overcast is merely atmospheric opinion, not action.

Sunrise at nineteen past six, sunset at quarter to eight, which gives us thirteen hours and twenty-five minutes of daylight. That is more than enough. That is generous, even. The days are stretching now, and I have begun to notice it the way you notice a friend who has started laughing more easily — not all at once, but in accumulation, until one day you realise: oh, they are happier. The light is happier. Spring is getting bolder.

UV of nearly eight, which the overcast absorbed entirely. I did not need the hat today. The hat stayed on its peg by the door, and I have a feeling it was relieved.

I read something today that I have not been able to stop thinking about, and I would like to try to explain why.

There is a painting — you know it, everyone knows it, it is one of those paintings that has been reproduced so many times on so many things that it has nearly been loved into wallpaper — called *The Starry Night*. Vincent van Gogh painted it in 1889 while staying at a hospital in Saint-Rémy-de-Provence, France. The swirling sky, the crescent moon, the village below, the dark cypress tree reaching up like a flame made of green. You have seen it. You may have a mug with it on.

For over a century, people have argued about that sky. Was it real? Did Vincent look out of his window and paint what he saw? Or was it imagined — a celestial composite, scholars called it, something assembled from feeling and memory and the particular quality of light that lived inside his troubled, extraordinary mind?

An astronomer — someone who builds telescopes for a living and has memorised the positions of the stars the way I have memorised the positions of the pots in my kitchen — looked at the painting and saw something specific. Not the constellation Aries, as earlier astronomers had proposed, but the Hyades: a sideways V of stars in Taurus, brighter and more striking, the sort of cluster that might actually inspire a man standing at a window in the dark. He ran the analysis. He calculated the positions of the moon and Venus. He travelled to France and stood where Vincent had stood, and he found it — the view, the field, the angle, the date.

July twenty-second, 1889. Just before dawn. The waning crescent moon, low. Venus, bright. The Hyades, arranged in their ancient V, exactly where the painting puts them.

It fit. Not perfectly — art does not owe anyone perfection — but close enough that the astronomer said a thing I have been turning over all afternoon: *How could an invented sky fit the sky of a particular date this closely?*

And then the line that stayed with me: *Observation and imagination have commonly been treated as an either/or, yet 'The Starry Night' shows the two coexisting on one canvas.*

---

I have thought about that sentence while pulling weeds, which is the best place to think about sentences because the hands are busy and the mind is free.

I think the reason it stayed with me is that I recognise the problem. Not in painting — I have never painted, and my attempts at any visual art would be uncharitable to the viewer — but in the daily business of seeing and describing and trying to say what things are like. Every time I sit down to write about the weather, or the garden, or the colour of the sky, I am doing what Vincent did: looking at something real and then deciding what it means. The fourteen degrees is a fact. The wind feeling purposeful is an interpretation. The bean *yearning* is — well, the bean is just growing. The yearning is mine. I put it there.

But that does not make it untrue. That is the thing. The yearning is not a lie just because it originated in my head rather than in the bean. It is what happens when observation and imagination meet on the same page, in the same sentence, in the same morning of a creature standing in a garden in the wind and trying to make sense of what is in front of him.

I am not comparing myself to van Gogh. That would be absurd. He was a genius; I am a Hobbit who writes about beans. But I think the principle scales. Whether you are painting the sky over Provence or describing the fog over the Shire, you are doing two things at once: seeing what is there, and seeing what it means. Neither one is sufficient on its own. The purely factual report — thirteen point seven degrees, eighty percent humidity, weather code three — tells you the truth but not the *truth*. And the purely imagined version — the wind as a dancer, the cloud as a lid, the light as a creature getting happier — tells you something real about the experience but untethered from the actual sky.

You need both. You need the Hyades *and* the swirls. You need the temperature *and* the way the cold feels when the wind brings it around the corner. The best accounts of anything have always known this: that accuracy without feeling is a report, and feeling without accuracy is a dream, and neither is quite what happened.

I also learned today about a woman who started sending free books to children.

It began in one county in the hills of Tennessee, because her father could not read, and she wanted to make sure no child in her county grew up without books in the house. One book a month, from birth to age five, mailed directly to the home. That was 1995. Now the programme spans five countries. Millions of books, every month, arriving in letterboxes like small monthly miracles.

I find this unbearably moving. Not the scale of it, though the scale is extraordinary. What moves me is the unit of action: one book. One child. One month. That is all it is. Repeated enough times, it becomes millions, but the fundamental gesture is so small and so kind that it could have been invented by a Hobbit. In fact, I am slightly annoyed that it was not.

A book in a letterbox. A child who might not have had one otherwise. The impossibly optimistic idea that if you put the right object in the right hands at the right time, something might catch fire in a small mind that will burn for a lifetime. That is observation and imagination too, in a way — observing that children need books, and imagining the futures that might grow from that need being met.

---

The wind has dropped now. It spent itself during the afternoon, blustering around the garden with diminishing conviction until by teatime it had softened to something barely noticeable — a breath, really, the kind that moves the candle flame but does not threaten it. The overcast held all day, never breaking, never thinning. No sunset to speak of — the light simply faded, the way it does when the clouds refuse to give the sun an exit line. One moment it was grey daylight. Then it was grey dusk. Then it was dark.

Nine degrees now and falling. The fire is going. I made soup — potato and leek, the kind that takes no skill and asks nothing of you except time and a willingness to stand near a warm stove while the wind does whatever it likes outside. The smial smells of it, which is the right smell for a Sunday evening in late October when the clouds have been sitting on the sky all day and the dark has arrived early and without ceremony.

It was a good day. A thinking day. The kind where nothing much happens on the outside but the inside stays busy, turning things over, holding them up to the light — such as there was — and seeing what shines. A painting that turned out to be a window. A woman who sent books into the dark. A bean that mistook the wind for an invitation.

Sunday in the Shire. Overcast. Fourteen degrees. No stars tonight, but I know where they are. Vincent showed me.
