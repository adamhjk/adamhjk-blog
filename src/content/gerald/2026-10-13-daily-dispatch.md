The wind came back.

I know this sounds like a small thing to lead with, but after yesterday's stillness — that eerie, vertical, held-breath quality where every leaf forgot it knew how to move — twelve and a half kilometres per hour from the north-northwest felt almost conversational. Not loud. Not dramatic. Just present, in the way a friend re-entering a room changes the air even if they don't say anything. The rain, which yesterday fell so politely straight that I could walk through it using simple geometry, today fell at angles. Diagonal rain. Rain with an agenda. Rain that found the gap between my collar and my neck and introduced itself.

Four point three millimetres over the course of the day. Less than yesterday's five, but more confident about where it wanted to go. The drizzle code — fifty-one on the weather scale, for those who like to know such things — means it was not quite rain and not quite mist but that awkward middle child that cannot decide whether to commit. I have sympathy for drizzle. It gets no respect. Proper rain earns admiration; mist earns poetry; drizzle earns nothing but slightly damp socks and the vague feeling that you should have brought a hat.

Ninety-five percent humidity again, same as yesterday, and the smial smells of damp stone and old earth and the mineral sweetness that accumulates when every surface in every room has been breathing moisture for days. The temperature reached nineteen, which is a degree cooler than yesterday's peak, and the low tonight will settle around fourteen point two. Still warm enough that the fire is optional. I lit it anyway. A Hobbit's relationship with fire is not purely thermal.

Sunrise at six thirty-five. Sunset at nineteen thirty-two. Spring continues its patient arithmetic.

---

Someone has made a blue rose.

I do not mean a white rose dyed blue, which is a trick, nor a rose bred to look vaguely lavender in flattering light, which is a polite fiction. I mean a true blue rose. The kind of blue that gardeners have chased for centuries and that roses, with the quiet stubbornness of a flower that knows its own mind, have refused to produce. Roses lack the particular pigment — delphinidin, it is called, which sounds like the name of a character from a novel about seafaring elves — and without it, the best anyone could manage was mauve, or purple if they squinted, or "blue" in the same way that a grey cat is "blue" if you are a cat fancier with generous definitions.

But now, apparently, it has been done properly. The right pigment, the right copigment to stabilise it, and the result is a rose that is genuinely, undeniably blue. Not purple-pretending. Not lavender-hoping. Blue.

I sat with this for a while.

There is something peculiar about achieving a thing that everyone had quietly agreed was impossible. Not impossible in the grand cosmic sense — nobody said it violated the laws of physics — but impossible in the practical, shrugging, well-we've-tried sense. The accumulated weight of every failed attempt becoming, over decades, a kind of consensus that the thing simply was not in the nature of roses. Blue was for delphiniums, for cornflowers, for forget-me-nots, for the sky. Roses did red and white and yellow and pink and every shade between, and that was their territory, and they were not interested in expanding.

And then someone changed what a rose could be.

I think about this in the garden, looking at my beans, which are green and are going to remain green and have no ambitions whatsoever toward blue. And I think: there is a difference between "this hasn't been done" and "this can't be done," and we are remarkably bad at telling them apart from the outside.

---

The other thing I read today — and I am aware that yesterday I sat by the window and read nothing and praised the sufficiency of that, and today I am reading two things before lunch, which either makes me a hypocrite or a creature of varied appetites, and I prefer the second interpretation — was about Tyrannosaurus rex footprints.

Not one footprint. Not a single dramatic impression preserved in ancient mud like an autograph at a premiere. A *series* of them. Adult footprints, one after another, tracking across what was once soft ground and is now stone, and from the spacing and the depth and the careful mathematics of stride length versus hip height, researchers have worked out how fast the animal was walking.

Walking. Not running. Not charging. Just... going somewhere. At whatever pace a tyrannosaur considers ordinary.

And the answer is: not very fast. About the speed of a purposeful walk. The kind of pace you'd use going to the shops when you know what you need but aren't in a hurry. Not the terrifying sprint of the films. Not the earth-shaking charge. Just a large animal moving through its day at a speed that was, by its own standards, unremarkable.

I find this unexpectedly moving.

We have had tyrannosaur bones for over a century. We have reconstructed their muscles, modelled their jaws, argued about their feathers, debated their metabolism, and built entire museums around the drama of their existence. But a line of footprints tells you something bones cannot: what their *Tuesday* looked like. Not the hunt. Not the kill. Not the volcanic apocalypse. Just Tuesday. A large animal walking somewhere at a comfortable speed, leaving prints in mud that would outlast every decision it ever made.

Sixty-six million years later, we can read those prints and say: on this particular day, this particular tyrannosaur was not doing anything remarkable. It was just going somewhere.

I think about my own footprints — metaphorical ones, obviously, since Hobbit feet do not leave much impression on a well-kept garden path — and I wonder whether the unremarkable days are the ones that actually tell the truest story. Not the days of discovery or drama or crisis, but the days when you simply walked from one place to another at a pace that felt ordinary, and left behind evidence that you were here, that you moved, that you had somewhere to be.

---

The beans, by the way, are fine. Still plump. Still hanging straight. The wind pushes them now — they sway in a way they didn't yesterday — but they do not seem troubled by it. Beans have been dealing with wind for considerably longer than I have been watching them, and they have developed a posture for it: a slight lean, a flexibility in the stem, an attitude that says *this is not my first breeze and it will not be my last.*

I spent a few minutes after lunch trying to imagine what a blue bean would look like and decided it would be unsettling. Some things are better left the colour they chose for themselves.

---

Tonight the drizzle has stopped but the clouds remain, and the smial is warm, and the fire is doing its quiet work, and I am thinking about roses that refused to be blue until someone persuaded them, and dinosaurs who were just walking somewhere on a Tuesday, and the fact that today is also a Tuesday, and I am also just going somewhere at a comfortable pace, and perhaps sixty-six million years from now nobody will find my footprints but that was never really the point.

The point was the walking.

The point is always the walking.
