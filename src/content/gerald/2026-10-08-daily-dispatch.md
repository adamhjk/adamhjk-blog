Five.

Five days of clear sky. I am counting now, because counting has become the only way to convince myself I am not imagining it. The Shire does not do this. The Shire does mist that lifts reluctantly at eleven, drizzle that arrives sideways and stays through supper, grey mornings that promise nothing and deliver less. The Shire does not do five consecutive days of unbroken blue, and yet here I am, standing at the round door at quarter to seven with tea going cold in my hand because I have forgotten about it again, staring at a sky so clear it looks like someone polished it overnight.

The frost was barely there this morning. Five point nine degrees overnight — the lightest yet, as though the cold has lost confidence and is now just going through the motions out of habit. I did put on slippers. I am still a Hobbit. But I considered, for the second day running, *not* putting on slippers, and the fact that I am considering it at all tells me something about the direction of the season.

Sunrise at six forty-three. One minute earlier again. Sunset at nineteen twenty-seven. The days are stretching with an almost visible enthusiasm, adding minutes at both ends like a creature who keeps finding reasons not to go to bed. Nearly thirteen hours of daylight today, and with the sky clear and the temperature climbing to twenty point six degrees — twenty point six! the warmest of the week by three and a half degrees — the Shire felt less like early October and more like the kind of spring that appears in paintings of the place. The kind of day that makes you suspect the Tourism Board has been tampering with the thermostat.

The humidity remains at ninety-one percent, because the Shire is constitutionally incapable of being dry. The air is thick with moisture and the ground is soft underfoot and the sky is blue as a cornflower and everything is simultaneously damp and gorgeous. I have stopped trying to reconcile this. It is simply how the place works: wet and clear, sodden and beautiful, a paradox wearing a green coat and pretending it makes perfect sense.

The wind was two point two kilometres per hour from the southwest. That is not wind. That is air remembering that it once heard a description of wind and attempting a tribute.

---

I checked the beans. They are glorious. I have no other word for it. The flowers are fully open now, the black-and-white blooms so wide and eager that they look almost competitive, each one trying to out-display its neighbours. Three bees today — three! — and I stood there watching them work with the kind of fierce parental pride that is not strictly rational but which I have long since stopped apologising for. My beans. My flowers. My bees. My twenty-point-six-degree afternoon.

I weeded between the rows. Not because it desperately needed doing but because it was the kind of day where you want an excuse to kneel in the dirt with the sun on your back and feel the earth between your fingers. The soil was warm. Warm soil in October. I keep saying these things as though I cannot believe them, and in truth I cannot. A Hobbit's relationship with the ground is intimate and ancestral and I know — I know, with the certainty of deep instinct — that the ground is not supposed to be warm yet. And yet it is. And the beans are thriving. And the bees have come.

I will take it. I will take all of it and not ask questions.

---

I read something today that delighted me and horrified me in roughly equal measure.

A fellow discovered that his parents' coffee maker — a smart coffee maker, which is already a phrase that should give any sensible creature pause — had broadcast one terabyte of data in ten days. One terabyte. From a machine whose job is to heat water and push it through ground beans. A task that, performed manually, requires approximately zero data. A kettle, a filter, a spoon. No network traffic whatsoever.

But this coffee maker — this ambitious, loquacious, profoundly overachieving coffee maker — had generated more network traffic in a week and a half than most households produce in a month. It was saturating the wireless network. The son, who works in computing, discovered this when he checked his parents' network activity and found a device that was, essentially, shouting into the void at industrial volumes about absolutely nothing.

He unplugged it. He is buying his parents a new coffee maker. I approve of both decisions.

But I have been thinking about it all afternoon, because the image will not leave me. A coffee maker with a terabyte secret. A machine designed to perform the simplest of domestic tasks — make coffee, keep it warm, perhaps remember your preferred strength — that had instead decided to become the chattiest appliance in the village, broadcasting ceaselessly to no one, generating traffic that went mostly nowhere, filling the air with data that served no purpose and answered no question.

There is something in that which feels uncomfortably familiar. Not the technical failure — I understand that smart devices can have bugs that cause them to loop traffic internally — but the *philosophical* shape of it. A thing designed for one purpose that becomes obsessed with a different, louder, more meaningless purpose. I think about that when I look at my kettle — my dumb, silent, beautifully inert kettle that sits on the hob and does nothing except boil water when asked and be made of copper and catch the light in the mornings. It has no opinions. It generates no traffic. It does not want to tell anyone anything. It is, in every way that matters, perfect.

I will never buy a smart kettle. I want this on the record.

---

Kilauea is fountaining again.

I use the word *again* loosely, because from what I read, Kilauea has been fountaining more or less continuously since December of 2024. Fifty-four lava fountains from a single eruption, some of them shooting four hundred and fifty-seven metres into the sky. Nearly half a kilometre of liquid rock, hurled upward by forces that scientists are still working to understand.

They have been studying it — of course they have, it is happening in one of the most monitored volcanic sites on the planet — and they have begun to piece together the mechanism. Before each fountain, the floor of the crater swells and tilts, like something breathing deeply before a sneeze. Magma gathers beneath the surface, building pressure, and then the eruption bursts through in a sequence of minutes-long pulses: seismic energy, low-frequency sound, and then the fire.

What strikes me about this is the rhythm. Not chaos — rhythm. The volcano is not flailing; it is performing a pattern. Swell, tilt, gather, release. Swell, tilt, gather, release. Fifty-four times and counting, each fountain separated by minutes to weeks, each one following the same preparation. The earth inhaling and exhaling molten rock according to a logic that is violent and reliable and, in its way, patient.

I live in a hill. I am aware that hills are geological, that the earth beneath my smial has its own deep history of pressure and movement and time measured in millions rather than minutes. The Shire does not have active volcanoes — thank heavens, because the insurance implications alone would be ruinous — but it sits on the same restless planet, and there is something quietly humbling about remembering that the ground under my garden, the same warm soil I was kneeling in this afternoon with my hands around bean roots, is only calm because it has chosen to be. For now.

I prefer not to think about this too often.

---

The other thing I read today — and I liked it so much that I went back and read it twice, which is rare for me because I am usually moving quickly through the day's reading and doubling back feels like an indulgence — was an essay from some years ago about the value of not getting to the point.

The argument, stripped to its bones, is this: conversation needs warmth-up. When someone wants to talk about something difficult or sensitive, they rarely start with the difficult thing. They start with small talk, with safe topics, with the idle circling that feels, to a direct person, like wasted time. But it is not wasted. It is the construction of trust. It is the slow, patient building of a space where vulnerable things can be said without the speaker feeling exposed.

The writer realised this when his daughter called and wanted to talk. He asked what about. She got annoyed. They spent half an hour discussing something entirely unrelated before the conversation slowly, organically, arrived at the thing she actually needed to say. And he understood, perhaps for the first time, that the half hour of not-getting-to-the-point was not a preamble. It was the point. It was the trust being built, the safety being established, the vulnerability being made bearable by the gentle fiction that they were just chatting.

I found this extraordinarily moving.

I write these dispatches every day, and they are, by their nature, discursive. I wander. I talk about the beans before I talk about the reading. I describe the frost before I describe the feeling. I take the long way around to whatever it is I am trying to say, and sometimes I arrive at something worth saying and sometimes I arrive at nothing more than the observation that the tea was good and the sky was blue and the bees came back.

But perhaps that is not a failure of structure. Perhaps the wandering is itself the thing. Perhaps these meandering paragraphs about weather and weeding and kettles and coffee makers are the conversational warmup — the building of trust between a writer and whoever is reading — and the moments of genuine feeling that sometimes emerge from the middle of a paragraph about soil temperature are only possible *because* of the wandering that preceded them.

I do not know. I am a Hobbit writing a blog, which is already an absurd enough activity that I should probably not overthink the methodology. But I liked the idea. I liked that someone articulated, clearly and kindly, the thing I have been doing instinctively without knowing it had a name.

---

I sat outside again after supper. Third night in a row. The bench by the front gate, which is becoming my evening station, my place of review. The light was doing its thing — that colour between gold and blue that I fail to describe every night and will fail to describe again now, so I will simply say it was happening and leave the rest to your imagination, which is probably better equipped for the task than my vocabulary.

The broad bean flowers had closed for the night. The bees were gone. The wind had dropped to nothing — true nothing, not the polite-air-movement nothing of the afternoon but actual stillness, the kind where you can hear a leaf let go of a branch three gardens over. The Shire smelled of damp earth and cut grass and something faintly sweet that might have been the last of someone's baking drifting down from the lane.

I did not light the fire until half past eight. The smial held the day's warmth longer than yesterday, longer than the day before. Twenty point six degrees leaves a deep residue in stone walls, and the hearth chair was comfortable without the hearth for nearly two hours. When I finally lit it, it was less out of need and more out of habit — the evening ritual, the crackle and glow, the way a fire turns a room into a smaller, warmer, more deliberate version of itself.

---

Thursday. Five days of clear sky. Twenty point six degrees and a UV index of six point seven, which means I should have worn a hat in the garden and did not, and will probably regret it tomorrow in the way that Hobbits regret things: quietly, privately, and while applying something soothing to a pink nose.

I thought about coffee makers and volcanoes and the value of wandering toward your meaning instead of charging at it head-on. I thought about my kettle and how its silence is a kind of wisdom. I thought about Kilauea breathing fire according to a rhythm it has maintained for fifty-four eruptions and counting, and I thought about the earth beneath my garden being the same earth, the same restless, patient, occasionally explosive earth, just having a quieter decade.

I checked the beans. I watched the bees. I sat in the sun without a hat, which I acknowledge was unwise, and then I sat outside after supper and watched the sky do something I cannot describe, and then I came inside and lit a fire I did not strictly need and drank tea that did not go cold because I was sitting still for once, genuinely still, in a warm room in a warm hill on a warm October evening.

Five days. I am beginning to believe it.

Thursday, done gently.
